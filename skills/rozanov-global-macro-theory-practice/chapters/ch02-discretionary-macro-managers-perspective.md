# Chapter 2: Discretionary Global Macro: A Manager's Perspective

**Author**: Kenneth G. Tropin, Graham Capital Management (pp. 21-37)

## Core Idea
A discretionary macro firm is a talent business wrapped in rules. The author's recipe has five parts: a defined business model, trading talent, a disciplined allocation process, risk management and infrastructure. He writes from one firm's experience and says so.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Why macro has low correlation** (pp. 22-23). Four attributes:
  1. Liquid markets, so the manager can sell under duress.
  2. Multiple sectors, no single asset class.
  3. No embedded long or "value" bias. Beta can be positive or negative.
  4. It captures *divergence* (directional breakouts), not convergence. Convergence strategies "can suffer significant losses if markets move far from a historic mean".
- **Themes as a compass** (p. 24):
  - The manager builds themes from growth, inflation and monetary policy, or from micro items such as a law's effect on a part of the yield curve.
  - Themes give the presumed direction of markets. A price that deviates from the compass may be a trade.
  - Two entry types. *Forward-looking*: expect the market to "catch up" with the view. *After the fact*: the market misread a data point and moved too far, so "fade" it.
- **Single-manager versus multi-manager model** (p. 25):
  - Single: the founder or CIO is the largest risk taker and may "size up" others' positions. Other managers know the principal's view, which makes opposing views hard.
  - Multi: each manager gets a capital allocation and agreed rules, and trades independent views. The aim is low correlation between teams.
- **Eight questions for assessing a portfolio manager** (pp. 26-27):
  1. Long, good-quality track record from a consistent, replicable strategy? Little style drift?
  2. Credentials? A history of moving firm to firm may signal a problem.
  3. A well-articulated method: how they build themes, pick instruments, set stops and take profits?
  4. A detailed market view that maps to positions? "Experienced portfolio managers have a 1:1 mapping of views and positions."
  5. Beta to indexes? It should be "episodic and not a consistent presence".
  6. Has the manager run substantial capital? "A 1.2 Sharpe ratio on US$5 million in capital is very different from a 1.2 Sharpe on US$200 million."
  7. Has the manager traded through several market cycles?
  8. Does the style fit the firm's risk culture, liquidity limits and processing capability?
  - Extra question for a multi-manager fund: is the candidate correlated with existing managers? If so, factor it into capital.
- **Two capital allocation models** (p. 31):
  1. *Discretionary*: the committee allocates on its view of opportunities. It gets "tedious and less realistic" with many strategies and invites bias and bad timing.
  2. *Quantitative*: a model optimises on returns, volatility, downside volatility, drawdowns, cross-correlations and manager tenure.
  - The author's firm uses the model with a qualitative overlay. His reason: a model "confines risk taking to the portfolio managers' trading books". A market view at the allocation level adds a second layer of risk.
- **Risk management layers** (pp. 32-34):
  1. The manager's own measures: first-order sensitivities (for example DV01), stress loss, explicit stop-loss points.
  2. Firm-enforced drawdown limits, tied to the intended volatility of each strategy and of the portfolio.
  3. Liquidity review: expected cost to exit in normal and stressed markets. These are assumptions and need regular review.
  4. VaR plus stress tests, with VaR's limits understood.
  5. Counterparty due diligence and back-up counterparties.
  6. A risk committee. At the author's firm it meets daily and can order a position cut.

## Key Concepts
- **Divergence versus convergence**: breakout capture versus mean-reversion capture (p. 23).
- **Sizing up**: a principal concentrating another manager's position (p. 25).
- **Runway**: the patience a firm gives a manager to become profitable (p. 28).
- **Hedging book**: a separate book run to offset an unwanted aggregate exposure (p. 34).
- **Basis risk**: the hedge and the position behave differently than expected (p. 34).

## Mental Models
- Think of a theme as a compass, and of the trade as the gap between compass and price.
- Use "cut, don't hedge" when aggregate risk is too high. The author's reason: "the most efficient way to reduce a risk is to eliminate entirely or reduce a position". A hedging book has basis risk and no clear owner of its profit and loss.
- Low correlation between managers is also a governance tool. It makes fund-level concentration, and therefore forced cuts, less frequent.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using VaR alone**: it "tends to break down" for options and for relative-value trades where two instruments diverge further than expected. It will not capture extreme moves (p. 33).
- **Opaque de-risking**: managers leave when they are de-allocated for unclear reasons. Some firms write risk limits and volatility targets into the employment contract (p. 29).
- **Trusting the back-up counterparty**: in extreme conditions it "may fail to materialise" (p. 33).
- **Assuming liquidity is fixed**: "liquidity in any market is a constantly moving target" (p. 25).

## Worked Example
The "virtuous cycle" theme (p. 24), as the author sketches it.
- View: low rates feed consumer and business demand. Employment and spending rise. Growth follows.
- Implied compass: equity and commodity prices drift higher. Yield curves are steep, because low rates now and growth later may bring inflation or higher rates "further down the road".
- Trade logic: if prices have not yet moved that way, position for the catch-up. If a data release pushes prices too far the other way and the manager judges it misread, fade the move.
- The chapter gives no numbers, instruments or sizes for this example.

## Key Takeaways
1. Demand a one-to-one map from view to position. A view with no position, or a position with no view, marks inexperience.
2. Persistent beta is a warning. Episodic beta is normal.
3. Tie drawdown limits to target volatility.
4. Allocate capital by rule. Keep market views inside trading books.
5. "A small shift in the percentage of winning trades can have an exponential impact on returns" (p. 37).

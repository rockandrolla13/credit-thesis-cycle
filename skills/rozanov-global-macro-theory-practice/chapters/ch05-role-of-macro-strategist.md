# Chapter 5: The Role of a Global Macro Strategist

**Author**: Grant Wilson, Civic Capital (pp. 77-95)

## Core Idea
A macro strategist forecasts markets, not the economy. The job has two functions: analysis and prediction. There are two schools. The *fundamental* school expects economic logic to assert itself. The *behavioural* school studies the reaction function of powerful actors and predicts what they will do. The author belongs to the second and says so.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Economist versus strategist** (pp. 78-79, 81):
  - Economist: analyses and predicts the economy and explains policy tools.
  - Strategist: focuses on markets and on "any economic, political or social development that may have relevance".
  - Payrolls example: the strategist "has very little business" forecasting the deviation from consensus. Predicting how participants respond to the deviation is the job.
- **Two functions** (pp. 79-81):
  1. *Analytical*: methodical examination of an issue, no prediction required. Includes the structure of markets and the identities and interests of participants.
  2. *Predictive*: forecasts of direction **and volatility**, with accountability.
- **Volatility forecast asymmetry** (p. 81): the volatility forecast sets position size.
  - Too high a forecast gives positions that are too small. "Sub-optimal, but quite benign."
  - Too low a forecast gives positions that are too large. "Quite dangerous."
- **Fundamental school** (pp. 82-84): economic variables drive prices "over the long run". Deviations from fair value can persist. Its toolkit, as cited:
  - *Impossible trinity* (Mundell-Fleming): a fixed exchange rate and an open capital account cannot persist unless the central bank gives up monetary policy. A country in breach is flagged as vulnerable.
  - *Triffin dilemma*: the US deficit needed to supply reserves would oversupply dollars relative to gold. Validated in 1971.
  - *Canonical currency crisis model* (Krugman): (1) a peg backed by finite reserves, (2) open capital markets, (3) a budget deficit funded by money creation. A speculative attack follows. Higher rates repel it at first. Political limits on rates appear, reserves run down, the peg goes.
- **Behavioural school** (pp. 84-86): built on Weber's *verstehen*, "interpretative understanding".
  - It is not Kahneman-Tversky behavioural finance. It is "not particularly interested in cognitive biases".
  - *Intellectual empathy*: understand the framework of the central bank, government or large private actor. Agreement is not needed.
  - Fundamentals matter because of how powerful actors react to them, not through a direct logical link to prices.
  - *Emotional empathy*: when the actor's framework breaks down, study the herd. Expect euphoria or panic. "Rationalist bounds no longer apply."
- **Framework-breakdown test** (p. 86). The actor's construct is failing when either:
  1. Economic developments have diverged enough from its expectations to question the framework, or
  2. Markets have moved far enough to invalidate it.
  Then assess the hit to the institution's "credibility and resources".
- **Good analysis** (pp. 92-93): a "genuine transfer of intellectual capital". Coherent, independent, timely, distinctive. No ad hominem attacks and no "unqualified advocacy". Acknowledge data sources.
- **Good prediction** (pp. 93-95): accuracy and accountability. Be time-specific. Do not mix short and long horizons. Analyse key risk events in isolation. State clearly when a view is ceded.

## Key Concepts
- **Reaction function**: how a participant responds to a stimulus. The strategist's central object of study.
- **Verstehen**: putting oneself "imaginatively in the place of the actor".
- **Positive versus normative analysis**: what is versus what ought to be (p. 93).
- **Real-time equity estimate**: the author's tool for tracking the SNB's balance sheet ahead of the official monthly release.
- **Lower bound**: the SNB's 6 September 2011 commitment to hold Eur/Sfr above 1.20.
- **Sterilised versus unsterilised intervention**: the axis of the economists' debate over the SNB (p. 88).

## Mental Models
- Use the fundamental lens when policy is "well calibrated and transparently applied". Change is then incremental and past cases give a volatility estimate (pp. 85-86).
- Switch to the behavioural lens when a powerful actor's framework is visibly failing.
- Think of a policymaker's academic record and early press conferences as data on the reaction function. The author's cases are Bernanke in 2006 and Draghi succeeding Trichet.
- Think of the strategist as a political economist: the object is "the exercise of discretion by powerful market participants" (p. 81).

## Anti-patterns
- **Insisting on a logical basis for a move**: the author's stated reason that fundamental strategists missed the franc surge (p. 91).
- **Anchoring on valuation**: all fair-value measures said the franc was extreme, so strategists could not "justify" more strength (p. 89).
- **Spinning a failed call** to avoid association with the loss (p. 94).
- **Forecast with no time element**: less useful and less accountable.
- **Data mining** and misuse of statistical methods (p. 93).
- **Orthodoxy**: the typical client complaint.

## Worked Example
The Swiss National Bank, 2008-11 (pp. 87-92).
- *Background*. After the crisis the SNB intervened in Eur/Sfr. Reserves had been Sfr 43-64 billion in the prior decade, 8-13% of GDP. By May 2010, when the programme stopped, they were Sfr 238 billion, 42% of GDP. The franc kept rising. Early August 2011, near parity, the SNB cut rates and expanded sight deposits. On 6 September 2011 it set the 1.20 floor. Eur/Sfr moved above it "without the SNB having even intervened".
- *Economist*: studied the effect of the strong franc on the Swiss economy and debated sterilised versus unsterilised intervention. Example: the IMF Article IV review of May 2011.
- *Fundamentalist*: started from valuation. The real exchange rate was about 10% above its 15-year average in 2010, matching the 1995 and 2003 highs that had been multi-year selling points. They forecast reversal. Consensus Eur/Sfr forecasts (39 contributors) lagged spot through Q2 and Q3 2011. They also noted the SNB's unlimited ability to sell francs, which set this apart from a classic peg crisis.
- *Behaviouralist*, in the author's steps:
  1. Drop any logical link between the Swiss economy and the level of Eur/Sfr.
  2. Identify the most influential participant: the SNB.
  3. Read the Governing Council's speeches and essays. Compile its published data. Study its mandate, constitutional base and relation to the cantons. Map its relations with parliament, the IMF and the private banks.
  4. Observe the framework breaking: the market ignored both valuation and verbal intervention.
  5. Find the pressure point. The franc rally eroded the SNB's equity and provisions. SNB profits had funded canton budgets, so this was politically sensitive.
  6. Track equity in real time. Official data lagged by a month.
- *Outcome*: the SNB began re-expanding its balance sheet "within two days of its equity moving into negative territory". Within a month it set the floor.
- The author adds that the case shows a difference of method. The two schools often converge.

## Key Takeaways
1. Forecast volatility with direction. Err high on volatility.
2. Identify who has the power to set the price, then study their constraints and balance sheet.
3. An institution's own balance sheet can be the trigger for a policy change.
4. A valuation extreme is not a timing signal when the dominant actor's framework is failing.
5. Put a date on every forecast and say plainly when it is withdrawn.

# Chapter 14: GTAA and Global Macro for Long-Term Institutional Investors

**Authors**: Gerlof de Vrij, Roy Hoevenaars and Pieter Jelle van der Sluis, Blenheim Capital Management BV (pp. 267-290). They draw on running an absolute-return GTAA fund inside "a very large pension fund".

## Core Idea
Risk premia vary with the state of markets, so a static strategic mix is not enough. The authors give a blueprint for an in-house macro mandate that mixes internal discretionary, internal systematic and external managers. They also argue that the mandate's insights should flow up into strategic allocation. Their sharpest claim concerns risk: downside risk is highest "when risky assets are rich and volatility levels are low", so volatility is the wrong measure.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Pyramid of the institutional process** (Figure 14.1, p. 269), top to bottom: asset and liability management, strategic asset allocation, dynamic asset allocation and overlay, GTAA, active management. "The most important and value-adding part of the pyramid is at the top", yet most resources go to the bottom. Market intelligence should flow up. Risk and portfolio-construction discipline should flow down.
- **Four limits of classic TAA** (pp. 269-270):
  1. Few asset classes, mostly cash instruments.
  2. Low decision frequency, typically monthly.
  3. Unbalanced risk: overweighting stocks against bonds in capital terms ignores that equities are riskier.
  4. The tactical call eats a disproportionate share of the active risk budget.
- **Three ways to place a macro mandate** (p. 271): (1) inside the hedge fund or absolute return allocation, (2) as portable alpha or an overlay, (3) as the successor to TAA.
- **Four sleeves of a typical GTAA programme** (pp. 271-272): asset class timing, equity country selection, fixed income country selection, currency selection. Extensions: commodities, emerging equity and bond markets, credit markets, and tradable dividends, volatility and correlation.
- **Five sources of active return** (pp. 277-279):
  1. *Time-varying returns on risky assets.* Expected returns are higher when conditions are weak. Calendar rebalancing does not exploit this.
  2. *A broader universe.* The equity premium, term premium, swap spreads and credit spreads "vary over the business cycle, as do volatilities and correlations". Derivatives allow "pure positioning in swap spreads, credit spreads and term spreads". Add timing of styles: value, size, carry, momentum.
  3. *More efficient portfolio construction.* Risk allocation in place of capital allocation. Asymmetric payoffs.
  4. *Inefficiencies from home bias and asset class silos.* Silo managers are "reluctant to trade on drifts in cross-asset valuations".
  5. *Non-profit-maximising agents.* Commodity producers pay for insurance. Central banks intervene in currencies.
- **The mandate blueprint** (pp. 280-283):
  - About a dozen seasoned professionals can run "a multi-billion dollar" portfolio "at 15% target risk". Target: cash-plus, not an index. Manage total risk.
  - Three competences: external manager selection, internal fundamental-discretionary, internal quantitative-systematic.
  - An allocation committee moves risk between them. The process is discretionary.
  - *External*: select only managers "differentiated enough" from internal books (specialism, or high barriers to entry). Spread across the four quadrants of systematic/discretionary and technical/fundamental. Start small and grow with confidence. Negotiate fees and liquidity. Use returns-based style analysis with methods that adapt to time-varying betas. Keep Chinese walls on sensitive information.
  - *Internal discretionary*: two books. Short horizon, three to six months, driven by fundamental macro, cycle, sentiment and themes. Long horizon, three years and more, entered only when an asset is "significantly under- or overvalued". Cap each theme's marginal risk contribution. Implement each theme "in as pure a format as possible".
  - *Internal systematic*: models that are "simple, robust and transparent, and well-rooted in economic fundamentals". Horizons from one day to three years.
- **Four uses of leverage** (pp. 283-284). "Leverage is not risk."
  1. Scale a low-risk asset to a risk comparable with equities.
  2. Use diversification more fully, so that gains in one asset can balance drawdowns in another.
  3. Remove the trade-off between return and diversification.
  4. Hold portfolio risk more constant through time.
  - The authors' rule: "Leverage is a tool to mitigate risks and not a control variable." Set it from the cash buffer that stress tests require.
- **Valuation-based view of risk** (pp. 285-286): "forward-looking downside risks are highest when risky assets are rich and volatility levels are low". Prefer downside deviation or "valuation-based forward-looking measures of risk". Use several measures and systems in parallel.
- **Governance** (pp. 287-288): minimise principal-agent conflicts. A dedicated, accountable, full-time team replaces the slow TAA committee. Keep independent lines for risk, valuation and compliance. Call ad hoc committee meetings on shocks, using quantitative triggers and qualitative assessment.

## Key Concepts
- **Dynamic asset allocation**: the layer between strategic allocation and GTAA that tilts the mix on medium-term valuation.
- **Portable alpha**: an active return stream carried on top of an unchanged strategic mix.
- **Risk allocation**: sizing by contribution to risk, not by capital.
- **Good and bad correlations**: correlation that helps in a sell-off versus correlation that hurts (Figure 14.2, graph A).
- **Straddle-like payout**: the shape of 21-day CTA returns plotted against the underlying's return (graph D).
- **Staying power**: the capacity to hold a long-horizon valuation trade through adverse momentum.
- **Capital-at-risk**: one of the measures the authors list for levered portfolios, with CVaR, concentration risk and stress tests.
- **One-day jump risk**: the limit of counterparty risk on exchange-traded futures (p. 287).

## Mental Models
- "A pound of feathers or a pound of lead": a levered low-risk asset can carry less risk than an unlevered risky one.
- Complacency is the dangerous state. Risk premia are low, markets "search for yield", and "applying pro-cyclical leverage at this stage to squeeze more yield out of tiny spreads is very dangerous". It is "much wiser, albeit unfashionable, to de-risk or buy protection".
- If you find yourself thinking about hedging an asset, treat that as "a strong warning signal to re-evaluate the holdings of that particular asset".
- The long horizon is an uncrowded edge. Most managers are judged on short records and cannot hold a valuation trade that takes longer than planned.
- A contrarian needs stop-loss and take-profit discipline too, because "markets do not correct by going sideways" (p. 276).
- In a swap the principal is not exchanged. If the counterparty fails, the exposure is the gain or loss on the contract.

## Anti-patterns
- **Capital allocation mistaken for diversification**: see the 60/40 example.
- **Backward-looking risk systems**: they add risk after prices rise.
- **Short-term risk parameters on a contrarian book**: "short-termism is a real danger for risk management".
- **Overreacting both ways**: "searching too desperately for yield on the upswings" and "buying too much expensive protection in the troughs". Pro-cyclical risk management pays "an excessive insurance premium".
- **Buying tail protection because its 2008 simulation looked good.**
- **Holding "collections of assets rather than portfolios of assets"** in compartments (p. 269).
- **Blurring alpha with alternative beta.**
- **Committee TAA**: infrequent meetings, no accountability, slow execution.
- **Leverage as a pure return enhancer**: the authors cite Jorion's study of LTCM (note 15).

## Worked Example
*1. The 60/40 risk split* (p. 278).
- Stocks: 60% weight, 15% standard deviation. Bonds: 40% weight, 5% standard deviation. Correlation 0.20. Both have a Sharpe ratio of 0.35.
- The authors state the result: "stocks contribute 92% to the risk of the asset mix".
- Check (the skill's arithmetic, using the authors' inputs). Weighted volatilities are 9.0 and 2.0. Portfolio variance = 81 + 4 + 2 × 0.2 × 9 × 2 = 92.2. Stock share = (81 + 3.6) ÷ 92.2 = 91.8%.

*2. Backward-looking versus valuation-based risk* (p. 285). A backward-looking system "would have allocated more to equity in 2007 (with the S&P 500 at 1500) than in 2003 (with the S&P 500 at 1000)". A valuation-based approach would have done the reverse.

*3. The authors' 2006-08 positioning* (p. 276), as they report it.
- Setting: "in the heyday of the leveraged finance market in 2006 and 2007, investors held large leveraged positions to squeeze the last juice out of tiny credit spreads".
- Their valuation process in 2007 gave: short credit, short carry, long volatility, long undervalued Asian currencies.
- In 2008, on valuation, they advised a defensive currency hedge for the non-euro part of the whole fund.
- Their point: such tilts can be passed up to the overall asset mix.

*4. Figure 14.2, January 2007 to December 2010* (pp. 273-276). The charts are lost. The text reports four findings from the authors' own fund:
- (A) The mandate's rolling 12-month correlations show it turned short equities and long bonds in 2008, and bullish again in 2009-10.
- (B) Average correlation among the mandate's own positions fell in 2008. It rose inside a typical pension mix and inside a fund of hedge funds.
- (C) The pension mix became more correlated with a standard hedge fund mix in 2008 and less with the mandate.
- (D) The CTA sleeve's 21-day returns against each underlying show a straddle shape.
These are results from one fund, selected and presented by its managers.

## Key Takeaways
1. Size by risk contribution. A 60/40 capital mix is about a 92/8 risk mix on the authors' inputs.
2. Tight spreads plus low volatility is the high-risk state.
3. Give the long-horizon valuation book real staying power, and still set stops and profit targets.
4. Use external managers only where they differ from what you can run inside.
5. Set leverage from stress-tested cash needs.
6. Route macro insights up to strategic allocation, rebalancing, hedging and benchmark choice.

# Chapter 3: Systematic Strategies: A Quantitative Approach to Global Macro

**Author**: Menachem Sternberg, Eagle Trading Systems (pp. 39-58)

## Core Idea
Systematic macro reads price behaviour as the summary of all information and all participants. It sizes by volatility, cuts losers by a preset stop and lets winners run. The author separates modern "tactical" systematic macro, which can stand aside, from the classic always-in-the-market trend-following CTA. He runs a systematic firm and argues its case.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The premise** (p. 40): price behaviour "encompasses the effect of a large body of information" and of traders, hedgers, investors and speculators with different objectives, timeframes and constraints. The "dynamic path of price behaviour" shows the market's "comfort levels and need to adjust to a new price level".
- **Five-step investment process** (p. 45):
  1. Quantitative analysis of historical price behaviour, pattern and volatility, over several timeframes.
  2. Decide whether it triggers a long, a short, or "sitting on the sidelines".
  3. Size the position on market volatility and on risk parameters for the market, the portfolio and the environment.
  4. Set a stop-loss level as a safety net.
  5. Apply dynamic rules for profit taking or position adjustment.
- **Cut losses, let profits run** (pp. 45-46): stops are tied to each trade. Dynamic rules keep winners. Result: a higher average gain on winners than average loss on losers. The strategy can "perform well even if less than 50% of their trades are successful".
- **Systematic macro versus classic CTA** (pp. 46-50, Figures 3.1 and 3.2):

  | Feature | Tactical systematic macro | Classic trend-following CTA |
  |---|---|---|
  | Position states | Long, short or none | Always long or short |
  | Entry trigger | Varied models, waits for a "clearer price picture" | Crossing of price moving averages |
  | High volatility | Reduce or exit | Stays in |
  | Profit taking | Partial, on profit objective or volatility spike | None described |
  | Stop | Trailing, to lock in profit | Exit is the reversal signal |
  | Weak spot | Not stated by the author | Choppy markets give "false" signals. Small losses "add up quickly" |
- **Structural advantages of futures-based trading** (pp. 50-51):
  1. Oversight by the CFTC and NFA. Many managers also register with the SEC.
  2. Exchange trading: transparency and independent daily valuation.
  3. Margin is a small share of notional. Most managers "do not need to borrow".
  4. Less counterparty exposure than securities and over-the-counter derivatives.
  5. Liquidity lets small and medium managers trade without moving prices.
- **Why it diversifies** (p. 52):
  - Stress periods require "many markets, across all sectors" to adjust with extended price moves.
  - Those moves grow when the economy has "little shock-absorbing capacity".
  - The author states the correlation pattern: positive with traditional portfolios when equities rise, "significant negative correlation" when equities fall.
- **Systematic versus discretionary** (pp. 53-57), as the author argues it:
  - Breadth: systematic covers more markets with one process.
  - Emotion: discretionary traders risk "falling in love" with positions and style drift.
  - Price level: systematic strategies are "ambivalent with respect to price levels". Value-minded traders step back at extremes, which is where large moves happen.
  - Timing: bottom-up systems build positions before others "unwind their previous state of mind". Latecomers meet worse volatility.
  - Concession: discretionary traders can take outsized positions on a fundamental view. That pays if right and costs heavily if analysis or timing is wrong.

## Key Concepts
- **Black box**: the investor's label for strategies whose formulas are trade secrets. The author's retort: the mind of a discretionary manager is also a black box (p. 43).
- **Model robustness**: "achieved by avoiding over-fitting and over-optimising the data" (p. 44).
- **Sidelines**: a deliberate no-position state when the picture is unclear or volatility is high.
- **Trailing stop**: a stop moved in the direction of profit so a winning trade cannot turn into a loss.
- **Bottom-up versus top-down**: analysing each market independently, versus adding an overall risk budget (p. 43).
- **Survival test**: long-lived managers that performed across environments raise investor confidence (p. 51).

## Mental Models
- Think of volatility as a participation dial. When it is elevated, participation falls (p. 41).
- Use "listen to the market" for regimes where policy distorts fundamentals. The author's case is the US yield curve in 2010-11.
- Think of a systematic process as the opposite of value averaging. It "tends to cut exposures and acknowledge that they are wrong" when the market moves against it (p. 41).

## Anti-patterns
- **Always-in systems in a choppy market**: repeated false signals (p. 49).
- **Giving back open profit**: the CTA approach "can often give back a significant part (or even all) of its unrealised profits" (p. 48).
- **Tying rates to fundamentals when policy sets them**: in 2010-11 the US curve was "openly and persistently manipulated" by quantitative easing and Operation Twist. Discretionary managers who related bonds to fundamentals struggled. Price-based systems caught the move to lower rates between spring and autumn 2011 (p. 56).
- **Lumping all quantitative strategies together** (p. 39).

## Worked Example

- *Swiss franc, tactical system*: stays out until the picture clears. Goes long. Takes partial profit several times and trails the stop. A correction exits the trade. A short attempt is minimally profitable. Waits some months. Goes long again, takes partial profit on a fast move, and exits when the market spikes on "extreme market volatility".
- *Swiss franc, CTA*: reverses long to short at once. Gains, then gives back over half in a sharp rally. Several long and short attempts all lose. A later long gains, then loses a large part in a sell-off before reversing.
- *LME aluminium, tactical system*: waits, shorts, takes partial profit when downside momentum fades, exits on the rally. Several good longs follow. Later attempts fail. The author's point: profits on winners "far outweighed the smaller losses".
- *LME aluminium, CTA*: a string of reversals. Three losing trades in a row sit between the winning ones.

## Key Takeaways
1. The stop is "the most important aspect of position sizing" (p. 41).
2. A win rate below 50% is acceptable if the payoff ratio is high.
3. The ability to hold no position is the author's main distinction between systematic macro and a classic CTA.
4. The author dates investor acceptance to 2000-02 and, decisively, 2008.
5. The chapter gives no formulas, parameters or backtest design. It describes a philosophy.

# Chapter 8: Geopolitical Risk in Global Macro Investing

**Author**: David Murrin, Emergent Asset Management (pp. 139-161). An amended chapter from his self-published book *Breaking the Code of History*.

## Core Idea
The author proposes a "Five Stages of Empire" cycle as a frame for geopolitical risk. He says plainly that it "is not a model in the traditional quantitative sense". It has no data inputs and no trading signals. It rests on three stated premises taken from technical analysis and analogy. Treat it as one practitioner's narrative lens. The chapter offers no test of it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The gap the author sees** (p. 139): there are "no geopolitical analogues" to VaR and no framework "linking geopolitical 'risk factors' to expected returns". Geopolitical analysis works as "a cottage industry".
- **Why it was ignored** (p. 140): the "golden age" of macro, 1987 to 1999, coincided with a unipolar moment. Geopolitical risk "faded into the background". The author says 9/11 and 2007-09 ended that.
- **Three premises** (p. 144):
  1. Recurring collective behavioural patterns seen in markets also drive geopolitical cycles. Approach history "from a market technician's perspective".
  2. The patterns repeat at every scale: tribe, nation, region, empire. The author ties this to the Elliott Wave principle and to Mandelbrot's fractals.
  3. Geopolitical risk is asymmetric: "slow build-up and fast release". An empire takes longer to grow than to decline.
- **Five Stages of Empire** (pp. 145-154). Power follows a skewed bell curve peaking "at about 60-70%" along the lifecycle.

  | Stage | Author's markers | Fiscal marker (Panel 8.2) |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1. Regionalisation | Growing population. States in one area amalgamate. Civil war often near the end (English, American, Chinese civil wars) | Debt-to-GDP rises, funded at home |
  | 2. Ascension to empire | Projection outward for resources. Young, risk-seeking population. "Income from expansion is vastly greater than expenditure" | Debt-to-GDP rises, funded at home |
  | 3. Maturity | Stable borders. Population growth slows. Prosperity. Internal power struggle near the end | "Empire dividend" brings the ratio to a low |
  | 4. Overextension | Cost of empire exceeds revenue. Will not cut social or defence spending or raise taxes. Runs deficits. Older, more conservative decision-makers | Ratio rises again, funded from outside the core |
  | 5. Decline and legacy | Collective denial. A symbolic event breaks the illusion. Rivals probe. Fast collapse. A value system remains as legacy | Spending falls. Ratio returns to a low |
  - Expansive phase traits: territorial expansion, risk orientation, infrastructure building, social cohesion, national pride, optimism. Contractive phase traits: shrinking influence, protectionism, emphasis on individual rights, social fragmentation, discord, pessimism (Figure 8.1).
  - The world tends to unipolarity as an empire rises and to multipolarity as it declines.
- **Fractal of regionalisation** (Panel 8.1, p. 148): the regional stage has its own five stages. The inflection point is regional maturity. If demographic momentum continues, the power bids for empire. It succeeds only if no rival exists, or if a rival "has reached the overextension stage" and left a vacuum. Failed bids named by the author: France against Britain, Germany in 1914 and 1939, Japan in World War Two.
- **Creditor leverage** (Panel 8.2, p. 155): early-stage debt is held by the population. Late-stage debt is held by foreigners, which gives them "control over empire finances and hence its rate of decline". The author's case: the US held British debt and used it to force withdrawal in the 1956 Suez Crisis.
- **The author's 2012 placement of actors** (pp. 155-158). These are his judgments:
  - Europe and Russia: late legacy or early regionalisation, with unfavourable demographics.
  - US: "late decline", expected to accelerate.
  - Middle East: late regionalisation. He reads the Sunni/Shia rivalry as a regional civil war.
  - Brazil and India: late regionalisation, unlikely to ascend "due to the absence of a power vacuum".
  - China: in ascension since the late 1990s. Favourable demographics "expected to peak around 2025".
- **Two practitioner postures** (pp. 158-159):
  - *Short-term trader*: the earthquake drill. Stay aware, map geopolitical stress scenarios, and "visualise" actions in an emergency.
  - *Long-term investor*: the case for explicit geopolitical analysis is stronger. Long horizons make a shock more likely. Less liquid relative-value positions "are bound to suffer the most" when slow risks release.

## Key Concepts
- **Core population and menial workforce**: the author's two-part social structure of an empire (p. 146).
- **Risk capital (demographic)**: his term for a young, male-heavy population's appetite for risk. He states China's population is "56% male". That is his figure. This skill has not verified it and you must not cite it as fact.
- **Kondratiev wave**: a commodity price cycle averaging about 53 years. The author says the world is in the ascending part "until 2025" (note 3).
- **Militarised Keynesianism**: defence spending used as fiscal stimulus (p. 160).
- **"Suez Moment"**: a creditor forcing a declining power to back down.
- **Legacy**: the value system that outlasts a fallen empire.

## Mental Models
- Think of trade links as raising "the threshold for all-out war". They do not remove it. The author's warning is 1914 (p. 146).
- Use the bubble analogy for slow geopolitical risk: ride it knowingly, with liquid instruments and asymmetric trades, because fading it early is "foolhardy and expensive" (p. 159).
- The author quotes Bruce Kovner on the trader's core skill: "the ability to imagine configurations of the world different from today and really believe it can happen".

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming geopolitical risk is priced**: the author says it is "not really priced in" (p. 159).
- **Holding illiquid relative-value positions with no geopolitical scenario.**
- **Trusting that trade prevents war.**
- **A caution on the chapter itself** (the skill's note, not the author's): the evidence is analogy, selected historical cases and two debt charts. The chapter states sweeping claims as certain, for example "There are no cases in history" of a wealthy country with strong demographics that did not militarise. It gives no falsifier, no dating rule and no base rates. Do not use the stage labels as a signal.

## Worked Example
British debt-to-GDP, 1702-1991 (Panel 8.2 and Figure 8.3, pp. 155-156). The chart is lost. The text and labels remain.
- Stages 1 and 2: the ratio climbs through the eighteenth-century wars to a peak around the Napoleonic Wars. The chart's axis runs to 300%. Debt is held at home.
- Stage 3, Pax Britannica: trade monopoly and peace pay the debt down to a low.
- Stage 4: the two world wars raise the ratio "to levels slightly higher than the previous peak". Foreign investors, mainly the US, hold the debt.
- Stage 5: the Suez Crisis of 1956 marks the end. Spending falls and the ratio returns to a low.
- The author then maps the US onto the same shape (Figure 8.4). The earlier US peak, labelled 1946, was held by the American public. The current debt "is owned by foreign powers, and particularly by China". He predicts the ratio "will rise steeply, until a sudden collapse".

The author's list of "likely" medium-term consequences, written in 2012 for the following 12 to 15 years (p. 160). They are his forecasts. The book cannot say whether they came true:
1. Highly inflationary policies in America and Europe.
2. Stagflation in the West.
3. "Militarised Keynesianism" in the US.
4. Protectionism towards foreign government investment.
5. America's "Suez Moment" with China.
6. The end of the US dollar's reserve monopoly.
7. Loss of safe-haven status for US and European government bonds.

## Key Takeaways
1. The author's usable claim is about shape: slow build-up, fast release.
2. His usable indicator is who holds a power's debt, and whether the holder is a rival.
3. Positions that depend on slow normalisation carry the most geopolitical exposure.
4. Everything else in the chapter is an untested narrative. Use it to generate scenarios. Do not use it to assign probabilities.

## Connects To
- **Ch 5**: a different non-economic lens, anchored in observable balance sheets.
- **Ch 7**: scenario stress tests are where a geopolitical scenario would enter a risk process.
- **Ch 1**: macro needs open trade and capital flows. Geopolitics can remove them.

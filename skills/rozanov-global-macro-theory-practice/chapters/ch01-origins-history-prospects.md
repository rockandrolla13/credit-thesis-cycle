# Chapter 1: Global Macro: Origins, History, Prospects

**Author**: Andrew Rozanov, Permal Group (pp. 1-20)

## Core Idea
Global macro has two lineages, a commodity-trader stream and an equity stream. It also has two temperaments: the trader who listens to price (Soros) and the researcher who holds against price (Robertson, the later Keynes). Risk control, not economic insight, decides who survives.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Five shared characteristics of global macro funds** (p. 1-2):
  1. Global scope.
  2. Multi-asset coverage.
  3. Top-down approach: position for broad market and asset class re-pricings.
  4. Focus on liquidity.
  5. Asymmetric trade construction: limited downside, unlimited upside.
  - "Style drift" is "built into the mandate".
- **Three dimensions of sub-strategy** (p. 2):
  1. Discretionary versus systematic.
  2. Fundamental versus technical.
  3. Trend following (momentum) versus relative value (mean reversion).
  - Systematic fundamental = typical of GTAA firms, leaning to relative value. Systematic technical = CTAs. Discretionary fundamental = one risk taker with a thesis. Discretionary technical is "relatively rare".
- **Four stages of modern macro** (pp. 8-9):
  1. Growth, from the early 1970s, dominated by Commodities Corporation. The commodity specialists "came first" and shaped the industry for 15 years.
  2. "Golden age", about 1987 to 2000, dominated by Soros and Tiger.
  3. 2000s: eclipsed by equity long/short and arbitrage. Macro's share of hedge fund assets fell to an all-time low of 15% in 2007.
  4. After 2007-09: acceptance in institutional portfolios.
- **Commodities Corporation risk system** (p. 10). The author says it survives in "practically all" macro firms:
  1. Treat each trader or module as its own profit-and-loss centre with a capital allocation.
  2. Raise the allocation after good performance. Cut it after bad.
  3. If the trader loses half the allocation, close all positions and impose a cooling period.
  4. The trader writes a memo on what happened and what was learned.
- **Soros method: "invest first, investigate later"** (p. 12):
  1. Treat a large unexplained move as a possible inflection or a change in the "rules of the game".
  2. Form a tentative thesis and take a small position at once, as a hypothesis test.
  3. If investigation explains the move and the trade makes money, raise size aggressively.
  4. If it loses and the hypothesis is wrong, close early.
  - "Listen to the market", not because it is right. Soros thought it mostly wrong, but strong enough to overwhelm a leveraged contrarian.
- **Tiger method** (pp. 12-13): exhaustive fundamental research first, then a position in size. A move against the position is not an exit signal and may be a chance to add. A large unexplained move is not a change in fundamentals.
- **Three developments that will shape macro in the 2010s** (pp. 15-17):
  1. The post-crisis environment: private deleveraging, rock-bottom rates, unorthodox policy, debt overhang. The "predictable pattern of normal business cycles and policy reactions has broken down".
  2. Emerging markets, China above all, are now needed to form a G10 view. Most macro firms lack matching local expertise.
  3. Institutionalisation. Short-term *macro trading* and long-term *macro investing* separate. The second is contrarian, research-based, relative-value and less liquid.

## Key Concepts
- **Reflexivity** (note 23): biased agents with imperfect knowledge can, if the bias self-reinforces, change the fundamentals.
- **Credit cycle strategy** (p. 4): Keynes's 1920s approach of switching assets on indicators that claimed to predict relative prices over the business cycle. He abandoned it.
- **Technical Computer System (TCS)** (p. 10): Commodities Corporation's trend-following system, first built by Frank Vannerson in 1970. It spawned the CTA industry.
- **Multi-strategy drift** (pp. 14-15): macro firms added credit, event-driven, statistical arbitrage, asset-backed and some private-equity-type books to attract institutions.

## Mental Models
- Use the Soros/Robertson split to classify a manager. Ask: is a price move against the position information, or an opportunity?
- Think of macro as needing a regime: floating exchange rates, free trade, free capital movement and predictable liquidity provision by monetary authorities (p. 7). Bretton Woods removed these and the style vanished "for almost three decades".
- A contrarian, value-seeking temperament "sits very uncomfortably" inside a leveraged short-term fund. The same person can flourish in an unleveraged long-term vehicle (p. 6).

## Anti-patterns
- **Brilliant economist, poor risk manager**: the author's first explanation for Keynes. He was "extremely stubborn during short-term market fluctuations", distracted by other pursuits, and did not learn from blow-ups (p. 6).
- **Diversifying macro into low-volatility strategies**: in 2007-09 strategies that looked low risk and uncorrelated "carried massive left-tail risks and became highly correlated". Core macro books outperformed while the firm's total return was dragged down. Some managers imposed gates and side pockets (p. 15).
- **Treating emerging markets as a sideshow** (p. 16).

## Worked Example
Keynes's syndicate (pp. 3-6), reconstructed.
- 1 January 1920: launch with family money. Long US dollar. Short German mark, French franc, Italian lira. Thesis: war-damaged economies inflate and their currencies fall.
- End of February: up more than 20%.
- Then a short sterling position lost when the Bank of England raised rates unexpectedly. A short-lived rally in European currencies then wiped out the leveraged fund's capital.
- He kept the short forward positions, funded by a bridge loan. By end-1920 he had repaid it with a small profit.
- He was wiped out by leverage three times: 1920-21, 1928-29 and 1937-38. The third came in his personal accounts, after he had already switched the endowment to value equity investing.
- The thesis was right each time the position survived. The author's lesson is about sizing and path, not about the view.

Second example, Tiger's copper short (p. 11): built in 1994-95 on fundamental research. Prices rose "for no apparent reason" and squeezed out other shorts. Robertson held and added. In spring 1996 a rogue-trader scandal at a Japanese trading house broke and the price collapsed.

## Key Takeaways
1. A correct macro thesis does not protect a leveraged position from the path.
2. The capital-allocation rule with a hard 50% loss stop is the oldest shared risk tool in the industry.
3. Macro and managed futures were, by the author's reading of Dow Jones Credit Suisse data, the only strategies with positive returns in 2007-09 and a positive long-run return (note 30).
4. Illiquid add-on books turn a liquid fund into a gated one in a crisis.
5. Expect a split between leveraged short-horizon trading and unleveraged long-horizon macro investing.

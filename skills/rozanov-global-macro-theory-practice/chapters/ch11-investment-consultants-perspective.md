# Chapter 11: Global Macro: An Investment Consultant's Perspective

**Author**: Simon Fox, Mercer (pp. 195-209)

## Core Idea
For institutions, macro arrived by two routes: as a sleeve in a fund of hedge funds, or as the descendant of tactical asset allocation and active currency mandates. Mercer's view is that macro belongs in the *growth* portfolio as a risk-reduction tool. It is not an asset class. Manager selection and position size carry the result.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Lineage** (pp. 195-196):
  - US: tactical asset allocation (TAA) on domestic stocks and bonds, then global (GTAA) in the 1990s, then non-benchmark assets such as commodities.
  - UK: active currency management in the early 2000s, then broader GTAA.
  - Both later adopted absolute return benchmarks and "decoupled" from the client's strategic mix. That blurred GTAA and macro.
- **Two-axis style map** (Figure 11.1, p. 197): fundamental versus technical on one axis, systematic versus discretionary on the other.

  | Quadrant | Type | Consultant's note |
  |---|---|---|
  | Fundamental, systematic | GTAA heritage. Economic modelling of over- and under-priced markets, fed to an optimiser | Largest exposure across Mercer clients at end-2011 |
  | Technical, systematic | Managed futures. Momentum | Second largest. Low correlation to the first type and to equities |
  | Fundamental, discretionary | Classic macro | "Most elusive" for direct allocators. Less institutionally friendly, often more expensive, "some of the richest return profiles" |
  | Technical, discretionary | Few managers | Not used as a category |
  - Specialists sit on the same map: active currency, active commodities, rates. Rates-focused macro "seems somewhat under-utilised".
- **Five sources of added value** (p. 199), especially for GTAA-heritage managers:
  1. Country selection within equities.
  2. Country selection within bonds.
  3. Equity/bond/cash allocation.
  4. Currency management.
  5. Commodity positions.
- **Why the returns should exist** (pp. 199-201):
  - *Trend*: explained by behaviour. Markets respond slowly to news through "anchoring and cognitive dissonance", then overshoot through "fair weather investing and fear and greed". Cited evidence: Miffre and Rallis (2007) on over 20 US commodity futures across 25 years. Baltas and Kosowski (2012).
  - *Fundamental*: value, carry and sentiment as "niche betas", plus informational advantage for discretionary managers.
  - *Price takers*: central banks, multinationals and tourists in currencies. Producers, hedgers and index investors in commodities. They are "fairly price insensitive".
  - *Fewer competitors*: fewer managers work top-down across markets than within them. Mercer believes inefficiencies between markets "are at least as significant and exploitable" as those within.
- **Three other attractions** (p. 201): low equity correlation, good liquidity relative to other alternatives, good transparency.
- **Base and growth portfolio** (pp. 205-206):
  - *Base*: matches liabilities and is defensive. High-quality sovereign debt, inflation-linked bonds, high-quality credit, cash.
  - *Growth*: the risk-seeking engine. Public equity is the cornerstone.
  - Alternatives serve three aims in the growth portfolio: diversify, raise expected return, add inflation sensitivity. Commodities and real assets serve the third. Private equity serves the second. Hedge funds, macro included, add most "as a risk-reduction tool".
- **One, two or many** (pp. 207-208): blend managers. The reasons: funds are small businesses, some will disappoint, and "hedge fund investors have few ways to limit the risks taken by a manager - the most effective measure is position size".

## Key Concepts
- **TAA / GTAA**: tactical tilts around a strategic asset mix, domestic then global.
- **Niche betas**: value, carry, sentiment and similar systematic return sources (p. 200).
- **Risk-averse month**: Mercer's label, from the Credit Suisse Global Risk Appetite index. About 10% of months since 1993 qualify (p. 202).
- **Headline risk**: the reputational cost of a hedge fund appearing in the press.
- **Limited liability backstop**: the investor's maximum theoretical loss is the amount allocated to the fund (p. 203).
- **The Greeks**: option sensitivities, which become a source of risk and return for discretionary managers using options (p. 199).

## Mental Models
- Think of macro as a risk reducer in the growth portfolio, not as a return enhancer.
- Use technical strategies as "a natural offset" to the fundamental bias of most institutional portfolios (p. 200).
- Handle macro's high volatility through the risk budget: size the allocation down. Do not screen the strategy out.
- Position size is the investor's only real risk control over a manager.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating macro as an asset class**: the universe "does not fulfil the criteria of an 'asset class'" (p. 208).
- **Generalising from the index**: the aggregate hides "some outstanding successes and some major failures".
- **Single-manager exposure.**
- **Assuming the edge is permanent**: "markets can remain irrational for significant periods" and fundamentals shift with government intervention. Portfolio construction and risk management "can be as important as getting the initial insights right" (p. 201).

## Worked Example
The consultant's sizing logic (pp. 202-203), reconstructed.
1. Concern: macro returns are more volatile than the hedge fund industry average and use embedded leverage.
2. Response: treat volatility in the risk budget and moderate the size.
3. Outcome reported: in equity-heavy countries such as the UK, investors became comfortable with "5-10% of total assets" across a portfolio of different macro strategies. Manager diversification is "very important" at that size.
4. Comfort with leverage came from two facts. The instruments are liquid, so leverage did not cause a squeeze in 2007-09. The fund vehicle has limited liability.
5. Fees: 1-2% base and 20% performance, "in line with the broader hedge fund industry". Investors accepted them on net-of-fee evidence. Mercer expects continued pressure.

Crisis data points the chapter gives:
- 2008 (note 2): S&P 500 down 37%. Dow Jones Credit Suisse Global Macro index "down only 5%". Managed Futures index up 18%.
- In risk-averse months since 1993, the only hedge fund indexes with positive average returns were managed futures, global macro and dedicated short bias (p. 202).

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify a manager on the two axes before comparing it with another.
2. The structural argument for macro alpha is price-insensitive counterparties.
3. Expect 5-10% of total assets as the scale institutions accepted, spread over several managers.
4. Macro kept redemptions open in the last crisis. That liquidity is part of what the investor buys.
5. A consultant with an advisory business in this area wrote the chapter. It reports client practice and Mercer's beliefs. It runs no tests of its own beyond the risk-aversion month count.

# Chapter 6: Emerging Markets in Global Macro Investing

**Author**: Gene Frieda, Moore Capital (pp. 97-118)

## Core Idea
An emerging market is a *situation*, not a country list. It is a place where markets deepen or disintegrate fast and where tails are extreme. Macro funds "bet on the credibility of macro regimes". The author shows a working method: a weighted scenario grid for the large economies, translated into themes, then into country trade ideas. He also shows where the method failed.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Emerging market "situation"** (pp. 98, 107): higher volatility, fat tails, opaque data, volatile policy, weak rule of law. Price risk "is often swamped" by expropriation, suspended convertibility or default. After 2007-09 the European periphery fits the description better than many emerging markets.
- **Two strains of emerging market trading** (pp. 99, 102-104):
  1. *Traditional*: uncorrelated special situations, going into or coming out of a crisis.
  2. *Post-Asia-crisis*: high-beta plays on global growth. The author calls the later thematic version "faddish".
- **Regime credibility questions** (p. 107). The author's list:
  1. Is the peg credible given the fundamentals?
  2. Can the central bank raise rates to defend it without undermining growth?
  3. Are real rates unnecessarily high after a swing from current account deficit to surplus?
  4. Can the country afford to restructure its banks after a credit bubble "without bankrupting itself"?
- **The two-objective conflict** (p. 101): in Asia in 1997 funds judged that reserves could not serve both a dollar peg and monetary policy loose enough for "increasingly credit-led" growth. Low volatility under the peg let them use large leverage against it.
- **Five macro trends from reserve accumulation, 2000s** (pp. 104-106):
  1. Intervention reflated emerging economies. Undervalued currencies became self-reinforcing. Sovereign spreads narrowed and a cycle of upgrades, equity gains and inflows followed.
  2. Loose conditions were re-exported as reserves bought US Treasuries.
  3. Reserves became a "fast ticket" to investment grade. That ended "original sin" and shifted managers to local currency debt.
  4. Intervention suppressed volatility. Falling volatility drew "ever-larger leveraged bets", this time on appreciation.
  5. A steadily depreciating dollar from 2002 added to the commodity rise.
- **Top-down plus bottom-up process** (pp. 107-108):
  1. Build scenarios for the US, Japan, China and Europe, with weight on central bank stances.
  2. Translate the themes to emerging markets.
  3. Run a separate bottom-up screen for country idiosyncrasies, conditioned on regime credibility.
  4. Work at the intersection: "the dynamic relationship between global financial conditions and a country's macro policy settings". An imbalance that is unsustainable in one state of the world persists in another.
- **Weighted scenario grid** (Table 6.1, p. 110): for each of Good, Neutral, Bad, assign a probability per region, then weight. "Joint probability = sum of country weights × scenario probabilities." Weights: US 35%, Europe 35%, China 20%, Other 10%.
- **Positioning toolkit** (p. 114): surveys of positioning, sentiment and flows. Non-commercial futures exposure. Option skew in risk reversals. Price action: is a market "failing to react to good news or overreacting to bad news?"
- **Four stylised facts where rich-country rules fail** (p. 117):
  1. Higher GDP growth does not mean stronger asset returns, for currencies or equities.
  2. Asset booms occur more often and are "mistaken for structural breaks". Busts destabilise more.
  3. Nominal rates are divorced from nominal growth and track the exchange rate.
  4. Small economies cannot absorb capital flows with standard tools. Expect transaction taxes and capital controls.

## Key Concepts
- **Crawling peg**: a peg that allows a small scheduled depreciation (Mexico, Turkey).
- **Original sin**: inability to issue much debt in one's own currency.
- **"Thin air"**: the author's 2H 2011 label. Modest progress takes great effort and downside is large on any shock.
- **KIKO / TARN**: knock-in knock-out options and target accrual redemption notes sold at "zero cost" to corporates. Long local currency, capped gain, unlimited loss accruing at about twice the rate.
- **Payer / receiver**: paying fixed is a bet on higher rates. Receiving fixed is the opposite (note 8).
- **One-touch**: a deep out-of-the-money option that pays if spot touches the strike (note 9).
- **Push and pull factors**: the economists' frame for flows to emerging markets (p. 106).

## Mental Models
- Think of yield as the price of a government promise. In emerging markets "these promises tend to be backed up by higher-than-normal yields".
- Use "vulnerability and consequences" over triggers. "Often too much time is spent trying to predict triggers."
- Look for trades that pay "under a variety of scenarios".
- Treat low volatility produced by official intervention as a source of leverage build-up, in either direction.
- Illiquidity "only manifests itself when the investor is trying to exit" (note 6).

## Anti-patterns
- **The 3% current account rule**: deficits above 3% of GDP were held to be unsustainable. It fit the 1990s. It "has failed miserably since the early 2000s". Turkey ran 4.6% in 2005 and nearly 10% in 2011.
- **Trusting positioning data**: measures are "partial and static". Most macro funds missed the KIKO exposure because it sat between banks and corporates.
- **Importing developed-market rules of thumb**: "hazardous to one's investment health".
- **Bank trade ideas in tiny markets**: a 20% move is no use if scale is impossible (note 6).
- **Mistaking a boom for a structural break.**

## Worked Example
*1. Mexico 1990-94* (pp. 100-101). Inflation fell from a peak of 180% in 1988 to under 20% at the start of 1992 under a crawling peg. Three-month rates fell from 24% (start of 1991) to 11% (early 1992). Domestic demand boomed. The current account deficit went from 2% of GDP in early 1991 to 5.5% by end-1992. In 1994 the Chiapas uprising (1 January) and the Colosio assassination (23 March) ended foreign confidence. The central bank spent most reserves. The peso floated on 20 December 1994.

*2. Turkey 2000-11* (p. 108). Deficit of 3.7% of GDP in 2000. The peg broke in early 2001. The lira fell 60% nominal and 35% real trade-weighted in nine months. Surplus of 1.9% in 2001. Then deficits again, as above. The lira weakened materially only from late 2010, and the central bank stabilised it with modest rate rises while real rates were negative.

Themes the author drew: search for "alternative" safe havens. Fund in euros and sterling, not only dollars. A poor backdrop for equities. Emerging equity underperformance during QE2 as a "canary in the coal mine". No major emerging market easing, but hikes could be priced out.

The author's trade ideas, as he recorded them in mid-2011. They are historical, not current advice:
- Long Swiss franc, Singapore dollar, Canadian dollar, Swedish krona. Local currency debt in Brazil, Mexico, Korea, South Africa.
- Short equity-sensitive emerging currencies, the Indian rupee first.
- Short central Europe. Relative value: short Polish zloty versus Czech koruna.
- Long Asian currencies, biased to low yielders with strong current accounts.
- Short Turkish lira and pay five-year cross-currency swaps.
- Euro as funding currency. Short Spanish and Italian sovereign debt.
- Receive two-year rates in South Africa, Malaysia, Mexico.
- Short oil proxies (rouble, Norwegian krone). Long China-sensitive commodity proxies (Canadian dollar, rupiah, rand).
- China credit: Chinese bank equity and CDS. Hong Kong bank credit-to-GDP went from 178% (end-2007) to 264% (May 2011). Buy a one-year 7.85 US$/HK$ one-touch for 4% of payout, that is US$40,000 for US$1 million.

The author's review (p. 114):
1. The macro backdrop "proved generally correct". The translation into trades "was far from perfect". He does not give a trade-by-trade score.
2. The list was ideas, not a portfolio. No sizing.
3. The view was strategic, six months. It said nothing on tactics.
4. The asymmetric China trades are his model for what a strategic outlook should find.

*4. KIKOs, 2008* (pp. 115-116). The Brazilian real, Mexican peso, Polish zloty and Korean won fell sharply after Lehman with no large macro imbalance behind them. The cause was corporate exotic options. The chapter cites an IMF estimate of US$530 billion of direct cost to firms.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask whether the regime's promise is credible under the current global liquidity state, not in the abstract.
2. Write the scenario grid before the event. Its use is "making adjustments to strategy as these different combinations materialise".
3. A correct macro call does not guarantee the trades. Track them apart.
4. Hidden derivative positioning can make a "liquid" market vanish.
5. EMBI Global spread reached 151 basis points in mid-2007 while the index's average rating was BB+, one notch below investment grade (p. 106, note 3). The author reads that as volatility suppression, not as credit quality.
6. "Think the unthinkable and do not expect normal rules to apply."

# Patterns: Global Macro: Theory and Practice (ed. Rozanov)

Each pattern is one contributor's method. The chapter cite names the author. The book does not present them as one system.

## Weighted scenario grid (Frieda)
**When to use**: a half-year strategic outlook that must turn into themes. (Ch 6)
**How**: (1) Define Good, Neutral and Bad states for each large region, in observable terms. (2) Give each state a probability within its region. (3) Weight the regions (the author used US 35%, Europe 35%, China 20%, Other 10%). (4) Joint probability = sum of weight × probability. (5) Write the market implication of each cell. (6) Draw themes, then run a bottom-up country screen for starting position and likely policy reaction.
**Trade-offs**: gives ideas, not a portfolio and not sizes. In the author's own case the backdrop was right and the trade translation "far from perfect".

## Regime credibility check (Frieda)
**When to use**: any peg, managed currency, yield commitment or bank rescue. (Ch 6)
**How**: ask four questions. Is the promise credible on fundamentals? Can the central bank raise rates without undermining growth? Are real rates higher than the external balance now needs? Can the state afford the bank clean-up? Then ask whether today's global liquidity lets the imbalance persist.
**Trade-offs**: thresholds are state-dependent. The 3%-of-GDP current account rule fit the 1990s and failed after 2000.

## Reaction-function study (Wilson)
**When to use**: a market dominated by one powerful actor. (Ch 5)
**How**: (1) Name the actor with power over the price. (2) Read its speeches and published data. (3) Study its mandate, legal base and political dependants. (4) Watch for framework breakdown: the economy or the market has moved too far for the actor's model. (5) Find the balance-sheet or political pressure point and track it in real time.
**Trade-offs**: demanding, and often gives "unremarkable conclusions". It tells what the actor must do, with limited help on timing until the pressure point is hit.

## Clean expression of a view (Schachter)
**When to use**: choosing instruments for a thesis. (Ch 7)
**How**: list every factor each leg responds to. Remove or replace legs that carry a factor outside the thesis. The author's case replaces a Euribor future with an EONIA swap to remove interbank credit and liquidity risk.
**Trade-offs**: the purer instrument may be less liquid or cost more.

## Exit before entry (Schachter)
**When to use**: every trade. (Ch 7)
**How**: assume the market turns against the position. Set the exit from position size, risk tolerance, conviction, volatility and, where useful, chart levels. Prefer structures with a known, limited downside.
**Trade-offs**: limiting downside with options lowers expected return.

## Cut, don't hedge (Tropin, Schachter)
**When to use**: a position or aggregate exposure must come down fast. (Ch 2, Ch 7)
**How**: reduce or close the position. If illiquidity forces a proxy hedge, lift it as soon as a direct exit or direct hedge is possible.
**Trade-offs**: selling into a falling market costs. A proxy hedge costs basis risk and, in a hedging book, has no clear owner.

## Loss limit with cooling period (Commodities Corporation, via Rozanov)
**When to use**: allocating capital to traders or modules. (Ch 1, Ch 7)
**How**: treat each as a profit-and-loss centre. Raise capital after gains, cut after losses. At a loss of half the allocation, close everything, pause, and require a written review. Decide between an absolute loss limit and a drawdown limit.
**Trade-offs**: a drawdown limit protects past gains and discourages adding risk when the trader is doing well.

## Stop-loss suitability test (Yelnik)
**When to use**: before adding a stop rule to a systematic strategy. (Ch 4)
**How**: test whether the strategy's own returns are positively auto-correlated. If yes, a stop can add value. If no, as in many value-based GTAA models, it tends to subtract. Define the re-entry rule at the same time.
**Trade-offs**: without a stop, investors and staff must tolerate deeper drawdowns.

## Value plus momentum pairing (Yelnik)
**When to use**: building a systematic macro book. (Ch 4)
**How**: run a valuation model (for example PPP in currencies) beside a trend model (for example 20-day against 60-day moving average). Check the correlation of their returns. In the author's A$/¥ sample it was 0.2.
**Trade-offs**: the value leg can lose for years against a trend. The trend leg bleeds in range-bound, volatile markets.

## Tactical participation rules (Sternberg)
**When to use**: designing or assessing a trend system. (Ch 3)
**How**: allow three states (long, short, none). Size by volatility. Attach a stop to each trade. Take partial profit on a target or a volatility spike. Trail the stop. Stand aside when the picture is unclear.
**Trade-offs**: the chapter gives no parameters or tests. Staying out can miss the first part of a move.

## Back-test start-date check (Yelnik)
**When to use**: judging any back-test. (Ch 4)
**How**: move the start date by a year or two and recompute. The author's example fell from an information ratio of 0.31 to about half with a 15-month shift.
**Trade-offs**: none. It only removes false comfort.

## Scenario stress test, five steps (Schachter)
**When to use**: measuring loss under an imagined break. (Ch 7)
**How**: (1) choose a global scenario, (2) pick core markets or historical dates, (3) set core shocks, (4) propagate through betas after deciding which arbitrage relations hold, (5) re-price and scale. Add a perturbation check.
**Trade-offs**: subjective at each step. Says nothing about nearby shocks. Limited to what can be imagined.

## Factor-count diversification check (Schachter)
**When to use**: a book with many positions. (Ch 7)
**How**: run principal component analysis on the book. Count the factors that explain most of the volatility. Manage concentration at factor level.
**Trade-offs**: factor structure shifts with the risk regime.

## Leverage read for a derivatives book (Casano)
**When to use**: assessing a macro fund's leverage. (Ch 10)
**How**: compute margin-to-equity and the capital utilisation ratio. Then check volatility of the instruments, relative-value versus directional mix, liquidity, and correlations. Track all of them monthly with one method. Investigate jumps.
**Trade-offs**: some positions need little margin and are very volatile, so no ratio is sufficient.

## Short-volatility signature test (Kodmani and Rozanov, Berkelaar)
**When to use**: comparing strategies or managers with smooth returns. (Ch 12, Ch 13)
**How**: compute skewness, excess kurtosis, equity correlation and one-month serial correlation beside the Sharpe ratio. Strongly negative skew with high kurtosis marks a short-volatility profile. High serial correlation marks smoothing. Unsmooth before optimising.
**Trade-offs**: one outlier month can dominate the higher moments. Check the data.

## Post-dislocation event study (Kodmani and Rozanov)
**When to use**: asking how a strategy behaves after a crisis starts. (Ch 12)
**How**: fix a list of dislocation dates. Compute one-, two- and three-year annualised returns from each, including the event month. Average and rank. Repeat on a second index family.
**Trade-offs**: eight events. Index biases flatter the result.

## Equal-Sharpe optimisation (Berkelaar)
**When to use**: testing whether an asset diversifies, apart from its past return. (Ch 13)
**How**: set every asset's Sharpe ratio to one value. Optimise on mean/variance and on mean/expected tail loss. Compare weights.
**Trade-offs**: unconstrained weights are illustrations. They are not an allocation.

## Risk-contribution check (de Vrij, Hoevenaars, van der Sluis)
**When to use**: any capital-weighted mix. (Ch 14)
**How**: compute each asset's share of portfolio variance. On the authors' inputs a 60/40 mix puts 92% of risk in stocks. Rebalance by risk, using leverage on the low-risk asset if needed.
**Trade-offs**: the right forward-looking risk measure differs by trade. Leverage raises liquidity and counterparty demands.

## Three-drift monitor (Kodmani and Rozanov)
**When to use**: monitoring a macro manager, where ordinary style drift is allowed. (Ch 12)
**How**: test for competence drift, liquidity drift, and discretionary overrides of a systematic model becoming routine.
**Trade-offs**: needs position-level transparency.

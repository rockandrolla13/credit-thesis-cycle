# Section 1: Introduction (main note)

Source: Berrospide, Cai, Lewis-Hayre, Zikes, FEDS Note, 23 May 2025, Section 1 and footnotes 2 to 4.

## Core Idea
Banks lend to private credit vehicles through credit lines. The note measures that lending, describes the loans, and tests what a full drawdown does to bank capital and liquidity. Its verdict: the financial stability implications "seem limited so far", but the link is "growing fast".

## Definitions the note uses
- **Private credit**: no universal definition. It "generally refers to direct loans made to mid-market businesses typically by non-bank vehicles".
- **Private credit vehicles**: private debt (PD) funds and Business Development Companies (BDCs). The note's totals cover only these two types.
- **NBFI**: nonbank financial intermediary. "Other NBFIs" is the note's comparison group.

## The note's five summary findings (Sec 1)
1. Bank credit to private credit vehicles is "still small relative to other NBFIs, but it is growing fast".
2. Credit quality of these bank loans "is high".
3. The loans have higher utilization rates and higher interest rates than loans to other NBFIs. They also have lower default probabilities and lower delinquency rates.
4. Private credit vehicles are "less engaged in maturity transformation": they use long-term capital for long-term lending. The authors read bank credit lines behind this lending as partly "efficient specialization in parts of the credit chain".
5. Banks are "expanding their strategic partnerships with private credit". This "warrants close monitoring".

## Why immediate risk looks limited, and why it is hard to judge (Sec 1)
- Limited: private credit vehicles have "moderate use of leverage and long-term capital lockups".
- Hard to judge: "the lack of transparency and understanding of the interconnectedness" between private credit and the rest of the system.

## Literature map the note gives (Sec 1)
Two competing readings of why private credit grew. The note reports both and does not adjudicate.

**Reading A: bank regulation pushed lending out of banks.**
- Chernenko, Erel, Prilmeier (2022): rules that limit bank lending to unprofitable and risky firms push those borrowers to nonbanks.
- Loumioti (2022): direct lenders step in when bank loan and securitized debt issuance weakens.
- Davydiuk, Marchuk, Rosen (2024): BDCs substitute for traditional lenders.
- Avalos, Doerr, Pinter (2025): private credit matters more where policy rates are lower, bank regulation is tighter and banks are less efficient.

**Reading B: banks and nonbanks are interdependent.**
- Chernenko, Ialenti, Scharfstein (2025): private credit funds run much lower leverage than banks, so capital rules alone do not explain the growth. Banks may find it more profitable to lend to risky corporates indirectly, through loans to private credit vehicles or affiliated funds.
- Acharya, Cetorelli, Tuckman (2024): banks act as liquidity providers for nonbanks and are not displaced by them.

**Other studies on confidential supervisory data.**
- Haque, Jang, Wang (2025): the largest US banks increasingly extend credit lines to BDCs. This lending helped sustain credit supply to middle-market firms during the recent monetary tightening.
- Degerli and Monin (2024), fn 3: dry powder at the funds also enabled continued lending through the tightening.
- Aldasoro and Doerr (2025), fn 3: private credit loans are increasingly collateralized. They "surmise" the collateral channel may make private credit more pro-cyclical.
- Levin and Malfroy-Camine (2025): similar growth in large-bank lending to private equity and private credit funds.

## What this note adds over Levin and Malfroy-Camine (Sec 1)
- A broader range of lenders for BDCs, by using BDC public filings.
- Riskiness measures for the bank exposures.
- The effect of credit line drawdowns on bank capital and liquidity.

# Chapter 1: Theories of Human Rationality and International Relations

## Core Idea
Before modeling geopolitics with games, fix a realistic picture of the agents: bounded, diachronic, norm- and commitment-driven rather than pure maximizers. Then fix a *worldview* (an IR theory) and a *unit of analysis* (individual, state, interstate) and ask whether a driver is internal or external to the actor. The chapter is conceptual: it has no equations, tables or code.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Geopolitical risk (portfolio definition)** (1.6): "any potential detriment to a portfolio's positions stemming from political and political-economic actions within and among states and/or non-state actors."
  - When to use: as the relevance filter. An investor, unlike a government or academic analyst, must first decide which developments matter to the portfolio.

- **Three levels of analysis** (1.6; adapted from Waltz 1959, who called them "images"):
  1. **Individuals**: state and military leaders, plus non-state individuals (UN Secretary-General, heads of large terrorist groups). A leader can override an otherwise attractive investment climate for the length of their tenure.
  2. **States**: "the aggregation of institutions which regulate the domestic workings of a given country." A capable leader is still constrained by the country's political, fiscal and monetary institutions. State-level risk is typical of emerging markets (immature judiciary, ill-formed elections and separation of powers).
  3. **Interstate relations**: political, military and economic relations between states. The level investors see most because market consequences are readily observable.
  - How: the levels are not mutually exclusive. Each informs the portfolio to a different degree, so assess risk "in an all-things-considered manner."
  - Interaction: an effective (ineffective) leader may lessen (increase) the risk carried by the state's institutions.

- **Worldview** (1.6; from the German *Weltanschauung*): a theoretical orientation that fixes assumptions about the primary goals of international actors and the nature of the international system. The analogue of an investor's mental model of market drivers. Build it *before* the analytical framework.
  - **Realism**: international relations are a struggle for power between states trying to survive in an anarchic world. States seek *relative* gains, so the game is treated as zero-sum.
    - **Defensive Realism** (Waltz 1979): states are "security maximizers."
    - **Offensive Realism** (Mearsheimer 2001): states seek power, with the goal of regional hegemony.
  - **Liberalism** (Keohane 1984 for the neoliberal view): states recognize interdependence, build institutions for jointly beneficial outcomes and care more about *absolute* gains.
  - Why it matters downstream: the worldview decides whether payoffs in Chapter 2 games are scored as absolute or relative (see ch02, section 2.5).

- **Internal versus external drivers** (1.6): for any state action, ask whether the driver sits inside or outside the actor.
  - Trade policy: tariffs raised under foreign competitive pressure = external driver. Domestic industries or free-trade groups lobbying for lower barriers = internal driver.
  - Security policy: "hawks" versus "doves" = internal. A change in an adversary's aggression = external.
  - Limit: jockeying among internal actors is present only where there is "some semblance of a democratic system." In totalitarian systems (the text names Azerbaijan, North Korea, Turkmenistan) only small elite groups influence policy.

- **Three corrections to the maximizer model** (1.4): human reason is (1) **bounded**, (2) often **diachronic** (present gains given up for future ones), (3) often **non-instrumental** (desires are not always the prime motivator).

- **Axelrod and Hamilton's three requirements for cooperation to emerge** (1.4; Axelrod and Hamilton 1981):
  1. The parties are in an **ongoing relationship** (one-off or sporadic encounters do not support cooperation).
  2. The parties can **identify each other**.
  3. The parties have **information about how others behaved in the past**.
  - Why it works: reputation needs repetition. Without identity or memory, an agent is not accountable and is motivated to behave selfishly.

## Key Concepts
- **Collective action problem**: cooperation and conflict are two answers to "Should we cooperate?" and "How do we cooperate?" (1.1).
- **Synchronic vs diachronic utility**: shorter-term versus longer-term utility. Alliances and new businesses accept early losses for later gains (1.1).
- **Public good**: a good with **jointness** (once produced for one, consumable by more at no extra cost) and **non-excludability** (nobody can be excluded once it exists) (1.1).
- **Free-rider problem**: the incentive to defect from producing a public good while still consuming it. Rational egoists provide it at suboptimal levels as long as their shortfall does not sink provision (1.1).
- **Bounded rationality** (Simon 1957): limits on computing the rational act and on carrying it out. Still instrumental ("means-end") rationality (1.2).
- **Satisficer** (Simon): accepts any decision that is "good enough" and stops searching there (1.2).
- **Constrained maximizer** (Gauthier 1986): chooses subject to constraints, long-term plans and commitments made. Complies with mutually advantageous commitments if others do (1.2).
- **Rational search**: stop searching when the benefit of a better option is equal to or less than the cost of further search (1.2).
- **Commitment** (Sen 1977, 1985, 2002): action taken independently of its envisaged consequences. A rational acknowledgment of the rules of group membership, not a passionate attachment (1.3).
- **Social norm**: a boundary on permissible action. Sunstein (1996): social norms determine what counts as rational, so individual rationality depends on collective rationality (1.5).
- **Defeasibility conditions**: the conditions that block a norm from applying. Two agents with equal commitment to a norm can still disagree about these (1.5).
- **Social role**: a set of norms binding anyone who occupies the role. The role sets the criteria for rational behavior (the firefighter who walks away is sanctioned, the passing civilian is not) (1.5).
- **Theory of conformity** (Bernheim 1994): agents who value status in a group conform to its norms. **Individualists** have "extreme preferences" and do not. Agents with several social identities can be said to have several utility functions (1.5).
- **"Hot" vs "frozen" conflicts**: active tension versus disputes that "may reignite at any moment" (1.6).

## Mental Models
- **Think of cooperation as an adaptation to necessity.** The text's broadly Hobbesian reading: agents form stable cooperative relationships because they cannot overcome environmental challenges alone (1.4).
- **Think of norms as cached decisions.** Norms emerge when a decision exceeds deliberative capacity, or when re-deciding a recurrent case is costly (1.5).
- **Use reactions, not behavior, to identify a norm.** Affirmative or negative social reactions define conformance. The text ties this to Wittgenstein's "agreement in judgment" (1.5).
- **Cooperation is more than coordination.** Rawls (2001): cooperation needs publicly recognized rules the parties accept, which lets deviation be sanctioned. Coordination by decree is not cooperation (1.5).
- **Use satisficing and constrained maximization as two routes to a global optimum.** Both accept a locally sub-best act because locally best may not be globally best (1.2).

## Anti-patterns
- **"Op-ed columns with charts"** (Preface): geopolitical research with no analytical depth, no historical context and no systematic, mathematical framework. This is the practice the book is written against.
- **Commenting on every current event** (Preface): most firms lack the resources to hold thoughtful views on all impactful factors.
- **Reading geopolitics from time series alone** (1.1): numerical data "precludes" rules, habits and conventions, which drive much cooperation.
- **Treating headline intensity as market reach** (1.6): Middle East hostilities grab headlines but generally pressure only oil markets. US/China interactions "almost always have global implications."
- **Tracking only hot conflicts** (1.6): frozen conflicts can reignite. The text calls the China-India border conflict "generally underappreciated" by investors.
- **Assuming a pure maximizer** (1.2): descriptively wrong for both *homo economicus* and *homo politicus*.

## Worked Example
The chapter gives three short cases, one per level or reach.

1. **Individual level, Turkey.** The text says Turkey has deep structural problems, compounded since 2003 under Erdogan, with institutional erosion running "from the judiciary to monetary policy." The conclusion it draws for emerging-market investors: while the country is governed by "Erdoganism", give the Turkish market "a strategic underweight relative to a given benchmark" or zero it out. The inference pattern is: individual-level driver, institutional channel, benchmark-relative stance.
2. **Interstate level, Russia.** Sanctions by the US and EU began in March 2014 in response to the annexation of Crimea and Russian-backed unrest in Eastern Ukraine. The text states the Russian market "did not recover its July 2013 level until January 2018."
3. **Reach, US/China versus the Middle East.** US/China conflict is multifaceted (trade, intellectual property, Taiwan, human rights in Hong Kong and Xinjiang) and global in impact. Middle East hostilities are localized to oil unless they escalate, which the text treats as a lower-probability case.

## Key Takeaways
1. State the worldview first. Relative-gain (Realist) and absolute-gain (Liberal) assumptions give different payoffs for the same event.
2. Tag every theme with its level (individual, state, interstate) and its driver (internal, external).
3. Check the three Axelrod-Hamilton conditions before assuming cooperation can hold: ongoing relationship, identifiable parties, known history.
4. Model agents as bounded and commitment-bound. Commitments and norms belong in the payoff structure (Chapter 2 does this with a parameter).
5. Separate reach from noise: ask which markets an interstate conflict can move, not how loud it is.
6. Keep a list of frozen conflicts alongside hot ones.
7. State-level risk is hard to quantify. Country scores are the common device, and the text treats them only as a rough analogue of credit ratings.

# Patterns: The Economics of Inflation (Bresciani-Turroni)

Techniques the author uses, restated so they can be applied to another case. Chapter codes as in the SKILL.md index.

## Deficit-or-exchange causality test
**When to use**: someone claims the currency fell for external reasons and that the deficit is only a result.
**How**: (1) Use the monthly rise in floating debt as the deficit gauge. (2) Find a spell of stable exchange. A deficit that persists there was not caused by the exchange (Germany 1920-21: gold mark 14.2 to 14.9, deficit 60 per cent of spending). (3) Find a spell of collapsing exchange. Check whether the deficit widened (second half of 1919: it did not). (4) Check that depreciation hits receipts and outlays alike.
**Trade-offs**: needs reliable debt data. Fails in the last phase, when causation runs both ways. (Ch 2)

## Limit test for a non-monetary depreciation
**When to use**: a depreciation is blamed on capital flight, a transfer, speculation or an import surge.
**How**: ask where the money comes from. With fixed money incomes, dearer imports become prohibitive, asset sellers need cash buyers, and cheap assets attract foreigners. A fall that goes on and on implies new money or credit.
**Trade-offs**: the author gives the bound only loosely (15 to 30 per cent for a pure trade shock). (Ch 2, Ch 2a)

## Decomposing an exchange-rate move
**When to use**: separating monetary from real causes of a currency move.
**How**: index of exchange rate = ratio of export-price indices times index of the barter terms of trade. The first factor passes through in full. The second is damped when trade is large and demand is elastic.
**Trade-offs**: built on a two-country, two-good model with constant costs. Domestic price indices fit only roughly. (Ch 2a)

## Coefficient of divergence
**When to use**: measuring how far a currency's external value has fallen below its internal value.
**How**: D = index of the gold price of the currency divided by the price parity (home price index over world price index). Track it monthly. D above 1 means cheap exports and an eroding tax base. D below 1 means home prices have overshot.
**Trade-offs**: depends on comparable price indices. The author warns that the German and US indices covered different goods. (Ch 3, Ch 13)

## Lead-lag "fan" of five series
**When to use**: locating an inflation in its life cycle.
**How**: rebase the dollar rate, import prices, home wholesale prices, cost of living, and circulation with floating debt to 100. Rank them. Money ahead of prices: early phase. Exchange ahead, prices behind: divergence phase. All in one bundle: terminal phase.
**Trade-offs**: descriptive. It does not by itself say what is causing what. (Ch 1)

## Velocity index from published data
**When to use**: gauging confidence in a currency.
**How**: velocity index = price index divided by money index, both on the same base. Compute once with wholesale prices (firms) and once with retail prices (households). A gap between the two marks a flight led by business.
**Trade-offs**: assumes constant real transactions. The author calls the result "very rough". (Ch 4)

## Real-balance collapse check
**When to use**: officials cite low real money as proof of tight policy.
**How**: compute the real value of the circulation. If it is falling while nominal issues accelerate, read it as flight. If it reaches a small fraction of normal, look for substitute monies.
**Trade-offs**: none stated. The author treats the opposite reading as a sophism. (Ch 4, Ch 9)

## Sensitivity ladder for budgets
**When to use**: forecasting whether depreciation widens a deficit.
**How**: sort receipts and expenses into four classes: moves with the exchange, with wholesale prices, with cost of living, or not at all. Add collection lags. The sign of the net effect depends on the mix.
**Trade-offs**: the mix changes as the inflation matures. In Germany the effect flipped in mid-1922. (Ch 2)

## Correct averaging of a collapsing exchange rate
**When to use**: converting a period's nominal totals to real or gold values.
**How**: prefer the arithmetic mean of daily rates of the hard unit in the weak currency. Never divide by a harmonic-type average. Never mix an arithmetic price index with a harmonic exchange average.
**Trade-offs**: the arithmetic mean is exact only if the gold value of daily transactions is constant. (Ch 3)

## Real-wage deflation with a spending lag
**When to use**: measuring real wages in fast inflation.
**How**: divide the wage paid at the end of period t by the price index of period t+1, when it is spent. Note pay frequency.
**Trade-offs**: breaks down in hyperinflation, when wages are spent at once and paid several times a week. (Ch 8)

## Phase model of inflation's trade effects
**When to use**: predicting the trade balance under depreciation.
**How**: home gold prices above world: imports favoured. Exchange ahead of home prices: exchange dumping, exports favoured. Home prices overshoot: true dumping and import pressure. For an industrial exporter expect imports of raw materials to rise first.
**Trade-offs**: trade values recorded in the falling currency are unreliable. Use volumes. (Ch 6)

## Regime map for equities in an inflation
**When to use**: judging whether shares will hedge inflation.
**How**: check (1) whether rival hedges such as foreign exchange are open, (2) whether money is abundant or scarce, (3) dividend policy and dilution, (4) who can gain control. Deflate the index three ways: by the exchange, wholesale prices and cost of living.
**Trade-offs**: yield still anchors value. The author rejects pure "intrinsic value" pricing. (Ch 7)

## Winners-and-losers ledger
**When to use**: forecasting the distributional and political effects of an inflation.
**How**: list holders of nominal claims (losers) and nominal debtors with real assets (winners). Trace second-round transfers, such as rent control lowering wages and so raising profits. Expect concentration, since debtors are fewer than creditors.
**Trade-offs**: country structure matters. Banks lost in Germany and gained in Hungary. (Ch 8)

## Stabilisation feasibility checklist
**When to use**: assessing whether a hyperinflation can be stopped quickly.
**How**: check that (1) the real value of the currency is tiny, (2) substitute monies fill the gap, (3) the short-term debt and foreign-held balances are wiped out, (4) central bank lending to the State stops on a fixed date, (5) the new issue has a credible cap that the issuer will defend, (6) credit to business is then restricted when the exchange weakens.
**Trade-offs**: needs a transitional source of State finance. Germany used the capped rentenmark issue. (Ch 9)

## Diagnosing a stabilisation crisis
**When to use**: after an inflation or a credit boom ends.
**How**: look for very high interest rates, idle capital-goods plant, unsold basic materials, a jump in consumer imports, strong consumer trades, and failures among recently built conglomerates. Read these together as too much fixed capital against the flow of consumption goods.
**Trade-offs**: foreign loans relieve the shortage but can prolong the adjustment and remove the interest-rate check. (Ch 10, Ch 12)

## Wage-price ratio as an employment indicator
**When to use**: explaining unemployment in a rigid-wage economy.
**How**: plot nominal wage index over a price index against unemployment. Rising ratio without matching productivity gains goes with rising unemployment.
**Trade-offs**: the author calls his own proof "somewhat vague" and warns against single-cause readings. (Ch 8, Ch 12)

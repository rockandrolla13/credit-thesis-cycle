# Chapter V: The Influences of the Depreciation of the Mark on Economic Activity

## Core Idea
Inflation stimulated German output and employment for a time, through "forced saving" taken from wage earners and fixed-income classes. It also bent production toward capital goods and away from consumption, bred industrial "aggregates", and suspended the weeding-out of weak firms. From the second half of 1922 the output-limiting effects, latent before, took over. In 1923 inflation "disorganized all economic life" and ended in mass unemployment.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Classical static theory against the dynamic view**
  - Static (Ricardo, Mill, Wagner): new paper money adds nothing to real wealth. It only redirects existing resources and transfers property. Wagner: "Paper-money is capital from the point of the private trader, but it is not so for the national economy."
  - Wagner's two exceptions, both of which the author says applied to Germany: gold displaced by paper is exported for real goods; foreigners accept the notes in payment for goods.
  - Dynamic (Thornton, revived by the author): the transition between price levels takes time, wages lag, and in that interval real resources are freed.
  - When to use: to judge any claim that inflation "raises output". Ask whether the gain is a transfer, a transition effect, or a real addition.
- **Forced saving** (the author traces the idea to Thornton and to Mill's "compulsory saving")
  - Mechanism with unemployed labour: entrepreneurs get new money, hire more workers, prices rise, old workers and fixed-income consumers buy less.
  - Mechanism at full employment: lower real wages mean less productive effort is needed to reproduce the workers' subsistence fund. The freed effort can build new means of production.
  - Conditions: wages start above the minimum, and workers do not or cannot resist the fall in real wages.
  - Limit: the source "was entirely dried up" when wages came to be adjusted quickly to the depreciation. Voluntary saving fell at the same time.
- **Inflation as a tax with a second edge**: the State takes part of national income, and at the same time the process "imposes on some classes of society a levy in favour of other classes".
- **Exchange-and-employment seesaw** (to mid-1922): every improvement of the mark raised unemployment; every depreciation improved the labour market. Depreciation raised foreign demand (cheap German goods) and home demand (anticipated purchases, flight into goods). Appreciation produced a "buyers' strike".
  - Break of the rule: after summer 1922 "for the first time unemployment was increasing in spite of the rapid depreciation of the mark".
- **Shift toward instrumental goods** (two causes)
  - (a) Direct State action: canals, ports, power stations, railway orders, shipping compensation, compensation for plants lost in Lorraine. Part of the new note issue became demand for capital goods at once.
  - (b) Indirect effect of inflation: consumers' real incomes fell, inflation profits went to entrepreneurs, short-term bank credit could finance long-term investment because the debtor repaid only part of the real value.
  - Reinforced by the "myth of real value" or "substance": the belief that plant keeps intrinsic value even if unused.
- **Inflation and industrial concentration**: concentration had many causes (coal scarcity, Versailles losses, the syndicate's "self-consumption right", the turnover tax that exempted intra-group transfers, the Ruhr occupation, compulsory cartel laws). Inflation gave "a very strong impetus" on top. It favoured large firms that could adapt prices, use bank credit, issue emergency money and steer State policy.
  - **Aggregates against systems**: aggregates are groups of dissimilar firms formed from financial motives, to turn paper marks into material goods. Systems are organised combinations with an economic logic. Inflation's mark is chiefly on the aggregates.
- **Seven output-limiting effects of advanced inflation** (sections XIII to XVI)
  1. Hypertrophy of trade: more middlemen and clandestine commerce.
  2. Hypertrophy of banking: new banks, branches and staff to handle speculation.
  3. Pathological speculation.
  4. More unproductive labour inside firms: calculation, conversion, tax compliance, wage disputes.
  5. Lower intensity and quality of labour: underfed workers, anxiety about the dollar rate, time lost in wage bargaining.
  6. Suspension of the natural selection of firms: bankruptcies vanish and unsound firms survive.
  7. Difficulty of rational production: costs cannot be calculated, cheap labour removes the incentive to improve technique, and the entrepreneur becomes a speculator.

## Key Concepts
- **Instrumental (producers') goods**: means of production, as against goods for direct consumption.
- **Flight from the mark to the machine**: a German author's phrase for farmers buying machines as a hedge.
- **General liquidation**: Press phrase for shops emptied by foreign and domestic buyers in late 1921.
- **Vertical and horizontal concentration**: successive stages under one control, against firms at the same stage.
- **Interessengemeinschaft (community of interest)**: a contractual pooling short of merger.
- **Scaling of the banks**: industry taking control of banks, the reverse of the pre-war relation.
- **Stable-value loans**: loans indexed to rye, coal, potash, gold or foreign exchange, first issued in 1922.
- **Produced income against consumed income**: the gap the author explains by weak agriculture and by capital-goods bias.
- **Inflationskonjunktur**: the inflation boom (the term is used for Austria).

## Mental Models
- Use the unemployment-exchange correlation as a phase marker. Negative correlation means the stimulus phase. A switch to positive correlation means the disorganisation phase has begun.
- Read near-zero bankruptcies as a symptom of sickness, "a symptom of the malaise of the economic system", not of prosperity.
- Read low unemployment with output at 70 to 80 per cent of pre-war as lower productivity and disguised unemployment. The railways employed over 1,100,000 in 1920 against scarcely 700,000 before the war.
- Think of the relative price of producers' goods to consumers' goods as the gauge of forced investment. It widened from summer 1921, through 1922 and into 1923.
- Expect the banks to lose and the industrial groups to win. Deposits lose real value, the central bank becomes the only source of credit, and banks shrink to intermediaries.
- A stimulus that works through isolation also postpones adjustment. The author says depreciation probably spared Germany the 1920 world crisis.

## Anti-patterns
- **Inferring prosperity from full employment**: output was below pre-war, living standards were low, and labour was wasted.
- **Treating inflation-era investment as rationalisation**: much of it was "an extension of plant rather than an intensification of production". The author places real rationalisation after stabilisation.
- **Treating the conglomerates as superior organisation**: many were "only a means, in the hands of able speculators".
- **Blaming the 1923 crisis on the Ruhr occupation alone**: it aggravated the crisis, but it was neither the only nor the principal cause. At first it even stimulated industry in unoccupied territory.
- **Attributing all of the 1919-22 recovery to the depreciation**: much of it was post-war reconstruction.

## Worked Example
**The boom of October 1921 to summer 1922, then the turn.**
1. November 1921: flight from the mark becomes a panic. Orders flood in. Metallurgy works overtime and refuses orders. Silk quotes delivery six months out. Linen is sold out until spring 1922.
2. 26 November to 1 December 1921: the dollar falls from 293 to 190 marks. The Press calls the mark's improvement "a catastrophe for German industry". Orders drop.
3. February to March 1922: the dollar rises again, to 262 on 7 March. The Leipzig, Breslau and Frankfurt fairs see panic buying. The Berliner Tageblatt: "it is a madness".
4. Summer 1922: unemployment has practically disappeared. Total output is probably 70 to 80 per cent of 1913 in the same territory. Steel output equals 1913, but cotton is below 60 per cent.
5. Second half of 1922: popular consumption falls sharply. Germany turns from coal exporter to importer. Unemployment starts to rise despite the falling mark.
6. 1923: costs rise from overheads, low labour intensity and risk premiums. German prices reach and pass world prices. Foreign demand falls.
7. 1 to 2 November 1923, the retailer's problem: the official dollar rate fixed on 1 November is 130 milliard. Shops price on it next morning. That afternoon the rate is 320 milliard. The morning's takings have lost 60 per cent. Retailers raise base prices. Sales stop, working capital cannot be replaced, output stops.
8. The threat of collapse forces the monetary reform.

## Key Takeaways
1. The stimulus was real but came from redistribution. The author's rough scale: a pre-war wage bill of about 25 milliard marks, so a 10 to 20 per cent fall in real wages meant several milliards of profit. House rents of about 5 milliards (3 from workers) fell almost to zero.
2. Steel capacity rose from about 12 to more than 15 million tons, and shipyard capacity doubled. The author calls the latter "a mistaken estimate of the post-war economic situation". The seeds of the 1924-25 coal overproduction were sown here.
3. By 1924 six groups held about 50 per cent of the Rhenish-Westphalian coal syndicate (Stinnes 18.9 per cent). Four groups held 47 per cent of the raw steel syndicate.
4. Banks came out weaker than industry. Their resources at the end were "much less" than those of the big industrial groups.
5. Capital-market financing dried up in 1923. Only stable-value loans still worked.
6. Austria followed the same stages: stimulus, then output limits from late 1921, stabilisation at end 1922, a stabilisation crisis, and recovery from spring 1923.

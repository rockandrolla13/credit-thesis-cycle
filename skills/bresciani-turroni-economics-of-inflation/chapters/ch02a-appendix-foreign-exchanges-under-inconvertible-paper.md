# Appendix to Chapter II: The Foreign Exchanges under Conditions of Inconvertible Paper Money

## Core Idea
The exchange rate between two paper currencies is fixed by two things at once: relative price levels and the conditions of international demand. The author's "equation of foreign exchanges" multiplies the purchasing-power ratio by the index of the barter terms of trade. A fall in a currency's internal value passes to the exchange in full. A balance-of-payments shock passes only in part, because it sets off compensations. This asymmetry is the theoretical base of Chapter II's "limit" argument.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Purchasing Power Parity as a one-good identity** (eq. 1, recovered from PDF p. 108)
  - `p_x = p_y · C_x`, where `C_x` is the price of Y's monetary unit in X's currency.
  - The author's objection: countries mostly export different goods. If X exports cotton and Y exports iron, eq. 1 "loses all significance" for absolute prices. For price changes it needs proof.
- **Two-country, two-good exchange model** (eqs. 2 to 4, recovered from PDF pp. 109-110)
  - Egypt's demand for German iron: `N = A / P^x`, with `P = I·R`.
  - Germany's demand for Egyptian cotton: `M = B / Q^y`, with `Q = C / R`.
  - `I` = price of iron in marks. `C` = price of cotton in piastres. `R` = piastres per mark. `A`, `B`, `x`, `y` are constants.
  - Aggregate expenditure functions: Egypt `Z = N·I·R`. Germany `W = M·C/R`, which is `M·C` in Egyptian money.
  - Equilibrium condition: `C·M = I·N·R` (eq. 4). Export value equals import value in one currency.
  - Three unknowns (`R`, `N`, `M`), three equations. Constant costs are assumed, so `I` and `C` depend only on each country's monetary conditions.
  - With variable costs add two export supply functions and solve five equations for five unknowns (footnote, citing Ohlin and Yntema).
  - When to use: to separate a monetary shock from a real demand shock in an exchange-rate move.
- **The equation of foreign exchanges under independent paper standards** (eqs. 5 and 6, recovered from PDF p. 113)
  - New equilibrium: `C'·M' = N'·I'·R'` (eq. 5).
  - Put `B = M/N`, `B' = M'/N'`, `S = C'/C`, `U = I'/I`.
  - `R'/R = (U/S) · (B'/B)` (eq. 6).
  - In the author's words: "the index of exchange rates is equal to the ratio of purchasing powers (in terms of export prices) multiplied by the index of the barter terms of trade".
  - The extract and the page both state the definitions as printed above. The author's worked numbers are consistent with them only if the indices are read in the direction his examples use. See Worked Example and Extraction Notes.
- **Common-goods identity** (eq. 7, recovered from PDF p. 113): `E/G = R'/R`, where `E` and `G` are the index of the same goods' prices in Egyptian and in German money. It holds whatever happens to the terms of trade, for any number of goods, if the averages are unweighted or use the same weights in both countries.
- **Non-merchandise payments (tribute, interest, loans, tourism)** (eq. 8, recovered from PDF p. 114)
  - `(M' + T)·C = N'·I'·R' + D`. `T` = cotton exported to cover the payment `D`.
  - With prices unchanged, `R'/R` equals the ratio of the new net barter terms `M'/N'` to the old `M/N`.
  - Depreciation depends on: (a) the elasticities of the two demand schedules. (b) The absolute size of the payment. (c) The volume of trade before the payment began. Less trade means a larger fall.
  - A payment fixed in the creditor's currency depreciates the debtor's currency slightly more. If trade adjusts slowly, a foreign-currency obligation causes "serious difficulties".
  - The debtor can offset the exchange effect by deflation (lowering `C`). Then parity and exchange diverge too.
- **Transport costs** (eqs. 9 to 11, recovered from PDF p. 116)
  - `C(1+x)·M = I(1+y)·N·R` (eq. 9).
  - `R = (C/I) · (M/N) · (1+x)/(1+y)` (eq. 10; the author credits Pigou).
  - `R = (C / C_m) · (1+x)` (eq. 11), where `C_m` is the price of cotton in Germany in marks.
  - A rise in the cotton transport cost `x` leaves `R` unchanged if German demand for cotton has unit elasticity. It depreciates Egypt's money if elasticity is greater than one. It appreciates it if less.
- **Elastic-schedule theorem** (section IV): when a country trades many goods and most are competitive with other suppliers' goods, international demand is very elastic even if home demand for each good is not. Then the terms-of-trade factor moves little, and PPP is "approximately valid, when it refers to exportable commodities". The author says this is the case for "a great modern industrial country" such as Germany. The original rate is still not fully restored: a permanent demand shift leaves a permanent, small effect.

## Key Concepts
- **Aggregate expenditure curve**: total spending on the foreign good, in one currency, at each exchange rate. Equilibrium is where the two countries' curves cross.
- **Barter terms of trade**: units of export given per unit of import (`M/N`).
- **Demand in the "schedule sense"** (Fisher's phrase): a shift of the whole curve, as opposed to a movement along it.
- **Normal class of demand curves** (Marshall): curves that cross once. Multiple crossings give stable and unstable equilibria, but the author follows Marshall in treating only the normal class as important.
- **Internal value against external value**: purchasing power over domestic goods against the exchange rate.
- **Domestic commodities**: goods with only a local market. Their absolute prices have no necessary relation to the exchange.

## Mental Models
- Use the pair "monetary shock passes through in full, real shock is damped" as the default prior. Section IV closes on it: a fall in internal value "tends to be reflected to its full extent" on the exchanges; a balance-of-payments change "provokes compensations which substantially limit the effect".
- Think of PPP as a long-run attractor and a poor short-run description. The author's closing sentence says the doctrine is "quite inadequate" for short periods and gains significance as "the expression of fundamental forces acting for longer periods".
- Think of a persistent gap between exchange and domestic price parity as an unstable equilibrium that slowly corrects through import substitution, new exports and inward investment. The author notes this was happening in Egypt.
- With inflation, income distribution shifts, so real demand for foreign goods may shift too. The proportionality of exchange and prices is then approximate even for a purely monetary shock.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating the exchange as the index of monetary excess in all cases** (the Bullion Report criterion): for identical international goods the price-ratio identity always holds, yet the exchange can move with unchanged money when international demand shifts.
- **Substituting cost-of-living or general price indices into eq. 6**: export and domestic prices move in the same direction after a monetary shock but not by the same amount. The formula is then "at best only approximately valid". The author cites Keynes's Treatise.
- **Ignoring trade volume when judging a transfer burden**: halving trade in the author's example raises the exchange effect of the same payment from 6.333 to about 6.67.
- **Over-correcting Cassel**: critics who stress the terms-of-trade effect underrate how narrow it is when schedules are elastic.

## Worked Example
**Solving the base case and the inflation case by hand.**
1. Base: `N = 43,200,000 / (10·R)^2`. `M = 2,000,000 / (100/R)^2`.
2. Impose eq. 4: `100·M = 10·N·R`. Try `R = 6`. `N = 43,200,000 / 3,600 = 12,000`. `M = 2,000,000 / 277.8 = 7,200`. Check: `100 × 7,200 = 720,000` and `10 × 12,000 × 6 = 720,000`. Equilibrium.
3. Inflation in Egypt raises `C` to 105. Egypt was willing to give 7,385 cotton units for 12,623 iron units. That bundle now costs her 775,400 piastres instead of 738,500. So she buys the same iron at a rate 5 per cent less favourable. Her whole expenditure curve shifts.
4. Germany's demand in marks is unchanged. She still buys 6,844 cotton at 17.1 marks. At the new price she spends 718,600 piastres, and the rate that makes 105 piastres equal 17.1 marks is 6.14 instead of 5.85.
5. The curves now cross at `R = 6.30`. That is 5 per cent above 6, the same as the rise in the cotton price.
6. Condition attached by the author: this needs Egypt's real demand for iron to be unchanged after the inflation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Write the exchange move as (price-level ratio) times (terms-of-trade index). Estimate each factor separately.
2. In a large inflation the first factor swamps the second. The author says the variations of the terms-of-trade factor are then "insignificant" by comparison.
3. A transfer or a demand shift depreciates a paper currency permanently but by a bounded amount. The bound is tighter the more elastic and the larger the trade.
4. For Germany the author judges both schedules very elastic. So reparations alone could not explain the collapse.
5. Divergence between exchange and domestic-price parity can last "a considerable time". It is a sign that trade, production and the currency are not in stable equilibrium.

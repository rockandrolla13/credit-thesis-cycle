# Chapter VII: The Course of Prices of Industrial Shares during the Paper Inflation

## Core Idea
Shares were a poor inflation hedge over the whole period and a good one only in parts of it. In gold, the German share index fell from above 100 in mid-1918 to about 8.5 in February 1920, hovered near 15 to 20 in 1920-21, sank to 2.72 in October 1922, and rose to 39.4 in November 1923. The author's explanation: yield, not "intrinsic value", governs share prices even in an inflation. Dilution by new issues, tiny dividends, tight money, competing hedges and loss of confidence did the rest.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three deflators for share prices** (the Reich Statistical Bureau's method, 300 Berlin shares, 1913 = 100): divide the paper index by (1) the gold mark's paper price, (2) the wholesale price index, (3) the cost-of-living index.
  - When to use: (1) measures adaptation to external depreciation; (2) and (3) measure it against home purchasing power. In early inflation the home deflators matter more, since the home price structure still rests on paper.
  - How: adjust for rights issues (options). Without that, paper quotations understate the fall, since capital was continually "diluted". 1913 quotations averaged 178.66 per cent of nominal value.
- **Four regimes of the share market**

| Regime | Period | Behaviour | Author's explanation |
|------|------|------|------|
| Collapse | Oct 1918 to Feb 1920 | Gold index 69.3 to 8.5; paper prices rise far less than the dollar | Defeat, revolution, fear of Bolshevism, economic crisis |
| Dollar-linked | 1920 and 1921 (to 1 Dec 1921) | Paper index tracks the dollar closely; all shares move together | Shares seen as "Goldwert" and a store of value; speculative fury; foreign buying; firms park working capital in shares |
| Undervaluation | Feb to Oct 1922 | Shares lag the dollar and prices badly; gold index bottoms at 2.72 | Black Thursday losses; money scarcity; prices now absorb new notes at once; flight goes to foreign exchange and goods; discounting the end of the export boom; Rathenau murder and reparations crisis; dividends near zero; plural-vote shares block foreign control |
| Revaluation | Late Oct 1922 to Nov 1923 | Shares outrun the dollar; gold index 39.4 in Nov 1923 | Foreign exchange decree of 12 Oct 1922 closes the rival hedge; liberal Reichsbank credit; Ruhr subsidies; switch to gold accounting reveals low gold prices |

- **Share market as an inverted barometer**: in the dollar-linked regime bad political news raised share prices, because it lowered the mark. Prices "had ceased to be the barometer of the general political and economic situation".
- **Reversal of the exchange-share relation in 1922**: a rise in the dollar depressed shares, because each sudden fall of the mark "recalled great masses of paper money to the foreign exchange and goods market". Mechanism: once home prices and wages follow the exchange at once, firms need more working capital and have less to put into shares.
- **Yield rule** (the chapter's conclusion): the formula "intrinsic value rules in inflation, capitalised yield rules in stability" is "not entirely accurate". Yield guided valuation throughout.
  - Evidence 1: houses were material wealth yet had "silly prices", because rent control destroyed their yield.
  - Evidence 2: "stable value" bonds were mere claims yet rose with depreciation, because their interest was indexed.
  - Evidence 3: shares of firms with foreign earnings ("Valutapapiere" in the wide sense) tracked the dollar better, since they could raise dividends.
- **Substanzwert against Ertragswert in the gold balance sheets** (decree of 28 Dec 1923)
  - Intrinsic-value side (Deutsche Bank): value plant at cost less wear. Low 1924 earnings are transitory. A big capital cut would hinder future debenture issues, force high dividend percentages later and misstate how much capital Germany uses.
  - Earning-capacity side: unused plant has only break-up value. A cut lets firms pay normal dividends and brings shares back to par.
  - The author's verdict: yield "ought to be the fundamental criterion", but applied with regard to likely future productivity and to the abnormally high 1924 interest rate. To hold shares at par in 1924 a dividend of about 10 per cent was needed, against 5 per cent before the war.
- **Apparent profits (Scheingewinne)**: paper profits that exist only because depreciation allowances were set on historical cost. The author grants this for the early phase and for small firms. He holds that the better companies soon separated apparent from real profits, and that profits in 1920-22 were often real, though partly drawn from other classes' losses.

## Key Concepts
- **Dilution**: continual issues of new shares below market price, turning gold capital into paper capital.
- **Black Thursday**: 1 December 1921, when the dollar and shares fell together.
- **Plural-vote shares**: issued to insiders to keep control from foreign buyers.
- **Invisible mortgage**: the future reparations burden expected to fall on industry's profits.
- **Gold balance sheet**: the compulsory restatement of company accounts in gold marks in 1924.
- **Secret reserves**: undervalued assets, common in the conservative 1924 balance sheets.
- **Proprietors' capital**: share capital plus published reserves, as against loan capital.
- **Valutapapiere**: strictly, securities issued in foreign currency; loosely, shares of firms with foreign interests.

## Mental Models
- Think of each inflation hedge as competing for the same flight money. Shares win when access to foreign exchange is restricted and lose when it is open.
- Use the liquidity test before the hedge test. In 1921 inflation came with abundant money and shares rose. In 1922 it came with scarce money and they fell.
- Read dividend policy as the anchor. Companies retained profits to build plant. Thirteen iron and steel firms earned 383.6 million marks net in 1920-21 and paid out 160.1 million. In 1922, 120 companies paid dividends averaging 0.25 per cent of share price.
- Use absurd cross-valuations as a signal of regime. In 1922 the whole of Daimler was valued at 327 of its own cars, and the sixteen Tietz department stores at 16,000 suits.
- Debt erasure is the shareholder's hidden gain. Gelsenkirchener's plant value fell from 268 to 140 million marks, but its mortgages and loans fell from 74 to 13 million, so capital fell only from 180 to 130.
- Expect banks and insurers to be the losers among listed firms, since law or business model ties their assets to money claims.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming equities preserve real value through an inflation**: the gold index never regained its 1913 level, apart from a brief distortion on 3 November 1923 caused by the gap between official and foreign dollar rates.
- **Judging industry's health by reported profits**: balance sheets were built backwards from the profit management wished to show.
- **Comparing 1913 and 1924 balance sheets naively**: firms had absorbed others, nominal capital differed from market value, and a fall in value is not a material loss.
- **Valuing plant at inflation-era cost converted at the dollar rate**: this undervalues it, because the mark's home purchasing power was long above its dollar value.
- **Treating a fall in asset values as proof of destroyed plant**: the author judges that plant was on the whole more extensive in 1924 than in 1913.

## Worked Example
**Why did shares fall in gold in 1922 while the mark collapsed?**
1. Start of 1922: gold index 16.27. The dollar-link of 1920-21 is still the public's model.
2. 1 December 1921 had shown that shares can fall with the dollar. Savers and firms shift to foreign exchange and goods.
3. Government finances improve in the first half of 1922. The circulation rises only 65 per cent from January to July. Bank deposits shrink in real terms and banks cut credit. The forced loan and new taxes drain liquidity.
4. Home prices now follow the dollar at once (January to December: dollar 100 to 3,956, wholesale 100 to 4,024). Each fall of the mark raises firms' need for working capital, leaving nothing for the Bourse.
5. Dividends are trivial. One firm's dividend equals four bottles of mineral water against 2,800 before the war.
6. Foreigners cannot buy control, and no longer trust the German outlook. A Dutch buyer could get nine Harpener shares for the 1,000 florins that bought one in 1913, and still stayed away.
7. Result: paper index 743 in January, 2,062 in October. Gold index 16.27 to 2.72. Shares of undiluted firms had risen 89 times since 1914 while the dollar had risen 1,525 times and wholesale prices 945 times.
8. Turn: the foreign exchange decree of 12 October 1922 restricts purchases of foreign exchange. Flight money returns to shares. Note issues rise from 100 to 267 in the fourth quarter. The share index doubles and redoubles.

## Key Takeaways
1. A buyer at the start of 1920 kept real value and made a small gain through 1921. A holder since 1913 lost four-fifths in gold by 1921 and more than nine-tenths by late 1922.
2. Mering's study of 1,458 companies put 1921 net profits at 28.81 per cent of 1913-14, or 3.04 per cent of capital against 10.55 per cent.
3. The Bourse may have begun to discount defeat in May 1918, when the gold index peaked at 112.75. The author says it was clearly doing so by early August.
4. In 1923 shares became the hedge of last resort and were bid up above any yield basis: "the over-valuation of shares, relatively to their yields, was colossal". Mining and metallurgical shares reached a gold index of 65.7 in November 1923 and fell to 39.5 in December.
5. The big industrial companies came through with capital preserved or enlarged. Banks, mortgage banks, insurers and shipping lines lost most of theirs.
6. Working capital was the typical loss. The gold balance sheets show the disproportion between fixed and floating capital that Chapter X makes a main cause of the stabilisation crisis.
7. Concentration of capital rose. In 1925 companies with capital above 5 million marks were 4.5 per cent of the number and held more than 65 per cent of total capital.

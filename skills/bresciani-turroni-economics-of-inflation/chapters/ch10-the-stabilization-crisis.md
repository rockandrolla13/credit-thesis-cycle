# Chapter X: The Stabilization Crisis

## Core Idea
Stabilisation exposed what inflation had hidden: a severe shortage of working capital, shown in very high interest rates. The author's explanation is real, not monetary. Inflation had "immobilised" capital in plant and in higher-order goods (coal, iron) and starved the flow of consumption goods. When forced saving stopped and wages, rents, taxes and interest became effective again, demand swung from instrumental goods to consumption goods. The capital-goods industries and the inflation conglomerates went into crisis, while consumption industries did well. Foreign loans rebuilt the subsistence fund, and "rationalisation" followed.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Stabilisation as a dynamic process**: the elements of the system moved at different speeds in the inflation. Stabilisation means bringing them back into balance, not freezing them where they stood (the author cites Del Vecchio).
- **Interest-rate law of stabilisation**: across European countries in autumn 1925, the more extreme the past depreciation, the higher the interest rate. The shortage of working capital "is the essence of the stabilization crisis".
- **Conversion of circulating capital into fixed capital ("immobilisation")**
  - Definition: a shift of the ratio between fixed and circulating capital away from the most advantageous one, toward too much fixed capital.
  - Classical roots: Mill (a railway consumes food, clothing and tools and leaves a railway), Cairnes (sudden large conversions can be "disastrous"), Wilson on the English crisis of 1847.
  - The author's restatement, after Cannan's objection: the composition of the stock of capital goods changes. Lower-order intermediate goods, near to consumption, shrink. Higher-order goods such as iron, and the plant to make them, expand.
  - Financing: forced saving. Entrepreneurs' profits rise faster than other classes' voluntary saving.
  - The same thing happens "in the expansion phases of the Trade Cycle".
  - When to use: to diagnose a post-boom or post-inflation slump where plant is abundant and credit is dear.
- **Three explanations of the capital shortage, with the author's verdict**

| Explanation | Claim | Verdict |
|------|------|------|
| Loss of money capital | Working capital sat in money and depreciated; prices lagged | Partly true for small firms and retailers; big firms protected themselves |
| Low real value of the circulation | Money stock was only 100 to 200 million gold marks | "An obvious sophism" (see Ch IV) |
| Crisis of markets (Hahn) | No shortage of real capital: coal and iron were piled up unsold; foreign loans went to food, so capital goods were not needed | Mistakes which goods were scarce |
| Immobilisation (the author) | Too much fixed capital and higher-order goods; too little flow of consumption goods | Correct. Unsold coal and food imports are both symptoms of it |

- **Timing of the capital shortage**
  - Not felt in 1914-21: money markets were liquid, issues easy, no need for foreign loans.
  - First symptoms from the second half of 1922: workers resisted further cuts in real wages. Inflation profits shrank. Bank deposits were gone.
  - Masked in 1922-23 by Reichsbank credit. Each rise in money wages was met by credit, then by prices: a wage-credit-price spiral, faster once wages were indexed.
  - Acute after November 1923, when voluntary saving became "the sole source of capital".
- **Four charges that became effective at stabilisation**
  1. Wages: nominal rises granted in the last months stayed while prices stopped. Rents rose to 70 per cent of pre-war by end 1924, which pushed wages up.
  2. Interest: still priced for depreciation, now a real burden.
  3. Taxes: high rates that inflation had nullified now bit. The total tax burden for 1925-26 was about 11 milliard gold marks.
  4. Social charges: 1,692 million marks in 1924 and about 2,220 million in 1925.
- **Demand reversal**: demand for instrumental goods fell (firms and public bodies stopped building plant). Demand for consumption goods rose (workers, salaried staff, small capitalists and house owners had more purchasing power). Result: "simultaneously an over-production of instrumental goods and an under-production of goods for direct consumption".
- **Two kinds of instrumental-goods industry**: those tied to current consumer demand (power plant, fertiliser, cement for housing, tram material) revived with consumption. Those making long-range capital works (new industrial plant, shipyards, large-scale re-equipment) were stopped.
- **Decentralisation and horizontal concentration**: inflation-era vertical groups, formed for scarce raw materials and for finance, loosened or broke up. Firms making the same goods merged to cut costs. Both moves served one aim: rational organisation. Cartels revived among the survivors.
- **Entrepreneurs' five responses to the shortage**
  1. Lower wages and longer hours (decree of 17 November 1923 in effect suspended the eight-hour day).
  2. Cut or pass dividends; bring home foreign exchange.
  3. Borrow from the Reichsbank again, until the credit stop of April 1924.
  4. Turn back to the commercial banks, which regained influence.
  5. Borrow abroad, after the Dawes Plan.
- **Stabilisation first, reconstruction after**: those who held that monetary reform must wait for economic recovery were wrong. Stabilisation "made it possible for the restorative forces" to work.

## Key Concepts
- **Working capital (Betriebsmittel)**: liquid means to buy materials and pay wages. Fürstenberg's book title, "a country without working capital", names the condition.
- **Subsistence fund**: the flow of consumption goods that supports workers during production.
- **Higher-order and lower-order goods**: far from and near to final consumption.
- **Monatsgeld**: one-month money. It reached a level equal to 72 per cent a year in Berlin from 28 April to 10 May 1924.
- **Valorization clause**: loan repayable in stable value. Rate 10 per cent under the decree of 10 October 1923.
- **Rationalisation**: the 1925-26 slogan for reorganising production to raise productivity.
- **Vereinigte Stahlwerke**: the steel trust formed to put the vertical groups under one management (nominal capital 800 million marks).
- **Rohstahlgemeinschaft**: the new raw steel syndicate of 1924, with quotas totalling 14.7 million tons.
- **Demolition**: the industrialists' own word in December 1925 for scrapping surplus plant.

## Mental Models
- Think of inflation as a disease that overgrows some muscles and wastes others. The cure is painful because the overgrown parts must shrink.
- Read unsold stocks of basic materials together with a rush of consumer imports as one signal: the capital structure is too "long" for the flow of consumption goods.
- Use the composition of foreign borrowing as evidence. If loans are spent on food and finished goods, the scarce item is the subsistence fund.
- Use the split between capital-goods and consumer-goods industries (share prices, protested bills, dividends, employment) to confirm a stabilisation crisis as against a general slump.
- Expect the speculator-built groups to fail and the old, conservative firms to survive. "No new aristocracy of captains of industry arose."
- Expect the profile of consumption to shift from luxury to mass goods as income is redistributed back.
- Expect the gap between domestic and foreign-currency loan rates to favour firms with access abroad.
- Debt relief from inflation is temporary. Agriculture shed 16 milliard marks of mortgages and by mid-1925 carried an interest burden equal to a pre-war debt of 8.4 to 9.2 milliards.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating plant as wealth regardless of use**: "the myth of the intrinsic value and of the real value was finally exploded".
- **Regarding the inflation crisis as a purge**: it "was not... a purging of the economic organism". Reconstruction still had to be done, and under worse conditions.
- **Fixing the official discount rate below the market rate**: the Reichsbank had to ration, and no open discount market could form.
- **Meeting a capital shortage with central bank credit**: this is "financing their firms at the expense of other classes of society" and nearly wrecked the reform.
- **Cartel pricing at the cost of the weakest member**: the Frankfurter Zeitung's 1925 demand was that the weakest "must not be maintained, but eliminated".
- **Vertical integration as an end in itself**: tied purchasing raised costs once goods were plentiful, and large groups became bureaucratic.

## Worked Example
**Reading the trade figures of 1924-25 as a test of the theory.**
1. Hypothesis: the shortage is in the flow of consumption goods, not in plant.
2. Prediction 1: foreign credit will be spent on food and finished goods. Result: food imports rise from 20.0 per cent of imports in 1923 to 36.1 per cent in the second half of 1924. In the second half of 1925 food and finished goods together are 54.4 per cent of imports against 33.4 per cent in 1923.
3. Prediction 2: Germany will import finished animal products instead of the feed to produce them. Result, first half of 1925, net imports: forage 194 million marks (401 in 1913); cattle and meat 158 (108); butter, milk, eggs and cheese 386 (201).
4. Prediction 3: exporters of consumer goods will turn to the home market. Result: finished-goods exports are flat, 5,200 million marks in the crisis year 1923 and 5,191 million in the recovery year 1924.
5. Prediction 4: makers of capital goods and basic materials will push exports. Result: the coal balance flips from deficit in 1922 to surplus in 1925; iron goods and machines rise less, because iron is in world surplus and France is dumping on a falling franc.
6. Scale: the trade deficit from 1 July 1924 to 31 December 1925 is about 4.8 milliard marks, the author's rough measure of foreign credit.
7. Conclusion: Hahn's observation (loans used "unproductively" for food) supports the immobilisation theory.

## Key Takeaways
1. The stabilisation crisis is a capital-structure crisis. Its marks are dear money, idle heavy industry and busy consumer trades.
2. Wages and rents catching up are part of the adjustment, since they restore consumption demand, but they also remove the profit cushion firms had lived on.
3. The central bank must refuse to replace forced saving with credit. The author states the rule adopted from April 1924: stability of the currency "had precedence over any other matter whatever".
4. Much inflation-era investment was written off or physically demolished. The author uses this against the "inflationists" who said depreciation had strengthened the country's equipment.
5. Stinnes, Sichel, Stumm, Kahn and others broke up or failed. Krupp, Thyssen, Harpen and Gelsenkirchener survived. Chemicals and electricity gained on coal and steel.
6. Production in 1925 was held by "eminent German economists" to have passed both 1923 and 1922.
7. Bank deposits of the six chief Berlin banks: 4,373 million marks at end 1913, 905 million on 1 January 1924, 4,936 million at end 1926 (Table XL, not checked).
8. Austria showed the same sequence after its 1922 stabilisation, with banks regaining supremacy even more clearly.

# Chapter I: Foreign Exchanges and Internal Price Movements in Germany, 1914 to 1923

## Core Idea
The chapter is the statistical spine of the book. It splits the mark's history into seven periods and shows, period by period, which of five series led and which lagged: the dollar rate, prices of imported goods, internal prices, cost of living, and the note circulation with the floating debt. The order of the curves changes from period to period, and that changing order is the evidence the later chapters argue from.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The lead-lag ordering of the five curves (the "fan")**: in every period rank the series by how fast they rose from a common base. A fan-shaped spread means the economy is in disequilibrium. A single tight bundle means all prices have learned to follow the exchange.
  - When to use: to locate an inflation in its life cycle from published indices alone.
  - How: (1) rebase all series to 100 at the start of the period. (2) Rank them. (3) Compare with the seven German orderings below. (4) Read the position of the circulation: above prices means money is being absorbed, below prices means the public is fleeing it.
- **Seven periods of the mark** (author's own division):

| # | Period | What led | What lagged | Stated end-of-period readings |
|---|--------|----------|-------------|-------------------------------|
| 1 | Aug 1914 to Armistice | Quantity of money (440 in Oct 1918, 1913 = 100) | Gold mark price (1.57 paper marks in Oct 1918); wholesale prices 2.34 | Floating debt from 300 million marks (Jul 1914) to 55.2 milliards (Dec 1918) |
| 2 | Nov 1918 to Jul 1919 | Dollar rate, then imported-goods prices | Internal prices; circulation and floating debt in between | Circulation and debt rose faster than internal prices, slower than the dollar |
| 3 | Jul 1919 to Feb 1920 | Dollar rate and imported-goods prices, clearly detached | Internal prices, then circulation and floating debt | Dollar 23.6 times parity; imported goods 40.63; home goods 12.10; all wholesale 16.85; cost of living 8.47 (1913 = 1) |
| 4 | Feb 1920 to May 1921 | Floating debt (89 to 176.6 milliards), then circulation (54,456 to 81,735 millions) | Dollar rate (still below Feb 1920) and imported prices | May 1921: dollar 14.83, wholesale 13.08 (imported 15.23, home 12.66), food 13.20. Near-equilibrium of internal and external value |
| 5 | May 1921 to Jul 1922 | Dollar rate and imported prices | Cost of living, then circulation, then floating debt | From Feb 1922 internal wholesale prices adapt to the dollar much faster. From Mar 1922 circulation outruns floating debt |
| 6 | Jul 1922 to Jun 1923 | Dollar rate tends to anticipate | Circulation and floating debt | When the dollar paused (Mar to Apr 1923) internal prices and cost of living overtook it. Over the year circulation rose less than prices |
| 7 | Jun 1923 to Rentenmark | All series move as one group | None | On 20 Nov 1923 internal wholesale index 1,374 milliards against dollar index 1,000 milliards; cost of living later 1,535 milliards |

- **Unequal depreciation across categories**: more paper money lowers the purchasing power of money "not uniformly, but in varying degrees" across goods, services and productive resources.
  - When to use: to predict who gains and loses in an inflation.
  - How: line up categories by how fast their paper price adjusts. Slow adjusters (salaries, wages, land) are the losers. Fast adjusters (raw materials, gold-linked securities, precious metals) are the hedges.

## Key Concepts
- **Gold mark**: the pre-war mark at gold parity. Its price in paper marks is the author's measure of external depreciation.
- **Floating debt**: Treasury bills discounted at the Reichsbank or placed outside it. It is the fiscal source of the note issue.
- **Quantity of money in circulation**: Reichsbank notes, private bank-of-issue notes, Reich notes (Reichskassenscheine), Loan Office notes (created 4 Aug 1914) and metallic money.
- **Prices of imported goods vs. home-produced goods**: the two official wholesale sub-indices. The gap between them is the first measure of internal vs. external value.
- **Trillion**: the book uses the long scale. A billion = 1,000,000 squared; a trillion = 1,000,000 cubed.
- **Official vs. open-market dollar rate**: the Berlin official rate understated depreciation in November 1923.
- **Logarithmic scale**: needed for the last period. The author notes the German inflation made such charts common.

## Mental Models
- Use the position of the circulation curve relative to prices as a regime marker. During the war money rose faster than prices and the exchange. After mid-1919 it rose slower. That switch is the subject of Chapter IV.
- Think of the last phase as a closed loop. The author states it: a rise in the dollar hit prices and wages at once, which raised the floating debt and the note issue, and the new paper in turn depressed the exchange further.
- Think of an appreciation episode (Feb 1920 to May 1921) as proof that the dollar rate is not a one-way function of the note issue. The debt nearly doubled while the dollar fell.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading the official November 1923 dollar quotations as the market**: the Cologne open market showed several times the Berlin rate.
- **Charting hyperinflation on an arithmetic scale**: the earlier periods vanish.
- **Treating "prices" as one series**: imported goods, home goods, cost of living, wages and shares moved very differently when converted to gold.

## Worked Example
**Reading the end of the inflation, November 1923.**
1. Official Berlin dollar quotations: 840 milliard paper marks on 13 Nov, 1,260 on 14 Nov, 2,520 on 15 Nov, 4,200 on 20 Nov.
2. Open-market quotations at Cologne (the author cites Schacht): 3,900 milliard on 13 Nov, 5,800 on 15 Nov, 6,700 on 17 Nov, 11,700 on 20 Nov, the highest quotation.
3. The Reichsbank stopped discounting Treasury bills on 15 Nov 1923. Reichsbank notes then stood at 92.8 trillion paper marks.
4. The dollar was fixed at 4,200 milliard on 20 Nov. The law of 30 Aug 1924 set one Reichsmark equal to a thousand milliard paper marks.
5. Retail prices in Berlin at the end of November: a kg of bread 428 milliard, a kg of butter 5,600 milliard, a newspaper 200 milliard, a tram ticket 150 milliard, an inland letter 100 milliard.
6. Comparison points given: continental money in 1781 was worth one thousandth of its original value. On 1 Jun 1796 a metal franc was worth 533 assignat francs. Early October 1923: a gold mark was 709 million paper marks and a gold rouble 505 million old paper roubles.

## Key Takeaways
1. The relation between money, exchange and prices is not constant. It passed through at least four distinct orderings before collapsing into one bundle.
2. In wartime, money ran ahead of prices and the exchange. From 1919 the exchange ran ahead of everything.
3. February 1920 to May 1921 is the counter-example period: floating debt nearly doubled, the dollar fell, internal prices were flat.
4. From February 1922 internal prices started adapting quickly to the dollar. By mid-1923 they adapted at once, and briefly overshot.
5. In March 1922 the circulation began to outrun the floating debt. The author attributes this "probably" to Reichsbank commercial loans.
6. Converted to gold, real wages and share prices were depressed for the whole inflation. Diagram IX shows this, but only as an image.

# Chapter VI: The Depreciation of the Mark and Germany's Foreign Trade

## Core Idea
The effect of inflation on the trade balance depends on the phase. When home gold prices are above world prices, inflation promotes imports. When the exchange falls ahead of home prices, it promotes exports ("exchange dumping"). When home prices overshoot, exporters must dump in the classical sense. German exports never came near 1913 volumes, so the "invasion" of foreign markets was a myth. The real damage abroad came from low prices and instability, and the terms of trade turned against Germany.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two theories of paper money and the trade balance**
  - (a) Goschen: paper issues raise home prices, attract imports, hinder exports. The balance turns passive.
  - (b) Fanno and others: issues hit the exchange before home prices. Home gold prices fall below world prices. Exports expand, imports contract. The balance turns active.
  - Mill's static view, rejected as incomplete: trade depends on metallic prices, so depreciation "does not affect the foreign trade of the country".
- **Three-phase reconciliation** (the author's own)

| Phase | Period | Price relation | Which theory applies | Trade effect |
|------|------|------|------|------|
| 1 | From the outbreak of war | Home paper prices up 50 per cent in a year; gold prices above those abroad | (a) | Imports stimulated |
| 2 | From mid-1918 (Dalberg's dating) to mid-1922 | Exchange falls ahead of home prices | (b), with modifications | "Exchange dumping" (Valutadumping); exports stimulated |
| 3 | From the second half of 1922, acute from August 1923 | Home prices reach and pass world prices | (a) again | Imports favoured, held back only by restrictions; exporters sell abroad below home prices: true "dumping" |

  - When to use: before predicting the trade effect of a depreciation, find the sign of the divergence between internal and external value (Chapter III's D).
- **Volume against value**: keep the effect of the exchange on physical volumes apart from its effect on values. German value statistics were badly distorted.
- **Why imports rose when the mark fell** (four reasons)
  1. Urgent need for food and raw materials after the war.
  2. Foreigners accepted newly issued marks, so inflation itself paid for imports. The deficit was "a consequence of the inflation", not of high home prices.
  3. An industrial exporter must import raw materials first. So the first effect of a depreciation was more orders placed abroad. The textbook case (Wagner's Russia, exporting crops and importing manufactures) does not fit Germany.
  4. Foreign goods were a store of value. The Vissering, Dubois and Kamenka report of 8 November 1922 noted abnormal imports from distrust of the mark.
- **Composition rule**: the export stimulus is weaker the larger the share of raw materials in cost, and stronger the larger the share of wages. German wages stayed far below those abroad (about 10 cents an hour against 50 to 75 in the United States, per the US Tariff Commission in 1922). So exports shifted to finished goods and imports to raw materials.
- **Three cases linking exports and production**
  - (a) Sudden, brief divergence: only existing stocks are sold. Exports rise at the expense of home consumption.
  - (b) Lasting divergence with full use of plant and labour: production shifts toward the favoured goods. Total output is unchanged.
  - (c) Lasting divergence with idle plant and unemployment: total output rises. The author says this "doubtless" applied to Germany for a time.
- **Export price and elasticity** (Diagram XIV, described in the text): depreciation ahead of home costs lowers the gold supply curve of exports. The gold export price falls, by less than costs, and the gap is the exporter's profit. The total gold value of exports rises if foreign demand has elasticity above one and falls if below one.
- **Terms-of-trade loss against the seigniorage gain from foreigners**: Germany sold goods too cheaply and sold houses and shares "at ridiculous prices". Against that, she obtained goods for marks that later became worthless. The author calls a net balance "a very difficult task" and does not strike one.

## Key Concepts
- **Exchange dumping (Valutadumping)**: exporting at gold prices below world prices but still above home prices, because the exchange has fallen further than home costs.
- **Classical dumping**: selling abroad below the home price.
- **Aussenhandelstellen**: Foreign Trade Control Offices that issued export licences and fixed minimum export prices. The author reports the charge that industrial groups used them against outsiders.
- **General liquidation**: the sell-off of every kind of German good to foreigners in late 1919.
- **Buyers' strike**: the fall in home demand when the mark rose in 1920.
- **Invisible exports**: transit traffic, port and shipping services, ship repairs, spending by foreign visitors.
- **Sliding scale**: price = base price times a "key" built from wages, raw-material prices and the exchange. Adopted generally at the start of 1922.
- **Export of national property**: foreign purchases of shares, houses and land.

## Mental Models
- Use the correlation between export volume and the divergence coefficient as a phase test. It held until mid-1922 and then broke.
- Think of a mark rally as a shock to industry. The author notes the "paradoxical situation" in which improvement was feared "as a catastrophe" and expansion seemed to need continual depreciation.
- Use the foreign share of visitors as a live gauge of how cheap a country is in gold. Hamburg's share peaked in 1922 and fell as German gold prices rose.
- Distrust trade values recorded in a collapsing currency. Lags between order, shipment and registration convert to very different gold sums.
- Think of foreign holders of the currency as lenders who will never be repaid. Their losses are the inflating country's gain, set against its terms-of-trade loss.

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing in the "invasion" of German goods**: partner-country import statistics show quantities "often far less" than in 1913 and a smaller German share.
- **Reading the fall in the gold value of exports during depreciation as proof of inelastic foreign demand**: it probably reflects the statistical errors.
- **Using official 1920-22 export values**: they imply prices at two-thirds of 1913. The Statistical Bureau itself replaced them with volumes at 1913 prices.
- **Fixed prices in paper marks for export contracts**: in May to December 1921, 75 per cent of export value was invoiced in paper marks, and the autumn collapse caused heavy losses.
- **Applying the agrarian-country model to an industrial exporter**: a fall in the exchange does not at once cut imports.

## Worked Example
**How the trade statistics understated export values.**
1. Registration lag. Exports of 1,000 million paper marks made in October 1921 are registered in November. At October's rate they are about 28 million gold marks. At November's rate they are scarcely 16 million.
2. Order lag. A good is ordered in September 1921 for 10,000 paper marks, then worth 400 gold marks. It crosses the frontier in November, when 10,000 paper marks are worth 160 gold marks. If the exporter in fact sold in foreign money, took payment in advance, or obtained a surcharge, the statistic is wrong. He had a motive to hide the surcharge: it avoided export tax and the surrender of foreign bills.
3. Evidence. At end 1922 more than half of declarations were still in paper marks, though about 90 per cent of sales were in foreign money. A survey of 44 firms that declared in paper marks found only 4 had really sold in paper marks. Conversion at the declaration-date rate gave gold prices of scarcely 60 per cent of what the firms received.
4. Correction. From February 1923 the Bureau removed the registration error; export values rose 20 per cent. For 1923 it used only foreign-currency declarations and found export prices 15 per cent above 1913.
5. Consequence. Official 1922 exports of 4 milliard gold marks against more than 6 milliard at 1913 prices cannot be taken as a one-third price cut.

## Key Takeaways
1. Exports in quantity: 3.2 million quintals in January 1919, 12.0 in August, 27.6 in December, as the mark collapsed. From 11.4 in May 1921 to 19.7 in October 1921.
2. Trade swung in cycles of a few months, driven by the exchange. Before the war a cycle took seven to nine years.
3. For the rest of 1923, after the Ruhr shock, export volume was "on the whole, unaffected" by the huge depreciation. The export premium was gone.
4. After stabilisation home prices stayed too high. At the spring 1924 Leipzig Fair many industrial prices were still above world levels.
5. German export prices averaged 76.4 per cent of world prices in 1922, with a spread from about 50 to 105 per cent. In 1913 the spread was 90 to 115. The author reads the wider spread as proof of a dislocated price system.
6. Gold prices of imports rose 26 per cent from 1913 to 1923 and export prices only 14 per cent (Statistical Bureau), a terms-of-trade loss.
7. Foreign holders of marks lost heavily. Singer: about 60 milliard paper marks held abroad in mid-1922, a loss of 14 milliard gold marks if bought at 25 gold pfennig. The McKenna Report: Germany gained between 7.6 and 8.7 milliard gold marks from sales of marks since the Armistice. About a tenth of German share capital was foreign-owned in 1923, and about 25,000 Berlin houses were bought for foreigners.

# Chapter XI: Conclusion

## Core Idea
The German experience confirms the core of the quantity theory: "the limitation of the quantity of money is necessary for the stability of the price structure." The exchange rate, velocity and business-created money all mattered, but only continued issues of legal money made the fall of the mark continuous. Stabilisation is the starting point of reconstruction, not its result. Inflation did create some new capital through forced saving, but the net effect on the economy and on society was heavily negative. The chapter is short and has no tables.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The quantity-limitation principle** (the author quotes Ricardo: nothing is more important in issuing paper money "than to be fully impressed with the effects which follow from the principle of limitation of quantity")
  - When to use: as the first test of any stabilisation plan or any claim that a depreciation is beyond the central bank's control.
  - How: ask whether the issue of legal money for the State and for business is capped by an authority that will hold the cap under pressure.
- **Walras's missing equation** (the author's formal argument)
  - With commodity money, `m` goods and `n` traders: unknowns are `m − 1` prices and `n·m` quantities. The number of equations is the same. The system is determinate.
  - With paper money as the standard: there are `m` prices to determine. One equation is missing. "It follows that prices are unstable."
  - A central authority that fixes the quantity of money supplies the missing condition.
  - Consequence: limitation of quantity "is one of the conditions of economic equilibrium", so monetary reform must precede economic stabilisation.
- **Reply to the "prices are active" critics** (Nogaro, De Bordes, Mitchell)
  - Their claim: the exchange raises prices from outside the equation of exchange; the business system then creates the money it needs or uses money more fully.
  - The author's reply: such devices "only had a limited effect". Proof by experiment: when Government issues stopped in November 1923, the exchange and prices stabilised and "no external influence" depreciated the currency. The rentenmark held with no gold cover, through quantity control alone.
  - Even in the last weeks, when firms priced in gold and paid with foreign balances and emergency money, they still needed legal money to pay wages. If the Reichsbank had refused, the fall would have stopped.
- **Scarcity test**: each time the Reichsbank made means of payment relatively scarce, firms had to sell foreign exchange and the mark rose. Cases given: February 1923 (support action), August 1923 (paper marks commanded a premium over the official rate), April 1924 and May 1929 (credit contractions).
- **Reply on velocity**: velocity depends on the share of income and capital people hold as money. It normally changes slowly. In a panic it jumps, but "if Governments and banks of issue hold fast and refuse to increase the issues", the crisis of confidence passes and velocity falls back. Cases given: after November 1923, and after the banking crisis of July 1931.
- **Confidence rule**: declarations and energetic measures can stop a depreciation "at once", but confidence lasts only if the public knows issues are strictly limited. Early in an inflation confidence survives rising issues, because the public blames other causes. Later "each new issue weakened confidence more and more".
- **Dynamic verdict on inflation and production**: the classical claim that inflation only redistributes existing resources "no longer seems tenable". Forced saving does divert productive effort to new capital. But the resulting disequilibrium is so deep that "the national economy gains little". Much new fixed capital proved useless. Quantity of equipment rose, quality did not.

## Key Concepts
- **Principle of limitation of quantity**: Ricardo's phrase, adopted as the chapter's thesis.
- **Legal money against business-created money**: notes of the State and central bank against emergency money and foreign balances used in payment.
- **Metallic cover against quantity**: the author holds that "the value of the mark does not depend on the metallic cover but on the quantity of marks in circulation".
- **Dollar exchange standard with controls**: after 1931 stability was kept by control of foreign payments, which worked only because note issues were not increased.
- **Usurpers of national property**: the author's term for the class enriched by the inflation.

## Mental Models
- Treat a central bank's plea that it must supply the money trade demands as the root error. The author traces the astronomical price level to "the erroneous idea that the issues of notes made in response to the demands of commerce are legitimate".
- Use wage payment as the choke point. Substitute monies can carry wholesale trade, but wages need legal tender. Control of that issue is control of the process.
- Think of exchange controls as effective only alongside monetary restraint. The same controls failed during the inflation.
- Distinguish firms that used the inflation to adjust and concentrate (large chemical, electrical and potash concerns, which "probably profited in a lasting manner") from the economy as a whole.
- Read official fear of a new depreciation as revealed preference. If depreciation had been good for Germany, the author asks, why did officials from 1931 make "desperate efforts" to hold the mark?

## Anti-patterns
- **Waiting for economic recovery before monetary reform**: the order is the reverse.
- **Relying on gold cover or guarantees instead of quantity control**: the rentenmark had no gold cover.
- **Believing velocity makes the price level uncontrollable**: it reverts when issues stop.
- **Expanding credit while pledging exchange stability**: the author notes that after 1933, with public works and rearmament, "it has become increasingly difficult for the Reichsbank to maintain a stable value of the mark while it favours an expansion of credit".
- **Concluding from the survival of big firms that inflation was beneficial**: "these great businesses are not the whole of the German economy".

## Worked Example
**The author's balance sheet of the inflation, item by item.**
1. Timing: it "retarded the crisis for some time, but this broke out later, throwing millions out of employment".
2. Production: it stimulated output at first, through the gap between the mark's internal and external value. Later it disorganised and limited production.
3. Saving: "it annihilated thrift".
4. Public finance: it made budget reform impossible for years.
5. Foreign relations: it obstructed the solution of the reparations question.
6. Society: a few accumulated wealth, "whilst millions of individuals were thrown into poverty".
7. Morals and politics: it spread the spirit of speculation through all classes and caused "incessant political and moral disturbance".
8. Capital: part of the forced saving built useful plant. A large part of the new fixed capital in iron, steel and engineering "was, later, revealed as useless".
9. Net judgement: negative. The author says the years 1919-23 weigh "like a nightmare on the German people".

## Key Takeaways
1. Fiscal and banking policy was the fundamental cause of the fall of both the internal and the external value of the mark.
2. The decisive evidence is the stop itself: issues for the State ended, and the currency held.
3. A central bank can halt a run by restricting credit. The author counts four German demonstrations between 1923 and 1929.
4. Dependence on foreign credit weakened the system but did not remove the Reichsbank's control over the mark's value. The author repeats his pre-1931 statement and says events confirmed it.
5. Differences with Graham's 1930 study, as stated by the author: he puts more weight on fiscal and monetary policy and less on reparations, and he denies that the depreciation was on the whole advantageous to Germany.
6. Total production after 1923 was higher than during the inflation, "despite the apparent feverish activity of that time".

## Extraction Notes
- The chapter is clean in the extract. It has no tables, formulas or diagrams.
- The Walras passage prints the unknowns as "m-i prices, and n m the quantities" and the equation count as "m - 1 - f n m". These are read as `m − 1` prices, `n·m` quantities and `m − 1 + n·m` equations. The PDF page was not opened.
- The reference to "the systems invented... to which I have alluded in Chapter n" is read as Chapter II.
- The remarks on events after 1933 show the conclusion was revised for the 1937 English edition.

# Chapter VIII: Social Influences of the Inflation

## Core Idea
"Inflation was always a terrible instrument for the redistribution of wealth." In Germany it favoured entrepreneurs and owners of material means of production, lowered real wages, destroyed the old middle class of holders of fixed-income claims, and raised a new class of traders and speculators. The author calls it "the vastest expropriation of some classes of society that has ever been effected in time of peace". The chapter has three parts: the new fortunes, wages, and the creditors.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Winners-and-losers map** (the author's opening summary, expanded through the chapter)

| Group | Outcome | Mechanism |
|------|------|------|
| Industrial capitalists, owners of plant | Gain | Real wages lag; debts erased; taxes paid late; cheap bank credit; cheap asset purchases |
| Great speculators and merchants ("inflation profiteers") | Large gain | Borrow marks, buy shares, firms and goods; arbitrage home and world prices |
| Agricultural owners, large and small | Gain | Mortgages wiped out; product prices follow the exchange; freed from compulsory deliveries in 1922 |
| House owners | Loss | Mortgage relief outweighed by rents fixed near zero; forced sales; property concentrates |
| Tenants, and through them employers | Gain | Low rents allow low money wages |
| Wage earners | Loss in real wage rates, partly offset by fuller employment until mid-1922; heavy loss in 1923 | Wages lag prices; later extreme variability and unemployment |
| Salaried officials | Loss, larger for higher grades | Compression of differentials |
| Professions | Severe loss | Demand for their services is income-elastic |
| Rentiers: holders of government bonds, mortgages, debentures, savings deposits, life policies | Near-total loss | Nominal claims repaid in worthless marks |
| Banks, private bankers, mortgage banks, insurers, co-operatives, savings banks | Loss | Assets are money claims; legal limits on hedging |
| Small shareholders | Loss, larger than big shareholders' | Forced to sell low; diluted by insider share issues; out-voted by plural-vote shares |
| Foreign buyers of goods and houses | Gain | Purchasing power of foreign exchange inside Germany |
| Charities, foundations, pensioners, war-disabled | Near-total loss | Endowments in bonds and mortgages |

  - When to use: to forecast the political economy of an inflation. Count who holds nominal claims and who owes them.
- **Two conditions for rapid fortunes**: (1) some people obtain large liquid money capital (war profits, foreign exchange from exports, compensation for assets lost in Lorraine and the Saar, bank credit). (2) Assets are exceptionally cheap in gold (shares after the revolution, houses, firms in distress). Add borrowed money that depreciates before repayment.
- **Producers against speculators**: the pre-war magnates built fortunes on technical or organisational advances (Krupp, Siemens, Rathenau, Thyssen). The post-war "new men" were mostly "very clever speculators" whose success was "intimately connected with the inflation". Their wealth was a transfer, not a creation.
- **Concentration theorem**: transfers from creditors to debtors raise concentration, because "those who were in a position to benefit from the inflation were far fewer than the victims". Many depositors and bondholders stand behind each borrower.
- **Two-phase wage law**
  - Until summer 1922: nominal wages rise more slowly than prices, so real wages fall. Unemployment falls, which partly offsets the loss in workers' income. Demand for labour was "rather elastic".
  - End 1922 to end 1923: nominal wages rise even faster than wholesale prices and the dollar, under union pressure and indexation. Real wages become extremely variable, and unemployment rises with depreciation.
  - Reconciling rule: depreciation stimulates labour demand only while there is "a time-lag between the rise in wholesale prices and the rise in wages". Once wages rise with prices the stimulus is gone.
  - Statistical form: unemployment tends to fall when the ratio of the nominal wage index to the wholesale price index falls, and to rise when it rises (Diagram XXI). The author does not claim wages were the only or main cause of 1923 unemployment.
- **Correct deflation of wages in rapid inflation**: wages paid at the end of week 1 are spent in week 2. Real wage = `S1/C2`, `S2/C3`, not `S1/C1`. Even this fails in hyperinflation, since workers spend at once and pay intervals change.
- **Variability as a separate welfare loss**: the loss from wage variability comes on top of the loss from a lower average real wage.
- **Levelling of wage differentials**: unskilled wages hit the subsistence floor first and had to be raised, while skilled real wages were allowed to fall. Unions strengthened the unskilled. Result: the skilled-to-unskilled ratio fell from 145.9 (1914) to 106.8 (1922).
- **Revaluation as partial restitution**: courts and then law restored a fraction of old paper claims. See Reference Tables.

## Key Concepts
- **Inflation profiteers**: Stinnes, Otto Wolff, Herzfeld, Michael and others named by the author.
- **Brasseur d'affaires**: the industrialist turned general dealer in firms of every kind.
- **Verwertungsaktien (reserve shares)**: new shares not offered to shareholders and left at the disposal of boards.
- **Mark = mark**: the legal doctrine, upheld by German courts until early 1923, that a debt is discharged by its nominal amount.
- **Collective contracts**: wage agreements. They covered 1.5 million workers before the war and 14.2 million (about 75 per cent) at end 1923.
- **Normal fixed budget**: the method of the official cost-of-living index (family of five; 72 towns; monthly, then twice monthly in 1922, then weekly from March 1923).
- **Labour Charter**: the agreement of 15 November 1918 between employers and unions (union recognition, collective agreements, works councils, eight-hour day).
- **Old holders**: those who bought government securities before 1 July 1920 and received better revaluation terms.
- **Kleinrentner**: small rentiers ruined by the depreciation.

## Mental Models
- Think of the veil of paper values as the tool of expropriation. Shareholders sold at "apparently high prices". Option rights looked valuable in paper. Creditors accepted repayment at par. Only after the veil was torn did they see the loss.
- Follow the transfer through more than one hand. Low rents hurt the house owner, helped the tenant, allowed lower money wages, and so raised the employer's profit or the foreign buyer's bargain.
- Use understanding of inflation as the scarce resource. The winners were "those men who understood before others the phenomenon of the inflation".
- Expect the hedge to invert at stabilisation. Michael sold shares when others held "real goods" and lent money at very high rates when capital turned scarce.
- Read the politics from the balance sheets. Inflation enriched the groups that backed the Right and drained union funds and the middle-class supporters of the Republic. The author says nationalist propaganda spread among the ruined middle classes and communism among workers.
- Use low-grade substitutes as poverty gauges: horse and dog meat, rye for wheat, margarine for butter.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading large capital concentrations as national prosperity**: abroad they were taken as signs of German strength. They were "in great part" transfers.
- **Measuring workers' welfare by wage rates alone**: employment, variability and payment timing all matter.
- **Deflating a wage by the same week's price index in hyperinflation**: overstates real wages.
- **Treating banks as natural winners from inflation**: in Germany "financial capital" lost, unlike in Hungary.
- **Assuming secured creditors are safe**: the real value of the mortgaged property "provided no protection". Bondholders lost everything while shareholders saved part, inverting legal priority.
- **Assuming labour gains from a labour-backed government that inflates**: the wage gains of the 1918 revolution "were soon lost". The author faults union leaders for not opposing inflation early.
- **Blaming middle-class poverty on the peace treaty**: the author rejects this explicitly.

## Worked Example
**How small shareholders were expropriated.**
1. Gold share prices fall for transitory reasons: low productivity, tight money, and retention of profits.
2. Small holders need cash and fear further falls. They sell. Large holders can wait. Lewinsohn's Phonix data show shares moving from many small hands to a few large ones.
3. Companies raise capital. New shares are issued far below market price. Only part is offered to existing holders. The rest goes to syndicates of large holders, who resell at market.
4. The offered rights look valuable in paper marks, so holders do not see that their stake has been diluted.
5. Some new shares stay with supervisory boards as reserve shares, used in mergers and to control meetings.
6. Plural-vote shares (20, 30 or more votes each), first issued by the Aniline group in 1920 and justified as a defence against foreign control, take voting power from ordinary holders. The author says real foreign takeover attempts "were very rare".
7. At the 1924 gold conversion, announced capital reductions panic small holders into selling. Informed insiders buy.
8. Outcome measured by a financial journal in September 1924 for fourteen companies: gold capital was scarcely 25 per cent of what shareholders had put in since 1914. Old shareholders lost 75 per cent.

## Key Takeaways
1. Wartime real wages were well below 1913 outside munitions (printers 49.4 in 1917). The revolution's wage gains were erased by inflation.
2. Miners' real wages were about 90 per cent of pre-war in mid-1921 and 50 to 60 per cent at the end of 1922. In 1923 they swung between 47.6 and 86.2 from month to month.
3. Union membership rose from 2.5 million in 1913 to 7.9 million in 1920. Indexation agreements moved from the cost-of-living index, to the dollar rate on pay day, to forecast prices of the spending week (August 1923). None secured a stable real wage.
4. Agriculture kept its structure and gained. House property changed hands and concentrated.
5. Courts held to "mark = mark" until the Darmstadt appeal decision of 29 March 1923. The Leipzig Supreme Tribunal decided for mortgage revaluation on 23 November 1923.
6. The Reich debt was in effect cancelled. In September 1923 interest payments stopped because paying cost more than the interest.
7. The poverty of 1923 fell quickly after stabilisation, which the author takes as proof that inflation caused it. Crime, tuberculosis deaths and emigration peaked in 1923 and then fell.

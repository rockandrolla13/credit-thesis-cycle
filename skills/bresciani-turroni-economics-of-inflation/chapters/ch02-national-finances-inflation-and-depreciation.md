# Chapter II: The National Finances, the Inflation and the Depreciation of the Mark

## Core Idea
The budget deficit, financed by discounting Treasury bills at the Reichsbank, was the fundamental cause of the mark's depreciation. The German "balance of payments theory" reversed cause and effect. The author grants one qualification: in the last stages the relation became circular, because the falling exchange raised prices at once and the authorities answered every price rise with more notes. The chapter has five parts: finances, Reichsbank policy, balance of payments, reparations, speculation and vested interests.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The two rival causal chains**
  - *Balance of payments theory ("German" theory; Helfferich, Reichsbank, Havenstein, most of the Press)*: passive balance plus reparations, then exchange depreciation, then higher import prices, then higher internal prices and wages, then more need for money by public and State, then note issue. Inflation is the last link, not the first.
  - *"English" theory (British delegates on the Reparations Commission and Guarantees Committee; the author holds it "essentially correct")*: budget deficit, then note issue, then higher internal prices, then lower exchange. The author calls this the "classical scheme" and says it fits the war years exactly.
  - When to use: any dispute about whether a currency fall is "external" or "fiscal".
  - How to test (the author's method): (1) Take the monthly increase in the floating debt as the empirical index of the deficit. Gold-mark conversions of budget figures mislead. (2) Find a period when the exchange was stable. If the deficit persisted, the exchange did not cause it. In 1920-21 the gold mark moved only from 14.2 to 14.9 paper marks, yet the deficit was 6,050 million gold marks, 60 per cent of expenditure. (3) Find a period when the exchange collapsed. Check whether the deficit widened. In the second half of 1919 it did not: the smallest monthly rise in floating debt (0.7 milliard) came in February 1920, the month of the peak dollar rate. (4) Check that depreciation hit both receipts and expenditure. In 1919 and 1921 gold expenditure fell with gold receipts.
- **Limit theorem for depreciation without money creation**: an exchange fall caused only by extra demand for foreign goods, by capital flight, by reparation transfers or by speculation "cannot go beyond a certain limit" (the author suggests 15, 20 or 30 per cent of parity for a pure trade shock) unless the quantity of money and credit grows.
  - Why: with fixed money incomes, dearer imports become prohibitive. The public cannot cut consumption much to buy foreign exchange. Asset sellers must find buyers with cash, or else take losses that stop the selling. A cheap currency draws foreign buyers of shares, houses and land. Confidence returns once the public sees issues have stopped.
  - Use: continuous depreciation implies continuous money or credit creation. Capital flight financed by bank credit is stopped by credit restriction.
- **The vicious circle of the last phase**: exchange falls, internal prices rise at once, "scarcity of the circulating medium" appears, the Reichsbank and Government supply notes, the new notes lower the mark again. The circle is not a law of nature. "An energetic financial and monetary policy" would have broken it, "as in fact it was broken by the monetary reform of November 1923".
- **Scale of sensitiveness of taxes and of expenses** (see Reference Tables): whether depreciation widens the deficit depends on the mix of receipts and outlays across four sensitivity classes. A priori the sign is unknown.
- **Asymmetric price response**: internal prices follow a depreciation at once and an appreciation "weakly and slowly". So a speculative spike, once validated by new notes, sets a new equilibrium. This is why speculation, harmless in the early phase, became lasting in the late phase.
- **"Governmental" and "private" inflation**: note creation for the State and note creation for business. The Reichsbank believed discounting only "commercial" bills could not inflate. The author's rebuttal: when prices rise from 100 to 120, credit demand goes from 1,000 to 1,200 while the reflux is only 1,000, so net issue is 200 even on genuine bills. The discount rate must be adapted to the rate of depreciation.

## Key Concepts
- **Flight from the mark**: demand for foreign exchange born of distrust. The author dates its first appearance to mid-1919, after the Treaty of Versailles was signed.
- **Flight of capital**: export proceeds left abroad and savings moved into foreign currencies and securities. A "mass phenomenon" that also removed taxable wealth.
- **Latent inflation**: Treasury bills held by banks and firms outside the Reichsbank. They become notes when rediscounted.
- **Valorization**: charging taxes or debts at their gold value on the day the obligation arose.
- **Depreciation clause ("constant value" loans)**: from mid-August 1923 the Reichsbank borrower repaid principal plus four-fifths of the depreciation measured by sterling. Example given: 1,000 borrowed, sterling from 100 to 150, repay 1,400.
- **Atrophy of the fiscal system**: in the last ten days of October 1923 ordinary receipts covered about 0.8 per cent of expenses.
- **Fulfilment policy**: the German policy of trying to pay the first reparation instalments to show good faith.
- **Abnormal demand for foreign exchange**: demand for hoarding, for use as internal circulating medium, for speculation and for hedging forward sales, on top of normal trade demand.

## Mental Models
- Use the floating debt, not the gold-converted budget, as the deficit gauge in a high inflation.
- Think of placement of Treasury bills as a brake. March 1921: 101.9 of 166.4 milliards were held outside the Reichsbank. End April 1923: about three-quarters were inside it. End July 1923: 93 per cent. When the brake fails, "to every addition of Treasury bills there now corresponded an equivalent increase in the issues of notes".
- Think of depreciation as creating its own lobby. The author cites Wagner on Austria, Russia and the United States: those who profit from the gold premium oppose every reform.
- Use the location of the first move as a test for who is selling. In autumn 1921 and after, the dollar rose first in Berlin, and the mark was quoted higher abroad. The author reads this as domestic, not foreign, flight.
- A reparations transfer paid with newly created money is an inflation problem. The same transfer paid from taxes would have a bounded exchange effect.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading a multi-year import surplus as a cause of depreciation**: a surplus that persisted was financed by something (credits, asset sales, foreigners buying marks). Otherwise the imports could not have been paid for.
- **Helfferich's "the gold value of the circulation fell, so there is no inflation"**: the author treats this as the central sophism. Chapter IV answers it.
- **Supporting the exchange while still printing**: the Reichsbank lost 230 million of foreign exchange in 1922 and the February to April 1923 support broke on 18 April.
- **Fixed nominal rates in a depreciating currency**: the forced loan of March 1922 was payable at 70 paper marks per gold mark and so yielded almost nothing.
- **Rationing credit instead of pricing it**: this handed a privileged group the means to buy goods and foreign exchange.
- **Late, partial indexation**: the October 1923 tax multipliers were set below the market and were once cut on agrarian pressure.
- **Trusting speculators to stabilise**: the author lists swings such as 276 marks per dollar on 29 Nov 1921 to 190 on 1 Dec, and 4,465 on 1 Nov 1922 to 8,068 on 7 Nov to 6,711 two days later.

## Worked Example
**The first milliard of reparations, 31 August 1921.**
1. How it was raised (million gold marks): foreign exchange bought in the market or remitted by the Reichsbank 541; Dutch bank credits 270; silver pledged 58; gold bought at home 15; Bank of Italy credit 32; German bank loans 16; consigned in gold 68.
2. There was no export surplus, so the payment did press on the exchange. Depreciation quickened in October when the Dutch credits had to be repaid at short notice.
3. A second influence was larger in the author's view: panic at the League decision on Upper Silesia. The Berlin market broke on 17 October 1921.
4. Speculation overshot: the dollar passed 300 marks in early November and fell to 190 on 1 December.
5. The author's question to Elster: why were the consequences so serious? Answer: the Government bought the foreign exchange with newly created notes, not with purchasing power taken from citizens by taxes. Without new money the fall would have hit a limit quickly.
6. Check against later data: cash reparations stopped after 15 July 1922. One of the fastest falls of the mark came between 15 July and 31 August 1922. In 1922 the balance of payments was probably favourable, yet depreciation accelerated.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ten-year totals (gold): taxes 21.2 milliards, loans 52.6, Treasury bills 59.1. Taxes covered "scarcely 15 per cent" of expenditure from 1914 to October 1923.
2. The exchange can jump without a prior jump in notes (mid-1919, autumn 1921, July 1922, January and July 1923). Such jumps are psychological and political. They last only if money creation ratifies them.
3. Depreciation first helped the budget (it wiped out debt service: consolidated Reich debt was 58.5 milliard gold marks in March 1918, 33.7 in March 1919, 5.4 a year later). From summer 1922 it hurt, because prices and wages now reacted fast and receipts lagged.
4. Collection lags are a subsidy to payers. The wage tax sat about a fortnight with employers in summer 1923. In March 1923, 95 per cent of income-tax yield came from wage and salary earners.
5. A central bank rate far below the rate of depreciation finances the attack on its own currency.
6. The Ruhr occupation was financed by notes. Circulation went from 1,280 milliards at end 1922 to 6,546 at end April 1923.
7. The author rejects the charge that the Government deliberately destroyed the mark. He accepts that industrialists and agrarians obstructed reform and profited.

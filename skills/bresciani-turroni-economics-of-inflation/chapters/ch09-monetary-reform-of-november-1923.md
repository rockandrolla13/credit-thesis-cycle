# Chapter IX: The Monetary Reform of November 1923

## Core Idea
The mark was stabilised on 20 November 1923 at 4,200 milliard paper marks per dollar, without foreign loans, without a balanced budget and while the circulation went on rising. The author explains the "miracle of the Rentenmark" by the state the currency had reached: the real value of paper marks was tiny, velocity was at its peak, other monies had taken over much of the circulation, and the floating debt and foreign-held marks were worth almost nothing. Once the State stopped borrowing from the Reichsbank and the public believed the new issue was limited, demand for money recovered and absorbed a large new issue. Stability was then nearly lost in early 1924 through loose Reichsbank credit and saved by the credit stop of 7 April 1924.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Stabilisation, not revaluation**: the paper mark was fixed at its depreciated value, not raised. The rentenmark was a second currency, valued at one gold mark through the right to convert 500 rentenmarks into a 500 gold mark mortgage bond at 5 per cent. The legal devaluation came with the law of 30 August 1924 (1 reichsmark = 1 billion paper marks, long scale).
- **Why the simple quantity-theory account is wrong**: the paper mark was not withdrawn or converted at a fixed ratio, and legal currency expanded strongly after the reform. "The stabilization of the German exchange was not obtained by means of contraction, or even by a stoppage of the expansion of the circulation."
- **Explanations the author rejects**
  - A fall in consumption and imports (one view reported in the Dawes Report): false. Consumption and imports rose at once, and the trade deficit widened.
  - Balance of trade as a precondition: false. The exchange was stabilised while the balance was "markedly unfavourable".
  - The mortgage cover of the rentenmark: at most a psychological aid. Market yields were 15 to 20 per cent, far above the bond's 5 per cent, so conversion could not hold the value. The earlier Gold Loan notes held their dollar value with no cover at all.
- **The author's explanation: five pre-conditions created by extreme inflation**
  1. Real value of paper marks was minute: scarcely 150 million gold marks at end October 1923. By end 1922 it was already below the Reichsbank's gold reserve.
  2. Other monies had displaced the mark: foreign exchange and emergency money. The total value of emergency money came to exceed that of legal tender.
  3. Velocity was at a maximum, so any return of confidence would raise the demand for money sharply.
  4. Foreign-held marks and mark deposits (about 30 milliard paper marks at end 1920) had been wiped out, so no overhang could return.
  5. The floating debt was worth scarcely 200 million gold marks, so no short-term debt needed refinancing.
- **Paradox of advanced inflation**: "it became easier to redeem or convert paper money the more rapidly the paper inflation proceeded". The author holds that stabilisation with restored convertibility was feasible in the second half of 1922, had the Reichsbank agreed to use its gold and had issues for the State stopped.
- **Rule on adding a new money to an old one**: if the old money fills all channels of circulation, a new issue requires calling in an equal amount of the old. If the old money's circulation is restricted, the new can circulate beside it, replacing non-legal monies, "without the new issues causing a fresh inflation".
- **The decisive conditions**
  - Fiscal: the Reichsbank stopped discounting Treasury bills on 16 November 1923. The author calls this "a fact of fundamental importance".
  - Quantity limit: the rentenmark issue was capped at 2,400 million, of which 1,200 million for the Government. The Rentenbank's refusal of a Government request to exceed its share in December 1923 strengthened confidence. "The limitation of the quantity was then of primary and fundamental importance."
  - Psychology: a new name allowed the State to raise 1,200 million gold marks of transitional finance. Announcing the same sum in paper marks, about ten times the existing circulation, would have destroyed belief.
- **Why circulation could rise without depreciation**
  - Velocity fell as people stopped fleeing the mark.
  - Rentenmarks and new paper marks replaced emergency monies and Gold Loan notes (about 1,100 to 1,200 million marks of the increase).
  - The public sold hoarded foreign exchange to the Reichsbank for German money.
  - French francs were pushed out of the occupied territory from February 1924.
- **Credit policy as the test of the stabilisation (1924)**: easy Reichsbank credit in the first quarter weakened the mark and raised prices. Rationing of credit from 7 April restored it. The author states the causal link "was quite obvious" and was admitted even in official papers.
- **Budget follows the exchange**: taxes could not close the deficit while the currency fell, because real yields eroded and outlays rose with depreciation. Once the rate was fixed, receipts recovered within two months.

## Key Concepts
- **Rentenbank and rentenmark**: created by decree of 15 October 1923; first notes on 16 November. "A legal means of payment", not legal tender.
- **Goldanleihe**: Gold Loan of 500 million gold marks (law of 14 August 1923), issued in small denominations and used as money.
- **Wertbeständiges Notgeld**: emergency money "with a constant value", issued by provinces, towns, chambers of commerce and the railways against deposits of Gold Loan or Gold Treasury bonds.
- **Reichsmark**: the currency from 11 October 1924. Same gold content as the old mark (1,392 marks = 500 grams of fine gold). Notes stayed inconvertible; the author calls the system a "dollar exchange standard".
- **Golddiskontbank**: founded by the law of 13 March 1924 to finance foreign purchases, since rentenmarks were not international money.
- **Constant value clause**: borrowers from the Rentenbank repaid in gold-mark value. Abolished on 11 October 1924.
- **Quota**: the share of foreign exchange demand the Reichsbank met at the official rate.
- **Micum agreements**: from 23 November 1923, deliveries on reparations account were shifted for a time from the budget to industry.

## Mental Models
- Think of hyperinflation as burning off the two things that make stabilisation hard: the monetary overhang and the short-term debt. What is left is a currency so small in real terms that any credible stop makes it scarce.
- Use the central bank's foreign exchange inflow as the sign that confidence has turned. The Reichsbank's "other assets" rose from 18.8 million gold marks on 15 November 1923 to 1,183 million on 31 October 1924.
- Use the gap between the official and free rate, and the rationing quota, as the measure of a stabilisation's fragility. On 8 February 1924 the quota was 3 per cent for sterling and 2 per cent for dollars, and Cologne quoted the dollar about a tenth above Berlin.
- When working capital and deposits have been destroyed, all credit demand falls on the central bank. Its credit policy then decides the exchange.
- Names and symbols matter when trust is gone. The public "allowed itself to be hypnotized" by the word "wertbeständig", and accepted Gold Loan notes backed only by a vague promise of future taxes.
- A sudden stop is the normal form. Austria (1922) and Hungary (June 1924) stabilised just as abruptly, and in both, as in Poland, circulation kept rising afterwards.

## Anti-patterns
- **Waiting for a trade surplus or a reparations settlement before stabilising**: Helfferich called the attempt a "leap over a ravine". It succeeded without either.
- **Concluding from rising note circulation that stabilisation is fake**: Professor Harms called it "the most colossal monetary inflation ever recorded". The author says he missed the recovery of money demand.
- **Relying on exchange control instead of credit restriction**: rationing did not stop unnecessary imports and created a free-market premium.
- **Granting easy credit right after stabilisation to avoid a crisis**: short-term Reichsbank credit went from 364 million gold marks to 1,915 million in four months and nearly broke the peg.
- **Relaxing the budget when it shows a surplus**: tax cuts came in November 1924, and spending discipline lapsed soon after.
- **Believing in the "cover"**: the Dawes experts judged the rentenmark "precarious and artificial" for lack of liquid cover. It worked anyway, for other reasons.

## Worked Example
**How 2 milliard gold marks of new money entered without inflation.**
1. Starting point, mid-November 1923: paper marks worth about 93 to 150 million gold marks at the official rate. Pre-war circulation was about 6 milliards. Trade is carried on with foreign exchange (estimates range from 1.5 to 4 milliard gold marks, all unreliable) and with perhaps 2,000 kinds of emergency money.
2. The Government needs 1,200 million gold marks to bridge to a balanced budget, of which 300 million to redeem paper Treasury bonds. No foreign loan is available.
3. It cannot say "we will print ten times the present paper circulation". It issues the sum as rentenmarks, under a decree capping the total at 2,400 million.
4. The Reichsbank stops discounting Treasury bills. The floating debt of 197 trillion paper marks is repaid with 197 million rentenmarks lent interest-free by the Rentenbank.
5. Confidence returns. People hold the new notes instead of spending them at once. Velocity falls, so the same trade needs more money.
6. Rentenmarks and new paper marks replace emergency money (728 million gold marks at end 1923, 38 million by July 1924) and foreign currency, which the public now sells to the Reichsbank.
7. By July 1924 the legal circulation is about 1,211 million gold marks of paper marks plus 1,803 million rentenmarks. The dollar rate is unchanged.
8. The limit of the process shows in February to March 1924. Commercial credit from the Reichsbank grows too fast, the New York quotation drops to 20.40 cents on 9 February against a parity of 23.82, and prices turn up. The credit cap of 7 April reverses it. The author dates true stabilisation from then.

## Key Takeaways
1. The gold reserve of the Reichsbank fell from a milliard at the start of 1923 to 444 million on 15 October. The reform was carried out with almost no reserves.
2. Luther's own description of the situation before 16 November: the starving of the towns was so plain that "a dissolution of the social order must have been expected almost from hour to hour".
3. Relief was immediate: trade revived, food reached the cities, factories reopened, unemployment fell.
4. Consumption jumped. Imports of butter went from 5,234 quintals in the first half of 1923 to 181,655 in the first half of 1924 (official statistics, footnote table not checked). The trade deficit rose from 136 million gold marks in January 1924 to 354 million in May. Passports were priced at 500 gold marks in April 1924 to curb foreign travel.
5. Debt service after the reform was 156 million gold marks out of 4,942 million of ordinary expenditure, little more than 3 per cent. In 1919 interest had been 10 of 17.5 milliard marks.
6. The public debt of the Reich was barely 3,265 million gold marks after stabilisation, including 926.4 million of 1924 foreign loans. The date prints as "October 31st, 1923", which cannot be right given the 1924 loans.
7. From 1925 to 1931 the mark's external value was governed by the flow of foreign loans.

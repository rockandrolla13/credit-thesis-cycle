# Chapter III: The Divergences between the Internal Value and the External Value of the Mark

## Core Idea
A depreciating paper currency has two values: what it buys at home (internal) and what it buys in gold or foreign goods (external). In Germany the external value stayed below the internal value from the Armistice to mid-1923, and the gap closed and then reversed in the last months. The author measures the gap with a "coefficient of divergence" and reads its path as three phases. The gap drove export premiums, tax erosion and class conflict. The chapter has an appendix on how to average a collapsing exchange rate.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three measures of depreciation** (recovered from PDF p. 123)
  - (a) Internal: `(p − 100) / p`, where `p` is the German paper price index (July 1914 = 100).
  - (b) Discount against gold: `(0.2382 − C) / 0.2382`, where `C` is the dollar price of the paper mark and 0.2382 is the mint par.
  - (c) External, in foreign goods: `(0.2382·P − 100·C) / (0.2382·P)`, where `P` is the gold price index in the world market.
  - When to use: always state which of the three a "depreciation" figure means. They coincide only if world gold prices are constant and parity holds.
- **Coefficient of divergence D** (recovered from PDF p. 124)
  - Parity condition: `(p − 100)/p = (0.2382·P − 100·C)/(0.2382·P)` (eq. 1), which gives `P/p = C/0.2382` (eq. 2).
  - `D = 0.2382·P / (p·C)`.
  - Equivalent readings: actual exchange rate of the gold mark over the "price parity"; or the index of world gold prices over the index of German prices converted to gold.
  - `D > 1`: external value below internal value. German goods are cheap in gold. `D < 1`: German gold prices above world prices.
  - How: (1) Compute the price parity as German paper index over the US (Bureau of Labour) index. (2) Divide the index of the gold mark's paper price by it. (3) Repeat with the gold index of imported goods as a cross-check.
- **Three theories of which side is active** (author's classification)
  - (a) The home price level is fundamental (Lexis, Dalberg, Pohle).
  - (b) The exchange moves first, from the balance of payments, and drags home prices (Wagner in substance; the prevailing German view).
  - (c) Interdependence: one or the other prevails by circumstance (Angell reports this view gaining ground).
  - The author's own conclusion is phase-dependent. See next item.
- **Phase rule for the direction of causation**
  - Early phase: the external value was "dominated mainly" by the fall of the mark's purchasing power at home. A rigid "system" of internal prices acted as a centre of gravity and pulled the exchange back.
  - Later phase: the dollar rate moved first under causes acting directly on it. Internal prices followed with a lag, so external stayed below internal.
  - Last phase (summer to autumn 1923): no price system was left. Prices followed "every breath in the exchange market". They then overshot, because they priced in future depreciation.
  - The correction mechanism mattered: each widening of the gap was closed "not in an improvement of the exchange" but by a rise in internal prices, "because of the continual note issues".
- **Order of transmission of new money**: in the divergence period new issues "acted first on the exchanges and afterwards on internal prices", through speculation or direct Government purchase of foreign exchange. Later the money entered internal circulation.
- **Coefficient of inertia of prices** (Messedaglia's term): prices follow the exchange slowly. German sources of inertia were:
  1. The "political price" of bread: compulsory deliveries (2.5 million tons in 1921-22) at legal prices, and imported cereals sold at a loss.
  2. Coal held below the world price.
  3. Rent control.
  4. Railway rates below pre-war levels.
  5. Hoarding of notes, especially in the country, even into 1922.
  6. Pricing at purchase cost. Pricing at "repurchase" or "reproduction" cost was at first treated in law as usury. It spread late in 1921 and was general by the second half of 1922.
- **Premium against the risk of depreciation**: in the last phase sellers raised the gold "basic price" to cover the loss between receiving paper and converting it. Result: internal gold prices above world prices, and retail above wholesale.
- **Reply to Aftalion (exchange-dominates-prices theory)**: without more money, extra spending on foreign exchange means less spending on home goods. Home prices then hold. The exchange permanently affects only import prices and temporarily export prices. "Internal prices can only be displaced under the pressure of the depreciation of the exchange in so far as there is a continual issue of paper money."
- **Averaging rule for a collapsing exchange (Appendix to Ch III)**
  - The exact monthly average rate is the transactions-weighted arithmetic mean of daily rates of the gold mark in paper marks. Weights are the gold marks exchanged each day.
  - Hypothesis I, constant paper marks exchanged per day: the harmonic mean of the gold mark's daily paper price. This equals the reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of the paper mark's gold price. The Paris office of the Reparations Commission used it.
  - Hypothesis II, constant gold marks exchanged per day: the arithmetic mean of the gold mark's daily paper price. The Berlin office and the Reich Statistical Bureau used it.
  - The author's verdict: neither is exact, but Hypothesis II "is much nearer to reality", because paper sums tended to grow in step with depreciation. The geometric mean sits between and understates.

## Key Concepts
- **Internal value (Binnenwert) and external value (Aussenwert)**: terms the author credits to Lexis.
- **Price parity**: ratio of German paper prices to US prices.
- **Export premium**: the gain to exporters when home gold prices are below world prices (Wagner).
- **Multiplier and basic price**: late-1923 pricing. Paper price = gold base price times an index tied to the exchange.
- **Geldentwertungszuschlag**: the official surcharge for currency depreciation on coal prices, August 1923.
- **Zwangswirtschaft**: the system of maximum prices and coercive measures kept after the war.
- **Vis inertiae of prices**: older writers' term for the same friction.

## Mental Models
- Think of D as a pressure gauge. High D means a subsidy to exporters, foreigners living cheaply, a tax base shrinking in gold, and pressure on wages and prices to catch up.
- Use the geography of price rises to tell the source. Citing Wagner: a rise that comes from the gold premium starts in ports and trading centres and spreads inland along transport routes. A rise from note issues can start from the centres of production.
- Use the sign of the first exchange response to a world-price move as a short-run test of import elasticity. In 1919 rising world prices probably weakened the mark, because German import demand was inelastic. In the second half of 1920 falling world prices probably helped it.
- When sellers quote different prices by means of payment, read the spread as the market's own estimate of expected depreciation.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using the official wartime import-price index as a measure of external value**: for the war years it rested on four commodities (lead, copper, zinc, tin). The author withdraws his own earlier use of it.
- **Comparing German and US indices without adjustment**: the German index covered raw and semi-finished goods; the US index also covered finished goods.
- **Supporting the exchange with asset or gold sales while inflating at home**: "a mistaken policy". Better to let the exchange fall and let imports contract.
- **Converting a year's paper totals at a harmonic-mean rate**: gives absurd gold values. See Worked Example.
- **Trusting the official Reich price index for summer and autumn 1923**: it understates the rise. The author prefers the Berliner Tageblatt index of 117 articles.

## Worked Example
**Which average rate converts 1922 exports to gold?**
1. German exports in 1922 were 1,732,093 million paper marks.
2. League of Nations Bulletin average for 1922 (harmonic type): 101.2 paper marks per gold mark. Dividing gives 17,116 million gold marks. The author calls this absurd.
3. Reich Statistical Bureau average (arithmetic type): 449.2. Dividing gives 3,865 million gold marks.
4. Month-by-month conversion by the Bureau: 3,970 million gold marks.
5. The arithmetic mean lands within 3 per cent of the true figure because the gold value of exports was fairly even across months.
6. Second check, October 1923: the daily rises in floating debt sum to 8,355,615 billion paper marks and 255.0 million dollars. That is 1,071 million gold marks, so the implied average is 7,801 million paper marks per gold mark. The arithmetic mean was 7,450 million, the geometric 1,675 million, the harmonic 353 million.

## Key Takeaways
1. The divergence was "one of the central facts of the economic life of Germany from 1919 to 1923".
2. D peaked at 3.25 in February 1920, then oscillated with every exchange shock: October and November 1921, August and November 1922, January and July 1923.
3. Price controls, rent control, cheap coal, cheap rail and note hoarding explain the slow early response of internal prices.
4. Regional dispersion of the cost of living rose with the inflation: 8.16 per cent in September 1921, 9.56 in September 1922, 17.64 in June 1923, 30.4 in September 1923.
5. In the last phase internal prices outran the exchange. In November 1923 wholesale food prices were 2.05 times pre-war in gold and retail 2.39 times. In that month retail prices rose 89-fold and wholesale 28-fold.
6. Stabilisation removed the risk premium and gold prices fell in December 1923. The author notes the same happened in Austria.
7. Market quotations themselves broke down. On 17 Aug 1923 the dollar was 3.2 million marks in Berlin and 4.4 million in Cologne. At Zurich on 5 Nov the bid and ask for a billion marks were 0.50 and 3.50 francs.

# Chapter IV: Relations between the Total Value and the Quantity of Paper Money in Circulation

## Core Idea
In an advanced inflation prices and the exchange rise faster than the note issue, so the total real (gold) value of the circulation shrinks. German officials read the shrinking real value as proof that there was no inflation. The author calls this "an economic sophism". The fall in real value is the effect of inflation: people cut the share of income they hold as money, velocity rises, foreign exchange and emergency money displace the mark, and fewer goods are offered for paper.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The sophism of the "real value of the circulation"** (Helfferich, Havenstein, Elster, Julius Wolf, the Reich Statistical Bureau)
  - Claim: on 7 Aug 1923 notes were 62,338 milliard paper marks and a gold mark cost 783,000, so the circulation was worth "scarcely 80 million gold marks" against about 6 milliards before the war. Hence no inflation. Variant: the circulation rose 15 to 20 times while prices rose 40 to 50 times, so money is scarce.
  - Rebuttal: the same test would show more inflation in England than in Germany at end 1922, and falling German inflation from 1920 to 1922.
  - When to use: whenever someone cites low real balances, low money-to-GDP or "tight" real credit as proof that policy is not inflationary during a rapid depreciation.
- **Marshall's cash-balance explanation** (the author's preferred statement): each person keeps a share of income as "ready purchasing power". The total real value of money tends to equal the sum of those desired holdings. If people want to hold less, the total real value falls and prices rise for a given quantity. "The total value of an inconvertible paper currency therefore cannot be increased by increasing its quantity." An increase expected to be repeated lowers the unit's value more than in proportion.
- **Keynes's explanation**: in an advanced inflation new issues come with higher velocity, and velocity can outweigh the issues themselves (the author cites "Inflation as a Form of Taxation").
- **Correct causal order** (section VII): it is "inaccurate to say that prices increase more rapidly than the quantity of money because the velocity of the circulation increases". The order is: desired money holdings fall, so more money is offered, prices rise, and money circulates faster.
- **Measuring velocity from the equation of exchange** (eq. 1 from the text layer of PDF p. 167; eqs. 2 and 3 recovered from PDF p. 168)
  - `M·V = p1·a + p2·b + p3·c + …` (eq. 1).
  - `M'·V' = p'1·a + p'2·b + p'3·c + …` (eq. 2), with quantities of goods held constant.
  - `M'V' / MV = P` (eq. 3), where `P` is the base-weighted average of price changes.
  - So `V'/V = P / (M'/M)`. With `M` = 6 milliard marks in 1913.
  - Series A uses wholesale prices: velocity of money capital among firms. Series B uses retail prices (cost of food): income-velocity in the sense of Schumpeter and Pigou.
  - Caveat stated by the author: the indices are "very rough". Real income was below pre-war and goods velocity changed. The same numbers follow from the Pigou or Robertson cash-balance equations.
- **Three-stage law of German depreciation** (section V, the author's conclusions)
  - (a) Early and longest stage: depreciation "mainly in proportion to the quantity of paper money".
  - (b) Summer 1921 to summer 1922: issues reinforced by higher velocity, by marks returning from abroad and by marks leaving hoards.
  - (c) After summer 1922: foreign exchange and other means of payment took the mark's place. The mark's "field of circulation" shrank and its value fell more than in proportion to issues.
- **Gresham's law reversed**: under great distrust "good money drives out the bad". The assignats showed the same: fast depreciation coincided with the return of metallic money.
- **Goods-withdrawal channel**: in summer 1923 farmers, industrialists and retailers refused to sell for paper marks. New money was "concentrated on a continually lessening quantity of goods". The quantity side of the equation fell twice over: lower output and fewer goods offered for paper.
- **Velocity formula against Bortkiewicz** (eqs. 4 to 6; eq. 4 recovered from PDF p. 179, eq. 5 from the text layer of p. 180, eq. 6 from the author's verbal statement and numbers)
  - `(1/a)·M = (1/b)·S·P` per day (eq. 4). `a` = days in which the money stock `M` is spent. `b` = days in which the goods stock `S` is exhausted.
  - `M·r = S·v·P` over a period (eq. 5). `r` = times money changes hands. `v` = times `S` is replaced (production cycles).
  - `r = v · (b/a)` (eq. 6): velocity equals the number of production cycles times the ratio of the days to exhaust goods over the days to spend the money.
  - Bortkiewicz's case is `b/a` constant: buyers hurry, prices are fixed, goods run out early, money then lies idle with producers, velocity is unchanged.
  - The author's case: hurried buying raises prices, so `b` falls less than `a`, and velocity rises.
- **Direct and indirect effects of an exchange fall on velocity**: indirect (expectation of price rises hastens purchases) is temporary if the exchange stops falling. Direct effects are slight while confidence holds, because gains to some traders are losses to others. Only in the last phase does an exchange fall raise income-velocity directly. That phase exists only because of excess issues, "hence monetary and banking policy always remains the dominating factor".

## Key Concepts
- **Total real value of the circulation**: nominal circulation divided by a price index or by the gold mark rate.
- **Ready purchasing power**: Marshall's term for money held available for spending.
- **Circulating period**: Pigou's term for the round in which wages flow to retailers, wholesalers, producers and back to incomes.
- **Income-velocity**: number of times a unit of money becomes income in a period.
- **Velocity of money capital**: money volume of transactions between stages of production over the money stock.
- **Notgeld**: emergency money issued by banks, firms and public bodies.
- **Field of circulation**: the range of transactions still settled in the paper currency.
- **Flight from the mark, dated by velocity**: first in July 1919; calmed in 1920; renewed and permanent from July 1921.

## Mental Models
- Use velocity as a confidence index. The author says the velocity diagram is an index "however rough, of the degree of confidence" in the currency. Falling velocity in wartime meant full confidence and hoarding.
- Cross-check velocity with Treasury bill placement. Bills placed outside the Reichsbank rose from 50.9 milliards in February 1920 to 113.9 in April 1921, the same period in which velocity fell.
- Firms move first. From the second half of 1921 series A is well above series B. The two converge in calm periods and separate in rapid depreciation.
- Rule out the output explanation before blaming velocity. In late 1919 and in 1921-22 output was rising (the author puts 1922 at 70 to 80 per cent of pre-war), yet real balances fell to a third.
- When real balances reach implausibly low levels (80 million gold marks against 6 milliards), look for substitute monies. Velocity alone cannot explain it.
- The rise in velocity is reversible. If the central bank "stands fast" and stops issues, a shortage of money is felt, prices stop, confidence returns and velocity falls. After November 1923 it fell at once.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging credit policy by the real value of the central bank's portfolio**: Havenstein argued the Reichsbank portfolio was worth less than half its 1913 gold value. The author: a doubling of nominal bills raised prices more than twofold through velocity, so the real portfolio fell because policy was loose.
- **Comparing real money per head across countries as an inflation index**: yields England above Germany.
- **Treating the rise in velocity as an independent cause that clears monetary policy**: it follows from the issues and from distrust.
- **Assuming payment habits adjust smoothly to higher prices**: each wave produced a real scarcity of money and new emergency monies.
- **Dividing an arithmetic-mean price index by a harmonic-mean exchange rate**: the League of Nations figures for October 1923 would imply gold prices twenty times pre-war.

## Worked Example
**The author's arithmetic against Bortkiewicz.**
1. Money stock `M` = 3,000 units. One-third is offered each day for 10 units of goods. Price = 100.
2. Production cycle = 6 days. Each cycle brings `S` = 60 units of goods. In 12 days payments total 12,000. Velocity `r` = 12,000 / 3,000 = 4. Formula: `b` = 6, `a` = 3, `v` = 2, so `r` = 2 × 6/3 = 4.
3. Now 1,500 units of money come to market each day (`a` = 2).
4. Case 1, prices fixed by law at 100: 15 units of goods sell each day, `S` is gone in 4 days, the market is empty for 2 days. Transactions in 12 days are still 12,000. `r` = 2 × 4/2 = 4. Velocity unchanged. This is Bortkiewicz's case.
5. Case 2, prices rise to 150: 10 units sell each day for 1,500, `S` lasts 6 days. Transactions in 12 days are 18,000. `r` = 2 × 6/2 = 6.
6. Conclusion: hurried spending raises velocity whenever it raises prices. Empirical support given: Berlin prices rose on the days quarterly salaries were paid. Wage payment intervals were shortened steadily in the last phase.
7. Even in case 1 there is an indirect effect. Producers holding idle cash with no goods to buy turn to foreign exchange, which lowers the external value.

## Key Takeaways
1. Real balances rose during the war and fell after it. The turning points match the confidence crises of July 1919 and July 1921.
2. Wholesale-based velocity went from 0.43 in June 1919 to 1.85 in February 1920, back to 0.96 in mid-1921, then to 17.79 in October 1923.
3. About 10 milliard paper marks, one-seventh of the issue, was estimated to be hoarded at end 1920. Hoards and foreign-held marks later returned and intensified the price rise.
4. In summer 1923 hardly any retailer or workman held cash for more than two or three days.
5. In 1923, 97.1 per cent of Reichsbank bills ran for about 15 days. In 1913 the figure was 35.3 per cent.
6. The pattern is general: assignats, Austria, Russia, Hungary and Poland all show real balances collapsing in the late stage and recovering after stabilisation.
7. Low real balances at the end are what makes stabilisation possible with a small new issue. Chapter IX builds on this.

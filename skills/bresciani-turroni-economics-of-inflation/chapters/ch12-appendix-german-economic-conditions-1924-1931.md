# Appendix: German Economic Conditions from the End of the Inflation until 1931

## Core Idea
After stabilisation Germany went through a long, stop-start stabilisation crisis (1923-26), a foreign-loan-financed boom of rationalisation and public works (late 1926 to 1928), and a depression that ended in the banking crisis of July 1931. The author explains the cycle by two things together: the supply of capital, above all foreign capital with too much of it short-term, and the profit expectations of entrepreneurs, hit by rigid wages, rigid cartel prices, taxes, mistaken fixed investment and political distrust. This file is ch12 in the skill. In the book it is the Appendix that follows Chapter XI.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four phases of the stabilisation crisis** (the author's division, which he calls "somewhat arbitrary")

| Phase | Period | What happened |
|------|------|------|
| 1 | Nov 1923 to Apr 1924 | No crisis at first. Trade and output revived. Short-time workers fell from 1.8 million (Nov 1923) to 90,133 (15 Mar 1924) |
| 2 | Apr to summer 1924 | Reichsbank stops credit growth. Interest up, unemployment up, bankruptcies up. Prices fall, exports rise, trade balance favourable in July and August 1924 |
| 3 | Late 1924 to early 1925 | Dawes loans flow in. Relief, but "they prolonged the condition of malaise". Production index 82 (Q3 1924) to 102 (Q1 1925), 1913 = 100 |
| 4 | Summer 1925 to early 1926 | The real adjustment. Inflation groups collapse. 31,183 bankruptcies from Q3 1925 to Q2 1927. About three million out of work in March 1926 |

  - When to use: to set expectations for the length and shape of a post-stabilisation adjustment. The author stresses that there was "not in reality a rapid deflation": the central bank hesitated, lent below market rates, and the Government propped up failing industries.
- **Liquidity pre-conditions of a revival** (from the 1926 depression)
  1. Central bank liquidity: Reichsbank gold and foreign exchange rose from 1,673 to 2,350 million marks during 1926. Reserve ratio rose from 40.8 to 48.0 per cent.
  2. Commercial bank liquidity: the "coefficient of elasticity" of the six great banks rose from 50 (August 1925) to 61 (December 1926).
  3. Business liquidity: stocks were run down, which freed money capital and improved the trade balance.
  - Summary in the author's words: "the general increasing liquidity of the central bank, of big commercial banks, and of most industrial concerns".
- **Triggers of the 1926 recovery, in the author's order**
  - Fall in interest rates. Discount rate 9 per cent at end 1925, 6 per cent by 6 July 1926. Cost of a mortgage credit about 15 per cent at end 1925 and about 10 per cent in summer 1926.
  - Share prices rise once the rate of interest drops below share yields, "independently of any improvement in economic prospects". The rise then works on business psychology.
  - Housebuilding responds first, since rents are insensitive to the cycle and building depends on mortgage rates.
  - Stocks are exhausted, so merchants reorder.
  - A good harvest (cereals 8.3 million tons in 1924, 11.4 million in 1925) cuts imports.
  - The English coal strike (May to November 1926) lifts German coal and steel. German fuel exports rise by 225 million marks year on year.
  - Underneath all: the "rational transformation" of plant, which acted like a wave of inventions.
- **Extension against intensification**: inflation-era investment enlarged plant without perfecting it. The 1926-28 investment aimed at higher productivity. So Germany could have surplus plant and still need heavy new fixed investment.
- **Forced saving in a credit expansion, tested**: the theory says rising prices cut the consumption of lagging incomes and so feed newly hired workers. German data are "not incompatible": in 1926 money wages were stable, employment rose and real wage rates fell. But real labour income rose, so forced saving here means more work for a real wage not raised in proportion. The author judges it secondary to foreign capital.
- **How foreign loans entered**: not as machines, since Germany makes them, but as food and raw materials for consumer goods. Loans paid wages, wages bought imports. The loans supplied the subsistence fund while German labour rebuilt plant.
- **Removal of the interest-rate check**: easy foreign borrowing paralyses the rise in interest that would normally stop over-production of instrumental goods.
- **Two theories of the depression, reconciled**
  - "Crisis of returns": weak profit prospects cut the demand for capital (Reichskreditgesellschaft).
  - "Shortage of capital": inflow of foreign capital gave way to outflow, raising interest (Reich Government statement, October 1930).
  - The author's synthesis: both. If only demand for capital had fallen, interest would have fallen. It rose in 1928-29, so supply fell too. Uncertain supply itself deters investment.
- **Wage-price ratio and unemployment, three periods** (Diagram XXVIII, described in the text)
  - 1926 to mid-1927: the ratio of money wages to consumer-goods prices rose because prices fell with better organisation. Profits held and unemployment fell.
  - Second half of 1927: large wage rises, absorbed by rising prices. Unemployment still fell.
  - From early 1928: prices no longer kept pace with wages. Unemployment rose and went on rising as real wages kept climbing in the slump.
  - The author's caution: the proof is "somewhat vague", other costs mattered, and a correlation does not exclude other causes.
- **Rigid system thesis**: a "quasi-elastic system" of falling wages, costs and prices in depressions was replaced by "an almost rigid system", which prolonged the depression. Cartel prices fell 11 per cent from end 1929 to end 1931. Free prices fell about 50 per cent from early 1928 to end 1931.
- **Barometer fallacy**: early 1930 looked like early 1926 (liquid money market, rising shares). It was not. In 1926 liquidation was far advanced, finances sound, politics calm and foreign lenders willing. In 1930 none of this held. The Bourse was "a false barometer" again in early 1931.

## Key Concepts
- **Coefficient of elasticity (or of liquidity)**: liquid assets over sight liabilities of the big banks.
- **Sensitive prices index**: scrap, pig-iron, steel, brass, lead, steel plates, skins, wool, hemp and flax. It peaked at 140.9 in January 1928, a year before general prices turned.
- **Public money**: surplus funds of the Reich, States and public bodies placed in the money market.
- **Disinvested working capital**: money freed by running down stocks, appearing as bank deposits.
- **Revolt of the capitalists** (Bonn's phrase): savers hoard, send money abroad or lend only at short term.
- **Frozen credits**: short-term bank loans that financed fixed plant and could not be recalled.
- **Crash of 13 May 1927**: the Reichsbank ordered banks to cut loans to stock-exchange speculators by 25 per cent.
- **Nordwolle and Danat**: the Bremen wool firm whose failure (liabilities 235 million marks, assets scarcely 39 million) brought down the Darmstädter und Nationalbank, which closed on 13 July 1931.

## Mental Models
- Think of a recovery financed by short-term foreign money as carrying "uncertain and unstable elements". The author says so before describing the collapse.
- Use the gap between short and long rates in two ways. In an expansion short rates rise faster and the gap narrows. In a confidence crisis the gap widens, because savers will only lend short.
- Use the trade balance as a capital-flow gauge, not a health gauge. Germany's export surplus of 1930-31 was "the most obvious symptom" of capital shortage.
- Consumption lags the cycle. Luxury demand fell first. Mass consumption held up through 1929 and fell appreciably only in 1930. So falling consumer demand "was not the cause of the depression".
- Read rises in the share of short-notice deposits as businesses bracing for a credit squeeze.
- A central bank order aimed at speculation can damage the whole capital market. After 13 May 1927 share and bond issues fell, mortgage lending shrank and building permits dropped from July.
- Read the first place rising unemployment shows up. In 1925 and again later it was the production-goods industries.

## Anti-patterns
- **Cheap money to deter foreign borrowing**: the discount cut to 5 per cent on 12 January 1927 was followed by rapid credit growth, higher imports and a loss of about a milliard of gold and foreign exchange in a few months.
- **Banks lending short-term funds for fixed plant, too cheaply, with no shared credit register**: no bank knew what a firm owed the others.
- **Rationalisation driven by technicians**: the desire for perfect plant overrode economic calculation. Capacity was built for demand that never came.
- **High wages as a crisis remedy**: the author calls the unions' theory "erroneous" and holds that cycle-sensitive real wages would have limited unemployment.
- **Rigid cartel prices**: they blocked the fall in costs that manufacturing and exports needed.
- **Blaming reparations alone**: "too one-sided".
- **Expecting falling prices to end a boom**: in Germany prices stayed high in 1928, and the high prices themselves hurt the expansion, because costs had not fallen as rationalisation had promised.
- **Fiscal laxity in the boom**: public spending rose sharply after 1924 and the tax burden shifted onto profits and capital.

## Worked Example
**The run of June and July 1931, step by step.**
1. Background: banks hold large foreign short-term deposits. Part of their lending is frozen in plant. Liquidity coefficients have fallen for two years.
2. September 1930: the election gains of the extreme parties start a withdrawal of foreign short-term credits. It pauses in early 1931. Shares rise from January to April.
3. Spring 1931: the collapse of the Credit-Anstalt in Vienna raises doubts abroad about German banks. Trouble at Karstadt, Nordstern and Nordwolle becomes known.
4. 5 June: an emergency decree with "draconian financial provisions" exposes the state of public finance.
5. Early June: the Reichsbank sells 50, 100 million marks and more of foreign exchange a day. On the morning of 12 June it sells 200 million. Next day 100 million.
6. The discount rate goes from 5 to 7 per cent: "only a temporary relief". The Reichsbank restricts credit, as it did in May 1929 and September 1930.
7. Hoover's declaration brings a short pause. The Reichsbank eases the restriction. Demand for foreign exchange returns within days.
8. Domestic capital flight joins foreign withdrawal. By early July the Reichsbank has lost about 1.5 milliards of gold and foreign exchange in a few weeks. Commercial banks have paid out about 500 million more.
9. Depositors learn that the Danat is heavily exposed to Nordwolle (40 million against capital of 60 million and reserves of 40 million). Its deposits run off. It closes on 13 July 1931.
10. The author's reading in his Conclusions: objective capital shortage, made far worse by a general loss of confidence in government, in the Weimar system and in the industrialists themselves.

## Key Takeaways
1. The stabilisation crisis was drawn out over more than two years because credit and public support repeatedly softened it.
2. The 1926 recovery began in the depression, from liquidity and falling interest, before any exogenous stimulus.
3. The boom of 1927-28 was real: the production index rose from 76.6 in 1925 to 100 in 1928. But it rested on foreign capital, increasingly short-term.
4. Warning signs appeared by mid-1927: rising rates, falling shares, a swelling Reichsbank portfolio, falling reserves, fewer security issues, more short-notice deposits, weaker building.
5. Steel consumption peaked in 1927, the sensitive price index in January 1928, raw-material imports in early 1928. Total production peaked only in 1929, helped by foreign demand.
6. Foreign capital supply was "completely elastic" in 1925-27. It turned in 1928 when New York drew funds, and dried up as confidence fell.
7. The policy of deflation begun with the decree of 8 December 1931 (cuts to wages, prices, rents, tariffs and interest) "did not have the success anticipated", the author says, "for entirely peculiar reasons" which he does not state.
8. The author leaves the agricultural depression outside his account, noting it as an important omitted aspect.

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Fiscal cause of depreciation, against the balance-of-payments theory (Ch 2)
- The "German" theory: passive balance and reparations, then a falling exchange, then rising prices, then note issue. Inflation is the result.
- The "English" theory, which the author holds: budget deficit, then note issue, then rising prices, then a falling exchange.
- Use the monthly rise in floating debt as the deficit gauge. Test causality on stable-exchange spells (1920-21: deficit 60 per cent of spending with a stable mark) and on collapse spells (late 1919: deficit did not widen).
- Taxes covered about 15 per cent of Reich spending from 1914 to October 1923. Treaty costs explain at most a third of the 1920-23 deficit.
- Qualification: in the last phase the chain became circular. See framework 3.

### 2. The limit theorem (Ch 2, Ch 2a)
- A fall caused only by import demand, capital flight, a transfer or speculation "cannot go beyond a certain limit" unless money and credit grow.
- Formal base: exchange-rate index = ratio of purchasing powers (export prices) times index of the barter terms of trade.
- A fall in internal value passes to the exchange in full. A balance-of-payments shock is damped, more so when trade is large and demand elastic.
- Purchasing Power Parity is a long-run force and a poor short-run description.

### 3. The vicious circle and how it breaks (Ch 2, Ch 9, Ch 11)
- Exchange falls, home prices follow at once, money looks scarce, the central bank supplies notes, the exchange falls again.
- Prices follow a depreciation fast and an appreciation slowly. So a speculative spike that is ratified by new notes becomes permanent.
- The circle is not a law. It broke when the Reichsbank stopped discounting Treasury bills on 16 November 1923.
- Prefer pricing credit to rationing it. The discount rate stood at 5 per cent until July 1922 and 18 per cent in April 1923 while the gold mark went from 160 to 7,100 paper marks in nine months.

### 4. Internal against external value (Ch 3)
- Coefficient of divergence D = actual exchange rate of the gold mark over the price parity. Equally: world gold prices over home gold prices.
- Path in Germany: about 1 in wartime, 3.25 at the peak (February 1920), 1.5 to 2.4 in 1921-22, then to 1 and below in autumn 1923.
- Early on, a rigid home price system pulled the exchange back. Later the exchange led and prices followed with a lag. At the end prices priced in future depreciation and overshot world levels.
- Sources of price inertia: bread and coal price control, rent control, cheap rail rates, hoarded notes, pricing at historical cost.
- D above 1 means an export premium, a tax base shrinking in gold, and foreigners buying assets cheaply.

### 5. Real balances, velocity and the "sophism" (Ch 4)
- In a late inflation prices outrun money, so the real value of the note issue collapses (about 6 milliard gold marks pre-war; under 100 million at times in August 1923).
- Officials read this as "no inflation". The author calls that a sophism. Low real balances are the result of flight.
- Causal order, after Marshall: people cut the share of income they hold as money, so prices rise and money circulates faster.
- Three stages: depreciation proportional to issues; then issues plus velocity, dishoarding and returning foreign-held marks; then displacement of the mark by foreign exchange and emergency money ("Gresham's law reversed").
- Velocity index = price index over money index. It falls back when issues stop.

### 6. Forced saving and misdirected production (Ch 5, Ch 10)
- Rising prices take real income from wage earners and fixed incomes and hand it to entrepreneurs. Output and employment rise for a time.
- The gain goes into instrumental goods and "substance". Consumption industries shrink.
- The source dries up when wages are indexed. From mid-1922 depreciation no longer lowered unemployment.
- Seven output-limiting effects grow with the inflation: swollen trade and banking, speculation, unproductive labour in firms, lower labour intensity, suspended selection of firms (bankruptcies near zero), and impossible cost calculation.

### 7. Phase model of trade effects (Ch 6)
- Home gold prices above world: imports favoured. Exchange ahead of home prices: "exchange dumping". Home prices overshoot: classical dumping.
- An industrial exporter must import raw materials first, so imports rise when the currency falls.
- German exports never neared 1913 volumes. The "invasion" was a myth. The terms of trade turned against Germany.
- Do not trust trade values recorded in a collapsing currency.

### 8. Shares are governed by yield, even in inflation (Ch 7)
- Gold share index, 1913 = 100: 8.47 (February 1920), about 15 to 20 in 1920-21, 2.72 (October 1922), 39.36 (November 1923).
- Shares tracked the dollar when money was abundant and foreign exchange was open. They collapsed in 1922 when money was scarce, dividends were near zero and flight went to foreign exchange.
- Houses (real assets, no yield) were worthless. Indexed bonds (paper claims, real yield) held value.

### 9. Redistribution (Ch 8)
- Winners: industrial owners, large borrowers, farmers with mortgages, speculators, foreign buyers. Losers: holders of bonds, mortgages, deposits and insurance, house owners, small shareholders, professions, officials, wage earners.
- Real wages fell while employment rose until mid-1922. In 1923 real wages swung between about 48 and 86 per cent of 1913 from month to month.
- Debtors are fewer than creditors, so inflation concentrates wealth.
- Revaluation in 1924-25 restored 25 per cent of mortgages, 15 per cent of debentures and 2.5 per cent of public loans.

### 10. Why sudden stabilisation worked (Ch 9)
- Not by contraction: legal circulation rose many times after November 1923.
- Pre-conditions made by extreme inflation: tiny real money stock, substitute monies in use, velocity at a peak, floating debt and foreign-held marks wiped out.
- Decisive acts: end of central bank credit to the State, a capped rentenmark issue (2,400 million) that the Rentenbank defended, and a balanced budget within two months.
- "It became easier to redeem or convert paper money the more rapidly the paper inflation proceeded."
- Stability was nearly lost through easy credit in early 1924 and secured by the credit cap of 7 April 1924.

### 11. The stabilisation crisis (Ch 10, Ch 12)
- Essence: shortage of working capital, shown in very high interest. Cause: "conversion of circulating capital into fixed capital" during the inflation.
- At stabilisation wages, rents, taxes and interest become effective. Demand shifts from instrumental goods to consumption goods.
- Heavy industry and the inflation conglomerates fail. Consumer trades prosper. Foreign loans rebuild the subsistence fund. Rationalisation follows.
- Stabilisation is the prerequisite of reconstruction, not its result.

### 12. The quantity-limitation principle (Ch 11)
- With paper money one equation of the price system is missing. A limit on quantity supplies it.
- Velocity and business-created money matter, but both revert when issues stop.
- Net verdict on inflation: some real capital created, much of it useless, at great social cost.

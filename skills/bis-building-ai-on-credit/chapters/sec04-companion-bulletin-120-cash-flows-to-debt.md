# Section 4 (Companion): Financing the AI Boom: From Cash Flows to Debt
Source: BIS Bulletin No 120, Iñaki Aldasoro, Sebastian Doerr and Daniel Rees, 7 January 2026. Six pages, four graphs. All figures are for the United States unless stated.

## Core Idea
AI investment needs outgrew operating cash flows, so firms moved to debt, with private credit growing fastest. Macro and financial stability risks "appear moderate". The warning sign is that private credit lenders price AI loans like any other loan while equity prices imply outsized returns.

## Frameworks Introduced

### What counts as AI-related investment (pp 1-2)
1. Data centres: construction plus IT and electrical equipment.
2. IT manufacturing facilities that make the chips and hardware.
3. Broader IT investment, when AI prompts firms to upgrade hardware or buy software.
- Measurement rule (Graph 1 footnote, footnote 2): data centre equipment investment is estimated as three times data centre construction investment. The building is about one quarter of the cost and IT equipment three quarters (Noffsinger et al (2025)).

### Why the financing moved from cash flow to debt (p 3)
1. AI firms historically ran with much less debt than other firms and funded investment from operating cash flow.
2. Capex rose in absolute terms and as a share of revenue.
3. Free cash flow "recently lagged capital expenditures in absolute amounts".
4. Equity is "neither timely nor cost-effective": valuations are volatile and concentrated, issuance windows are narrow, and new stock is costly and dilutive for long-dated, asset-heavy projects.
5. Debt spreads the cost over time and matches the long life of data centre assets.
6. Construction, power availability and tenant concentration risks can put deals outside traditional bank and bond financing. Private credit fills the gap with bespoke terms.

### The debt-equity pricing gap test (p 5)
- Observation: spreads on private credit loans to AI firms are close to those on loans to other firms. The difference stays insignificant after controls for loan size, maturity, security, borrower location and age (footnote 6).
- Inference: if spreads reflect risk, lenders judge AI loans to be as risky as the average private credit loan.
- Contrast: AI equity valuations "imply outsized future returns".
- Conclusion: "either lenders may be underestimating the risks of AI investments (just as their exposures are growing significantly) or equity markets may be overestimating the future cash flows AI could generate".
- How to use it: compare the risk premium in the debt of a sector with the growth premium in its equity. When they disagree, one market is wrong.

### The investment boom comparison (pp 5-6, Graph 4)
- Size: the AI boom at about 1% of GDP is similar to the US shale boom of the mid-2010s and half the rise in IT investment in the 1990s dot-com boom. The commercial property boom in Japan (1980s) and the mining boom in Australia (2010s) were over five times as large relative to GDP.
- Aftermath: the end of past investment booms came with a GDP growth slowdown "of more than 1 pp on average". The largest contraction followed the US dot-com boom, although that boom was small relative to GDP.
- Medium term: "little evidence" that investment booms raise GDP growth over the following five years, even technology-driven ones.
- Amplifier: if the decline comes with a significant stock market correction, spillovers could be larger than past booms suggest. Investors favoured US equities for AI exposure, and "hidden leverage may lead to credit market spillovers".
- Booms in the sample: Australia 2005-12 (mining), Canada 2004-12 (mineral exploration), Japan 1984-91 (non-residential), US 1974-81 (oil), US 1994-2000 (tech), US 2003-14 (oil), US AI 2020s.

## Key Concepts
- **AI firms (Graph 2)**: Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle.
- **AI firms (Graph 3)**: firms in the PitchBook verticals "Artificial Intelligence", "Big Data" and "Cloud Tech". This is a wider group than the five hyperscalers.
- **Private credit**: non-bank credit from specialised funds, negotiated directly, held to maturity, with bespoke covenants.
- **Closed-end structure**: most private credit funds lock in capital for about four to eight years, which the authors say mitigates liquidity and maturity transformation risk.
- **Free cash flow**: operating cash flow minus capex.
- **Hidden leverage**: financing structures that "might mask leverage by moving it off the balance sheet". "Yet leverage does not disappear by being out of sight."
- **IT-producing versus IT-using firms**: the dot-com boom was driven by firms using IT. The current boom is driven by firms that produce it.

## Mental Models
- Use the capex-to-free-cash-flow crossover as the date when a cash-rich sector becomes a credit story.
- Think of similar loan terms as a statement by lenders. Same spread, same maturity, same security share means lenders see average risk.
- Size the boom against GDP before judging macro risk. Then recall that the dot-com bust was the worst aftermath despite its small size.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating low historical leverage as safety**: the starting point was low, but leverage is rising and some of it is off the balance sheet.
- **Reading growth contribution at face value**: imported equipment lowers the net contribution.
- **Assuming a technology boom raises later growth**: Graph 4.C shows little evidence of it.
- **Treating average fund exposure as the whole risk**: the average is about 5%, but one fund in five lends to the sector and loans are almost twice the usual size.

## Worked Example: the private credit extrapolation
1. Start with outstanding private credit to AI-related firms: over $200 billion.
2. Take projected AI investment growth of 50% to 300% from the Graph 1.C scenarios.
3. Result in the text: "around $300-600 billion by 2030".
4. Status: an authors' extrapolation. The source does not show the arithmetic.
5. Check: scaling $200 billion by 50% gives $300 billion, which matches the low end. Scaling by 300% gives $800 billion, which does not match the high end. So the top of the range rests on a step the Bulletin does not state. Cite the range as the authors' and do not rebuild it.

## Key Takeaways
1. By mid-2025 AI-related structures and equipment were about 1% of US GDP and total IT investment about 5%.
2. Free cash flow fell behind capex, so debt took over.
3. Private credit to AI-related firms passed $200 billion and almost 8% of the market.
4. AI loans carry the same spread as other private credit loans (6.2 against 6.1 pp).
5. That spread and AI equity valuations cannot both be right.
6. The authors rate the risks as "moderate" as of January 2026.

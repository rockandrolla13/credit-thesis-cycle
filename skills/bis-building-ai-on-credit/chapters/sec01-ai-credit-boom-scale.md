# Section 1: Key Takeaways and the Scale of the AI Credit Boom
Source: BIS Bulletin No 140, pp 1-2 (key takeaways, introduction, Graph 1). Dated 27 October 2026.

## Core Idea
AI capital expenditure is large, growing fast, and "increasingly built on credit". The debt is long-dated, and a rising share sits in off-balance sheet vehicles. Whether this ends well depends on productivity gains that nobody can observe yet.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The two questions of the Bulletin** (p 2):
  1. How do the financing structures allocate credit risk inside the AI ecosystem, and "who bears losses if returns fall short"? (Section 2)
  2. What spillovers does the borrowing create for borrowers with no link to AI, through long-term interest rates? (Section 3)
- **Good boom or bad boom test** (p 1, citing Gorton and Ordoñez (2020)):
  - Financial stability risk depends on two conditions. First, the promised productivity gains materialise. Second, the profits "accrue to those making the initial investments".
  - Limit of the test: the two types "are difficult to distinguish in real time". Credit evolves similarly, on average, in good and bad booms until the productivity gains arrive or fail to arrive.
  - How to use it: do not read the pace of credit growth alone as a verdict. Use it to size the exposure, then map where the risk sits.
- **Three channels of AI debt** (p 1):
  1. On-balance sheet bonds from the hyperscalers.
  2. Bonds and asset-backed securities issued by off-balance sheet vehicles.
  3. Commitments behind those vehicles: leases and guarantees that are not recorded as debt.

## Key Concepts
- **Hyperscalers**: large US firms that support the capacity build-out. The Graph 1 sample is Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle.
- **Neoclouds**: "a smaller set of newer cloud providers".
- **AI labs**: the third group that supports the build-out. The text does not define them further.
- **General purpose technology**: the authors' label for AI. Most gains arrive only once the technology is embedded in the capital stock and production processes.
- **Bad boom**: a credit boom that ended in a systemic banking crisis (Graph 1.C, footnote 4).

## Graph 1.C: the boom comparison (chart reading, approximate)
- Method: follow Gorton and Ordoñez (2020) for the dating and classing of past booms. The sample covers 37 countries. Set total credit to the private non-financial sector to 100 at the start of each boom. Set each AI firm's total debt to 100 in Q3 2025. Plot the median across Alphabet, Amazon, CoreWeave, Meta, Microsoft, NVIDIA and Oracle.
- Reading from the chart: past booms, good and bad, reach roughly 190 to 210 by year 5. The AI median reaches roughly 220 in under one year. The text says only that AI debt issuance is "rapid" even against past booms.
- Caution: the comparison sets country-level credit against firm-level debt. The authors state the change of normalisation. They do not claim the AI boom is a bad boom.

## Mental Models
- Think of the AI build-out as a credit boom whose type is still unknown. The data that would settle it (productivity, and who captures the profit) arrive after the debt.
- Use two lenses on the same debt: a credit-risk lens (who loses) and a duration-supply lens (who must absorb long bonds).

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling the boom good or bad from credit growth**: the source says credit paths look alike on average until the outcome is known.
- **Counting only reported debt**: the commitments behind the vehicles are also large, and Section 2 shows they exceed reported debt.

## Worked Example: sizing the issuance against benchmarks
1. Take hyperscaler gross bond issuance in 2026 to September: $222 billion.
2. Note that most of it is long-dated.
3. Compare it with long-term Treasury issuance: more than 10%.
4. Add the vehicle-linked placements: $75 to $100 billion.
5. Add the commitments that are not debt: $1.2 trillion of leases, $820 billion not yet started.
6. Conclusion in the text: issuance is rapid "even benchmarked against past credit booms".

## Key Takeaways
1. Hyperscaler capex is expected to exceed $800 billion by end-2026.
2. A rising share is financed with long-term debt and "complex off-balance sheet arrangements".
3. Risk depends on productivity gains and on who captures them. It is "too early to tell".
4. The authors report that questions emerged "in recent weeks" on whether data centre income can repay all debts, "especially those linked to neoclouds".
5. Until the outcome is known, the useful work is to map where the risks lie.

# Section 5 (Companion): Financing the AI Infrastructure Boom: On- and Off-Balance Sheet Borrowing
Source: box in the BIS Quarterly Review, March 2026, published 16 March 2026. Bulletin 140 cites it as Eren, Krohn and Todorov (2026). The box is short: five paragraphs and one graph.

## Core Idea
Hyperscalers fund a rising share of capex through borrowing. Bonds are the main channel. Off-balance sheet vehicles are the second channel, and the box calls them "shadow borrowing": obligations "economically akin to debt" that sit largely outside corporate balance sheets.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The common off-balance sheet structure
1. A dedicated vehicle, often a joint venture or special purpose entity, acquires or develops the data centre assets.
2. A consortium of sponsors puts in the equity.
3. The vehicle raises debt through private placements.
4. The hyperscaler "typically holds a minority stake".
5. The hyperscaler commits to long-term operating leases or capacity offtake agreements. It "may provide various guarantees".
6. Lease cash flows service the debt.
7. Private credit funds and other institutional investors hold the debt. It sometimes has investment grade features, supported by asset backing and guarantees from investment-grade hyperscalers.
- Economic effect: the structure "substitutes upfront capex with multi-year operating expenses while keeping most of the associated debt off the hyperscaler's balance sheet".

### Three shock transmission channels
Banks support the vehicles with funding lines. The box names three possible channels:
1. Refinancing pressures at the vehicle level.
2. Procyclical shifts in private credit appetite.
3. The activation of guarantees.

## Key Concepts
- **AI hyperscalers (AIHs)**: Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle.
- **Shadow borrowing**: the box's term for the off-balance sheet obligations.
- **Capacity offtake agreement**: a long-term commitment "to purchase or reserve power and compute capacity".
- **Private placement**: debt sold directly to a small group of institutional investors.

## Mental Models
- Think of the lease as debt service under another name. The hyperscaler pays operating expense. The vehicle pays the bondholder.
- Use the CDS split by rating as an early gauge. The lower-rated hyperscalers repriced first.
- Read the CDS rise as two things at once. The box says it reflects "both the volume of supply and uncertainties around the projects' payoffs".

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating operating expense as free of leverage**: the box classes these obligations as economically akin to debt.
- **Treating the sector as one credit**: CDS moved very differently by rating bucket.

## Worked Example: the three channels placed on the structure
The box names the channels and gives no numbers and no sequence. This places each channel on the structure it describes.
1. **Procyclical private credit appetite**: the debt holders are private credit funds and other institutional investors. Their appetite sets whether the vehicle can place debt.
2. **Refinancing pressure at the vehicle**: the vehicle's debt is privately placed and serviced by lease cash flows. Banks support the vehicle with funding lines.
3. **Activation of guarantees**: the hyperscaler "may provide various guarantees". An activated guarantee turns an off-balance sheet obligation into a claim on the hyperscaler.
4. **Links**: banks, private credit vehicles and insurers all connect to the hyperscaler through the same vehicle.

## Key Takeaways
1. Bond markets were the primary financing source in 2025, above $100 billion gross.
2. Most of the new debt matures after 2035, by the chart.
3. CDS spreads of lower-rated hyperscalers rose well above those of higher-rated ones by Q1 2026.
4. Off-balance sheet vehicles keep most of the debt off the hyperscaler's balance sheet.
5. The structures tie hyperscalers to private credit funds, insurers and banks.

# Chapter 1: Abstract and Introduction — The Great Moderation Consensus and Why It Broke

*Source sections: title block, Abstract, Keywords, "Introduction" (including Table 1). Published online 18 March 2010; draft dated January 28, 2010.*

## Core Idea
Before 2007, the consensus credited financial innovation and well-timed monetary policy for the "Great Moderation". After the crisis, the same two factors are the commonly cited causes of it. The paper therefore reopens two questions: how monetary policy should treat asset price booms, and how financial innovation should be regulated.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The "Greenspan doctrine" (asymmetric response to asset price booms)**: the pre-crisis consensus for optimal monetary policy. Central banks do nothing during the asset price boom, then cut interest rates during the bust to reduce the impact on the real economy.
  - When to use: as the baseline policy reaction function for the roughly 20 years after the early-1980s disinflation.
  - How: (1) during the boom, set the Federal funds rate for maximum sustainable growth and price stability only; (2) ignore the asset price level; (3) after the bust, cut rates.
  - Why it seemed to work: the listed episodes of financial instability "had little impact on the real economy", and the variance of output growth fell.
  - Failure mode: the authors say the asymmetric approach is "now being reconsidered, particularly for financial sector financed asset price booms such as in real estate". The doctrine's record came from booms that did not run through financial-sector balance sheets to the same degree.
- **The Great Moderation explanation (Bernanke 2004)**: two causal factors are cited for the calmer business cycle, namely financial innovation and appropriate monetary policy.
  - Financial innovation channel: it was believed to allocate capital and risk more efficiently.
  - Monetary policy channel: appropriately timed rate cuts were thought to offset financial shocks, giving long expansions and mild recessions.
  - Status after 2007: the authors say these explanations "have lost, at least, some force".
- **Three-part plan of the paper**: (1) general characteristics and theories of financial crises (ch02); (2) review of the 2007-09 crisis (ch03); (3) policies to reduce crisis frequency and real-economy impact (ch04).

## Key Concepts
- **Great Moderation**: the period after the early-1980s disinflation with two long expansions, two mild recessions, and lower volatility of inflation and real GDP growth.
- **Great Recession / "2nd Great Contraction"**: the authors' two labels for the downturn that followed the 2007-08 financial crisis.
- **Greenspan doctrine**: asymmetric monetary response, passive in the boom and easing in the bust.
- **Financial innovation**: development and marketing of new financial instruments, which together with deregulation doubled the financial sector's share of GDP.
- **Financial-sector-financed asset price boom**: a boom funded by financial institutions (real estate is the example); the category the authors single out for a changed policy response.
- **Value added of financial sector as a share of GDP**: the size measure in Table 1.

## Mental Models
- Use "low realized macro volatility" as a description, not as evidence that the financial system is safe. In Table 1 the lowest GDP-growth volatility (2000-2008) coincides with the largest financial sector share.
- Think of the Greenspan doctrine as a put written by the central bank: no action on the way up, rate cuts on the way down. The paper links this later to moral hazard (ch04).
- Use "who financed the boom" as the first sorting question. The authors distinguish booms in general from those financed by the financial sector.
- Treat a consensus explanation as a hypothesis with a date on it. The same two factors moved from "cause of stability" to "cause of crisis" within a few years.

## Anti-patterns
- **Inferring policy success from a run of harmless shocks**: the 1987 and 2000 stock crashes, the S&L crisis, Mexico 1994, Asia 1997, the 1998 "LCTM" bankruptcy (spelled this way in the source) and the 1998 Russian default had little real impact, and that record supported a doctrine that later failed.
- **Assuming a bigger financial sector means faster growth**: Table 1 shows the sector's share doubling while GDP growth did not rise.
- **Monetary policy that focuses solely on inflation and the output gap**: the abstract says the crisis called this into question.

## Worked Example

Reading Table 1 the way the authors do.

1. Financial sector share: 4.0% of GDP in the 1960s, 8.4% in 2000-2008. The text says the sector "doubled in size as measured by valued added to GDP". The table supports that over the full span (the text attributes the doubling to the 20-year post-disinflation period, while the table's doubling runs from the 1960s).
2. Growth: 4.5% in the 1960s, 2.3% in 2000-2008. The authors' statement is that the U.S. economy "did not grow faster". The table shows growth fell.
3. Volatility: standard deviation of real GDP growth went from 2.5% in the 1970s and 1980s to 1.5% in the 1990s and 1.1% in 2000-2008.
4. Authors' reading: output variance fell "even in the face of episodic financial instability", which "perhaps was offset by appropriately timed cuts in the Federal funds rate".
5. Conclusion to carry forward: a larger financial sector coincided with lower measured volatility and no growth gain, right up to the crisis. Low volatility did not signal low crisis risk.

## Key Takeaways
1. The pre-crisis policy rule was asymmetric: ignore the boom, cushion the bust.
2. The two factors credited for stability, financial innovation and monetary policy, became the two commonly cited causes of the crisis.
3. The financial sector's GDP share roughly doubled (4.0% to 8.4%) with no rise in GDP growth.
4. A string of contained financial shocks is weak evidence that the next one will be contained.
5. The policy rethink targets financial-sector-financed booms, with real estate as the named case.

# Chapter 2: General Characteristics of Financial Crises

*Source section: "General Characteristics of Financial Crises".*

## Core Idea
A financial crisis has three parts: initial causal factors, an asset price bubble and bust, and a propagation mechanism from finance to the real economy. Financial liberalization plus credit expansion raises asset prices, and a crisis follows only if a bubble-and-bust cycle occurs. The real damage scales with how much the financial sector financed the bubble.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three-part anatomy of a financial crisis** (the authors' organizing scheme):
  1. Initial causal factors setting up.
  2. An asset price bubble and bust, with implications for financial markets.
  3. A propagation mechanism from the financial to the real economy.
  - When to use: to place any episode in a stage before reasoning about what comes next.
- **Two key initial causal factors**: financial liberalization and credit expansion.
  - Evidence cited: Bordo et al. (2001), Reinhart and Rogoff, Kaminsky and Reinhart (1999). See Reference Tables.
  - Condition: these raise asset prices at first, and "may eventually cause a financial crisis provided an asset price bubble and bust cycle occurs". The bubble can be in stocks, real estate, or both.
- **Minsky's credit cycle model of five stages** (Minsky 1986): displacement, boom, euphoria, profit taking, panic.
  - Displacement can come from financial liberalization and credit expansion.
  - The asset price bubble is Minsky's "euphoria".
  - The pop is Minsky's "panic stage".
  - The source names "profit taking" but does not describe it further.
- **Asset price bubble (the authors' definition)**: a bubble occurs when a rise in asset prices causes a rise in expected future asset prices. Higher expected prices raise current demand, which raises prices and demand again, in a spiral.
  - Sustaining condition: the boom lasts "so long as new buyers are available who have access to credit and who also believe asset prices will continue to rise". Both conditions are needed: credit access and belief.
  - End condition: "At some point all so inclined buyers have brought into the market, asset prices peak and the asset price bubble pops."
- **Epidemic of beliefs** (Akerlof and Shiller 2009): during a bubble, the belief that prices will keep rising spreads like an epidemic from one investor to another.
- **Leverage cycle** (Geanakoplos 2009): links asset prices, credit availability and optimistic expectations.
  - Up-leg: asset prices rise, so collateral values rise, so banks lend more willingly, so demand for assets rises (most of all from the most optimistic borrowers), so prices rise again.
  - Down-leg: "scary news" causes sudden selling. Prices fall. Leveraged buyers lose wealth, which forces sales and a further downward spiral. Banks tighten lending standards because of "increased uncertainty and disagreement about the future".
  - Why it works: collateral value is the hinge in both directions, so the same loop that inflates prices deflates them.
- **Collateral amplification** (Kiyotaki and Moore 1997): small shocks can have large effects through collateral. A shock lowers asset prices, which lowers collateral value, which reduces possible borrowing, which lowers asset prices again.
- **Propagation through financial-sector involvement**: the pass-through of a bust to the real economy "depends on the involvement of the financial sector in financing the bubble".
  - How: more involvement means larger hits to financial institution balance sheets. Lenders to banks become uncertain about bank solvency. Depositors and other lenders withdraw funds or refuse to roll over debt. Institutions must sell assets. The result is a downward price spiral, a freeze in financial markets, or both. The number of secure institutions falls, either in fact or in perception because of asymmetric information.
- **Financial accelerator** (Bernanke and Gertler 1989): credit market conditions can amplify and propagate shocks.
  - Mechanism as stated in the source: because of differences in the cost of borrowing from banks versus the bond market, shocks are amplified if borrowers lose bank financing, or if a negative shock to borrowers' wealth costs them access to bank credit.
  - Implication: financial disintermediation in a crisis can have a large negative impact on the real economy.

## Key Concepts
- **Financial liberalization**: deregulation of finance, one of the two initial causal factors.
- **Credit expansion**: the second initial causal factor.
- **Displacement**: first Minsky stage, which the authors say could occur through liberalization and credit expansion.
- **Euphoria**: Minsky stage the authors equate with the asset price bubble.
- **"Scary news"**: Geanakoplos's trigger for sudden selling at the top of a leverage cycle.
- **Forced sales**: selling by leveraged buyers after wealth losses, or by institutions facing withdrawals.
- **Contagion**: spread of failure across institutions, which interventions aim to prevent.
- **Systemic financial institutions**: the institutions that central bank and government interventions keep from bankruptcy.
- **Dependence on external finance**: sector characteristic that predicts relatively worse performance in banking crises.
- **Financial disintermediation**: loss of bank intermediation, the channel to the real economy.

## Mental Models
- Use the three-part anatomy as a stage classifier: set-up, bubble and bust, propagation.
- Think of collateral as a two-way amplifier. Any story about rising prices and easier lending implies the reverse loop.
- Use "are there new buyers with credit and belief?" as the bubble's sustainability test.
- Use "how much did the financial sector finance it?" to size the real-economy impact of a bust.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating liberalization plus credit expansion as sufficient for a crisis**: the source makes the crisis conditional on a bubble-and-bust cycle occurring.
- **Assuming intervention reliably stops contagion**: after Lehman, the authors say the process "did not work as it was expected by the academic literature".
- **Ignoring perceived solvency**: asymmetric information means the perceived number of secure banks can fall even when the actual number does not.

## Worked Example

The Kaminsky and Reinhart (1999) crisis sequence, as the authors summarize it.

1. Set-up: financial liberalization and significant credit expansion.
2. Boom: stock prices rise on average "about 40% per year above that occurring in normal times". Real estate and other asset prices also rise significantly.
3. Bust: "Eventually the stock and real estate markets collapse."
4. Banking crisis: in many cases banks and other intermediaries were overexposed to equity and real estate. "About a year later on average a banking crisis ensues."
5. Real economy: output falls significantly, with a recession lasting "about a year and a half on average".

Applied as a template: an asset market collapse with heavy bank exposure implies a banking crisis roughly one year later, and then a recession of roughly eighteen months. These are sample averages from 20 countries, not bounds.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify an episode by stage: set-up, bubble and bust, or propagation.
2. Watch for liberalization plus credit expansion as the set-up, and for stocks or real estate as the bubble asset.
3. A bubble needs a continuing supply of new buyers with credit and belief. It ends when that supply runs out.
4. Collateral links prices and credit in both directions, so small shocks can become large.
5. Real-economy damage depends on financial-sector involvement in financing the bubble.
6. The average sequence is: market collapse, banking crisis about a year later, recession of about a year and a half.
7. Sectors dependent on external finance, developing countries, and countries with less access to foreign finance are hit harder by bank distress.

# Chapter 4: Economic Policies to Prevent Financial Instabilities, and Conclusions

*Source sections: "Economic Policies to Prevent Financial Instabilities", "Conclusions", "References". This chapter is thin on data: the source gives arguments and policy options here, with no tables or numbers.*

## Core Idea
Unregulated financial markets lead to crises, and the government's commitment to stabilize the economy forces bailouts, which creates moral hazard. The authors judge that keeping bailouts while increasing regulation is the practical answer. The emerging regime adds micro-prudential regulation and macro-prudential monitoring to the standard Taylor-rule goals.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The bailout moral hazard chain**
  1. Unregulated financial markets lead to financial crises.
  2. The post-WWII "social compact" makes government responsible for dampening the business cycle.
  3. So in a crisis the government bails out the parts of the financial sector that are "too big to fail or too interconnected to fail".
  4. The bailout creates the expectation that government will cover the bad outcome of any position taken by such an institution.
  5. Managers then have an incentive to take risks "where bad outcomes are borne by tax payers".
  - Failure mode if untreated: "potentially greater future financial crises and bailouts".
- **Four possible solutions to the moral hazard problem**: see the matrix in Reference Tables. The authors call solution 3 practical and note that "even conservative economists" say financial regulation needs reform.
- **New monetary policy regime (the building consensus)**: combine (a) the standard goals of price stability and maximum sustainable growth "as laid out in the Taylor rule" with (b) micro-prudential regulation and (c) macro-prudential monitoring.
- **Micro-prudential regulation, two main parts**
  1. Make the leverage of financial institutions counter-cyclical instead of pro-cyclical.
  2. Change financial market executive compensation so managers internalize the negative externality of their risk taking. Managers must "lose a substantial portion of their income when the trades they make turn out ex post to be bad".
  - Extension: leverage of non-financial institutions needs regulation as well. The example given is the size of real estate down payments.
- **Macro-prudential monitoring**: regulators such as the Fed monitor the trades of financial institutions.
  - Rule: "Many institutions making the same trade, i.e. the same bet, results in high systemic risk to all if the trade goes bad."
  - Link to micro-prudential action: when institutions take a similar position on similar assumptions (the example is all betting that real estate prices keep rising), regulators could require firms to hold more capital, which means reducing leverage.
  - Why it works: it treats crowding as the source of systemic risk, and raises the cost of the crowded position before the bust.
- **Political economy constraint**: this regulation is likely to reduce the profitability, size and political influence of the financial sector. It "will continue to meet substantial resistance from members of the financial community".

## Key Concepts
- **Social compact (post-WWII)**: government responsibility for dampening the impact of the business cycle.
- **Too big to fail / too interconnected to fail**: the two criteria the authors give for bailout eligibility.
- **Moral hazard**: risk taking encouraged by the expectation that taxpayers bear bad outcomes.
- **Micro-prudential regulation**: institution-level rules on leverage and compensation.
- **Macro-prudential monitoring**: system-level watching of common positions across institutions.
- **Counter-cyclical leverage**: leverage limits that tighten in booms.
- **Negative externality of risk taking**: cost that managers' trades impose on others, which compensation reform should internalize.
- **Monetary overhang**: consequence the authors attach to repeated bailouts without regulation, alongside bigger government budget deficits.
- **Taylor rule**: named as the statement of the standard monetary goals.

## Mental Models
- Use "who bears the bad outcome?" to find moral hazard. If the answer is the taxpayer, expect more risk taking.
- Think of crowding as systemic risk. The same bet held by many institutions is dangerous to all of them, regardless of each one's own risk controls.
- Use regulatory response as the variable that sets the severity of the next crisis. The authors expect more crises and say severity "depends on how regulation is adjusted".
- Treat industry resistance as a forecastable drag on reform, since reform lowers the sector's profitability, size and influence.

## Anti-patterns
- **Bailouts without more regulation (solution 2)**: the authors expect "even bigger financial crises in the future", bigger budget deficits and monetary overhang, with increased uncertainty, higher inflation and/or prolonged slumps.
- **Assuming the state will stop stabilizing (solution 1)**: a return to 19th-century policy is "very unlikely to happen".
- **Regulating only financial institutions' leverage**: the crisis suggests non-financial leverage, such as down payment size, matters too.
- **Compensation that pays on trades regardless of ex post outcome**: it leaves the externality with others.

## Reference Tables

## Worked Example

The macro-prudential rule applied to the authors' own example.

1. Observation: many financial institutions hold a similar position, all "betting that real estate prices will continue to rise".
2. Diagnosis: the positions rest on similar assumptions. If the trade goes bad, the loss hits all of them at once, so systemic risk is high.
3. Regulatory response: require firms to hold more capital, which reduces leverage.
4. Complementary micro-prudential settings: counter-cyclical leverage rules for financial institutions, and regulation of real estate down payments for non-financial borrowers.
5. Expected friction: the measures reduce the financial sector's profitability, size and political influence, so the sector resists.

## Conclusions of the Paper
- The crisis upset the view that the Great Moderation was due to financial innovations and appropriate monetary policy.
- Many economists now think those two factors caused the financial crisis and the Great Recession.
- Monetary policy "may respond directly to financial asset price booms", and financial markets "may be more tightly regulated". Both are stated as possibilities.
- Getting the mix of monetary and financial regulation policy right is "critical if future financial crises are to be avoided".

## Key Takeaways
1. Bailouts of too-big or too-interconnected institutions create moral hazard that regulation must address.
2. Of four options, the authors find "bailouts plus more regulation" practical.
3. Micro-prudential regulation has two parts: counter-cyclical leverage and compensation that penalizes bad trades.
4. Macro-prudential monitoring looks for many institutions making the same bet.
5. Crowded positions on shared assumptions justify higher capital requirements.
6. Expect resistance from the financial sector, and expect more crises. Severity depends on the regulatory response.

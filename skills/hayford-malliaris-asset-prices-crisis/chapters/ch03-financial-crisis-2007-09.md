# Chapter 3: The Financial Crisis of 2007-09

*Source section: "The Financial Crisis of 2007-09".*

## Core Idea
The 2007-09 crisis fits the general anatomy: credit expansion, a housing price spiral, a bust, then stress in bank capital and funding markets. Its distinctive features were rating agency flaws, pro-cyclical leverage, and disintermediation. Standard monetary tools did not restore normal conditions, so the Fed turned to unconventional actions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Buiter's (2007) causes: shared and unique**
  - Shared with past crises (credit expansion): excessive global liquidity creation by key central banks, and a global saving glut.
  - The saving glut had two sources: entry of high-saving countries (notably China) into the global economy, and a global redistribution of wealth and income towards commodity exporters with high short-run propensities to save.
  - Unique to this crisis: (1) fundamental flaws in the rating agencies' business model; (2) pro-cyclical behavior of leverage in much of the financial system; (3) "privately rational but socially inefficient disintermediation".
- **Origin account (Adrian and Shin 2010, Brunnermeier 2009, Taylor 2008)**: the crisis began with low interest rate policies by the Federal Reserve and other central banks after the technology stock bubble collapsed in 2001. The global savings surplus of fast-growing Asian nations contributed. Together they produced "a dramatic increase in the demand for housing".
- **Housing price spiral**: rising house prices raised housing demand, which raised prices again. This is the ch02 bubble definition applied to housing.
- **Bank capital channel**: bank lending is tied to bank capital (net worth). Regulators require that loans do not exceed a certain multiple of capital. Losses on subprime exposure therefore threatened "a sharp contraction in credit and bank lending in a way that threatened a recession".
  - How to apply: estimate losses, reduce capital by that amount, and the lending ceiling falls by a multiple. The source gives no multiple.
- **Agency spread as an early indicator of risk taking**: the average difference between U.S. government agency securities (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and similar) and Treasuries of equivalent maturity.
  - Normal reading: agencies are "only very slightly more risky and less liquid" than Treasuries.
  - Stress reading: a widening is a "flight to quality", in which investors shun everything except Treasuries. Thresholds are in Reference Tables.
- **Reinhart-Rogoff benchmark**: compare an episode's pre-crisis housing run-up with the eighteen bank-centered post-war financial crises. The U.S. run-up exceeded those in the sample. The authors' phrase: the United States "looks like the archetypical crisis country, only more so".
- **Conventional then unconventional policy sequence**: conventional tools first (discount rate cost and term, then Federal funds target cuts). When there was "no return to normalcy", the Fed moved to actions "not in the current textbook descriptions of monetary policy".

## Key Concepts
- **Global saving glut**: excess saving from high-saving entrants and commodity exporters.
- **Pro-cyclical leverage**: leverage that rises in booms and falls in busts across the financial system.
- **Securitized subprime mortgages**: the assets whose prices fell after house prices turned, transmitting stress worldwide.
- **Bank capital (net worth)**: the base that caps bank lending by a regulatory multiple.
- **Agency spread**: agency yield minus Treasury yield at equal maturity.
- **Flight to quality**: shift into Treasuries only.
- **Primary (discount) lending premium**: spread of the discount rate above the Federal funds target.
- **Term Auction Facilities (TAF)**: facilities created as part of the less conventional actions.
- **High Grade Structured Credit Funds**: the two Bear Stearns funds that ran into difficulty in summer 2007.

## Mental Models
- Use a "shared versus unique" split for any new episode. Shared features tell you the template applies. Unique features tell you where the template will mislead.
- Think of bank capital as a hard ceiling on credit. Asset losses become credit contraction through the capital multiple.
- Use a near-riskless spread (agency minus Treasury) as a risk-appetite gauge. A move in something normally "only very slightly more risky" is informative because it should not move.
- Use the failure of conventional easing to normalize spreads as a signal that the problem is risk and credit shortage, not the policy rate level.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating the cause of the housing boom as settled**: Taylor (2008) and others blame easy Fed policy in 2004-2006. Bernanke (2010) disputes this. The authors say it "remains to date a topic of intense discussion".
- **Expecting rate cuts alone to fix a funding and risk problem**: discount rate changes and funds rate cuts were followed by worsening "risk and credit shortage" through late fall 2007 and early winter 2008.
- **Reading "this time is different" from the details**: the crisis "shares many of the characteristics of previous crises while some of the details differ".

## Worked Example

Timeline of the crisis as the authors lay it out, mapped to the ch02 anatomy.

| When | Event | Anatomy stage |
|---|---|---|
| After 2001 | Low interest rate policies by the Fed and other central banks after the technology bubble collapse; Asian savings surplus | Set-up (credit expansion) |
| 2004-2006 | House prices rise significantly in the U.S. and several other countries | Bubble |
| July 2006 | Bubble peak | Peak |
| Early 2007 | House prices start to fall; subprime mortgage meltdown starts; securitized subprime prices fall | Bust |
| Summer 2007 | Two Bear Stearns High Grade Structured Credit Funds in major difficulty; expected bank losses of "tens or even hundreds of billions of dollars" | Propagation to financial institutions |
| August 2007 | Agency spread above 40 bp; discount rate action on August 17 | Flight to quality begins |
| Mid-September 2007 | Federal funds target cuts begin | Conventional response |
| Late fall 2007 to early winter 2008 | Risk and credit shortage worsen; less conventional actions begin | Conventional tools insufficient |
| March 2008 | Agency spread above 90 bp | Intensified flight to quality |

Reading: the template of ch02 predicts the order. The spread data show the stress indicator moving in August 2007, months before the March 2008 extreme.

## Key Takeaways
1. Set-up was credit expansion: easy global monetary policy plus a global saving glut.
2. House prices rose strongly in 2004-2006, peaked in July 2006, and fell from early 2007.
3. Three features were specific to this crisis: rating agency business model flaws, pro-cyclical leverage, and privately rational but socially inefficient disintermediation.
4. The agency spread moved from 15-25 bp to above 40 bp in August 2007 and above 90 bp in March 2008.
5. Subprime losses threatened lending through the regulatory capital multiple.
6. Conventional easing did not restore normal conditions, which led to unconventional Fed actions.
7. Whether easy Fed policy caused the housing boom is disputed (Taylor versus Bernanke).

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Three-part anatomy of a financial crisis (ch02)
Use this to place any episode in a stage.
1. **Initial causal factors**: financial liberalization and credit expansion.
2. **Asset price bubble and bust**: in stocks, real estate, or both.
3. **Propagation mechanism**: from the financial to the real economy.

The set-up is not sufficient. Liberalization plus credit expansion raises asset prices and "may eventually cause a financial crisis provided an asset price bubble and bust cycle occurs".

### 2. Empirical sequence (Kaminsky and Reinhart 1999, ch02)
Sample: 20 countries, 5 industrial and 15 emerging.
- Liberalization and significant credit expansion precede most crises.
- Stock prices rise about 40% per year above normal times. Real estate also rises.
- Stock and real estate markets collapse.
- Where intermediaries were overexposed, a banking crisis follows about a year later on average.
- A recession follows, lasting about a year and a half on average.

Supporting findings: crises are more frequent after financial deregulation in the post-WWII period (Bordo et al. 2001). Systemic banking crises are usually preceded by credit booms and asset price bubbles (Reinhart and Rogoff).

### 3. How bubbles form and pop (ch02)
- **Bubble definition**: a price rise raises expected future prices, which raises current demand, which raises prices again.
- **Sustaining condition**: new buyers must be available who have access to credit and who believe prices will keep rising. Prefer checking both conditions over either alone.
- **Minsky's credit cycle, five stages**: displacement, boom, euphoria, profit taking, panic. Euphoria is the bubble. Panic is the pop.
- **Akerlof and Shiller**: the belief in rising prices spreads like an epidemic between investors.
- **Geanakoplos's leverage cycle**: prices up, collateral value up, banks lend more, demand up (most optimistic borrowers first), prices up. The reverse starts with "scary news": selling, falling wealth of leveraged buyers, forced sales, tighter lending standards.
- **Kiyotaki and Moore**: small shocks have large effects through collateral. Lower prices cut collateral value, which cuts borrowing, which cuts prices.

### 4. Propagation to the real economy (ch02)
Use financial-sector involvement in financing the bubble as the severity variable.
- More involvement means larger balance sheet hits.
- Lenders to banks doubt solvency. They withdraw funds or refuse to roll over debt.
- Institutions sell assets. Prices spiral down, markets freeze, or both.
- Lending to the real economy falls. Sectors dependent on external finance do worse. Effects are stronger in developing countries and countries with less access to foreign finance.
- **Financial accelerator (Bernanke and Gertler 1989)**: credit market conditions amplify and propagate shocks when borrowers lose bank financing, or when a wealth shock removes their access to bank credit.

### 5. The Greenspan doctrine and its failure (ch01)
- **Doctrine**: respond to asset price booms asymmetrically. Do nothing in the boom. Cut rates in the bust.
- **Why it looked right**: the 1987 and 2000 stock crashes, the S&L crisis, Mexico 1994, Asia 1997, the 1998 "LCTM" bankruptcy (source spelling) and the 1998 Russian default "had little impact on the real economy".
- **Why it is being reconsidered**: "particularly for financial sector financed asset price booms such as in real estate".
- **Table 1 lesson**: the financial sector's share of GDP rose from 4.0% (1960s) to 8.4% (2000-2008). Real GDP growth did not rise (4.5% to 2.3%). The standard deviation of growth fell to 1.1%. Do not read low volatility as low crisis risk.
- **Reversal**: financial innovation and monetary policy were credited for the Great Moderation (Bernanke 2004). They are now the two commonly cited causes of the crisis.

### 6. The 2007-09 crisis mapped to the anatomy (ch03)
- **Shared with past crises**: credit expansion through excessive global liquidity creation and a global saving glut (China, commodity exporters).
- **Unique (Buiter 2007)**: flaws in the rating agencies' business model, pro-cyclical leverage, and "privately rational but socially inefficient disintermediation".
- **Timeline**: low rates after the 2001 technology bubble collapse. House prices up significantly in 2004-2006. Peak in July 2006. Prices falling from early 2007. Securitized subprime prices fall. Bear Stearns fund trouble in summer 2007.
- **Bank capital channel**: loans are capped at a multiple of capital, so losses threatened a sharp credit contraction.
- **Benchmark**: the U.S. housing run-up exceeded those in Reinhart and Rogoff's eighteen bank-centered post-war crises.
- **Disputed cause**: Taylor blames easy Fed policy in 2004-2006. Bernanke (2010) disputes it.

### 7. Agency spread as an early stress indicator (ch03)
Agency securities minus Treasuries at equal maturity.
- Typical: 15-25 bp.
- From August 2007: more than 40 bp.
- March 2008: more than 90 bp. The authors call this a "flight to quality".

Conventional tools (discount rate action on August 17, 2007, funds target cuts from mid-September 2007) did not restore normal conditions. The Fed then cut the discount premium from 100 to 50 to 25 bp, extended terms from overnight to 30 then 90 days, and created Term Auction Facilities.

### 8. Moral hazard and the four solutions (ch04)
Chain: unregulated markets lead to crises. The post-WWII social compact makes government dampen the cycle. Government bails out "too big to fail or too interconnected to fail" institutions. Expected cover encourages risk taking at taxpayers' expense.

Four possible solutions:
1. Break the social compact. Very unlikely.
2. Bailouts without more regulation. Likely bigger crises, deficits and monetary overhang.
3. Bailouts plus more regulation. "Seems practical".
4. An extensive public policy agenda in the spirit of Minsky (Tymoigne and Wray 2009). Not detailed in the paper.

### 9. The proposed policy regime (ch04)
Combine Taylor-rule goals (price stability, maximum sustainable growth) with:
- **Micro-prudential regulation**: (1) make financial institutions' leverage counter-cyclical; (2) change executive compensation so managers lose substantial income when trades turn out bad. Also regulate non-financial leverage, such as real estate down payments.
- **Macro-prudential monitoring**: regulators watch institutions' trades. "Many institutions making the same trade, i.e. the same bet, results in high systemic risk to all if the trade goes bad." Response: require more capital when positions are crowded on similar assumptions.
- **Constraint**: the financial sector will resist, because regulation cuts its profitability, size and political influence.
- **Outlook**: "The future is likely to bring more financial crises." Severity depends on how regulation adjusts.

# Chapter 1, Sections 1–4: The Credit Blind Spot in Economic Models

Source sections: chapter opening, THE ERROR OF OUR WAYS, THE MECHANICS OF ECONOMICS, ECONOMIC BLINDERS.

## Core Idea
Schulte claims that a banking system's capacity to create credit causes income, output, inflation and asset prices. Standard macro models leave credit and banks out. They therefore miss credit crises that he calls predictable.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Credit-first causality ("bank credit drives economics, not the other way around")**: the capacity of a country's banking system to create credit is the cause. Income, output, inflation and valuations are "a mere outcome" of the ability of governments and central banks to create credit.
  - When to use: before reading any GDP, inflation, earnings or money-supply series as a driver.
  - How: (1) ask what the banking system can still lend, (2) ask what funds that lending, (3) treat the macro series as lagging results of (1) and (2).
  - Failure mode: the text asserts this causality from 25 years of sell-side experience. This excerpt gives no formal test of it.
- **The four properties of "black swan" credit events**: Schulte says the book will show that these events
  1. are predictable, because definite and repeatable circumstances foretell credit crises,
  2. "happen like clockwork": banker behaviour (local and international) runs on a timeline and sets off a chain reaction across several asset prices with similar patterns,
  3. are a direct function of the credit cycle and have little to do with an economic cycle,
  4. can be seen "a mile away" if credit conditions get "primus inter pares" status among traditional economic indicators.
  - When to use: as the test for whether a "tail event" narrative is really an unmonitored credit cycle.
  - Note: the excerpt states these as promises for later chapters. Chapter 1 supplies thresholds (see ch02–ch05), not proof.
- **Bank equity prices as a leading indicator**: bank stock prices are "a pretty good indicator of problems to come" and sit in no economic model.
  - How: watch for large banks' share prices peaking and falling while macro forecasts stay benign.
  - Evidence given: Citi peaked in 2005 and fell for a full two years before the meltdown. Lehman Brothers peaked in 2006 and fell for 18 months before it imploded.
- **Credit-market prices versus official forecasts**: in September 2008 credit spreads and credit default swap prices on auto loans, mortgages, corporate debt, high-yield debt and commercial property had moved to levels the text calls never before seen. The Fed's forecast had not moved.
  - How: when credit-market prices and the official growth forecast disagree, Schulte sides with the credit market.

## Key Concepts
- **Dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) model**: the model most economists were trained in. The text says it adapts macroeconomics to microeconomics and seeks the price at which markets clear for a firm under near-perfect competition.
- **DSGE assumptions, as listed by Schulte**: all agents are identical, markets are rational, everyone acts in a self-interest that is best for all, and people "essentially borrow from themselves".
- **Endogenous money demand**: an IMF working paper (Carpenter and Demiralp, 2010) is quoted. Most policy models either exclude money or model money demand as entirely endogenous. That rules out any causal role for reserves and money.
- **"The plumbing"**: Raghuram Rajan's term, as quoted, for the credit and lending machinery that economists ignored because it had not yet backed up.
- **Government-manipulated oligopoly**: Schulte's label for telecoms, defence contractors, banks, energy companies, port authorities and central banks. He says they distort wages, credit, growth and capital allocation.
- **Great Moderation**: the phrase attributed to Greenspan. Schulte says it overlooked explosive mortgage-debt growth funded by volatile offshore money.
- **Greenbook**: the Federal Reserve staff forecast, used here as the benchmark for official forecast error.
- **Deleveraging**: the unwinding of leverage. Banks call in loans on homes, cars, commercial buildings and businesses, and the economy shrinks.
- **"Harmless sideshow"**: the quoted description of how economics treated financial markets for decades.

## Mental Models
- Think of credit as the precondition for demand. The text says nearly every major life choice (marriage, a home, university, a business expansion, hospital care, children) depends on available credit.
- Think of the overshoot as a slingshot. Unmodelled credit drives demand far above trend, and the credit downturn then collapses it.
- Use the "hands over the face" test. If a model has no debt term, its users will read the absence of debt as the absence of danger.
- Use funding stability as a separate question from credit volume. US mortgage growth was funded by German Landesbanks, central banks and other large institutions that could pull money at short notice, and did.

## Anti-patterns
- **Modelling an economy with no banks**: the text says the model had no room for banking, or for Fannie Mae, which it says "happens to control 50 percent of the mortgages in the country".
- **Assuming the right amount of credit is "just there"**: supply-and-demand clearing then looks rational while credit drives an overshoot.
- **Treating financial regulation as outside macro**: the Olivier Blanchard quotation concedes this error.
- **Groupthink by authority**: if important people leave debt out, others conclude it does no harm.
- **Watching inflation into a credit collapse**: some Fed members worried about inflation, and considered raising rates, in mid-2008.

## Worked Example: the Fed in 2007–2008
1. Late 2007: the Fed voices concern that inflation is creeping up. Schulte, then in Lehman Brothers' Asian research department, reports deep anxiety inside investment banks about the leverage unwind.
2. Mid-2008: some Fed members consider raising rates. The Dallas Fed's Fisher warns about inflation.
3. September 2008: the Greenbook forecasts 2 percent growth for 2009 and 2.75 percent for 2010. Credit spreads and credit default swap prices across five loan classes already sit at extremes.
4. The crisis breaks. Bernanke responds with credit guarantees and the TARP program.
5. December 2008: the Greenbook forecasts a 4.7 percent contraction for 2009.

Schulte's reading: the credit data were public three months before the forecast changed. The model could not use them. Endnote 7 adds that the Greenbook cannot forecast accurately even one quarter ahead, because the models cannot take credit data.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat credit capacity as the driver and macro aggregates as outcomes.
2. Add bank share prices and credit-market prices to any indicator set. The text says neither sat in any economic model.
3. Check how a credit boom is funded. Offshore wholesale funding can leave at once.
4. An official forecast with no credit input can miss a recession that has already started.
5. A crisis called a black swan is, on this view, an unwatched credit cycle.

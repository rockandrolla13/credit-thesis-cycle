# Chapter 1, Sections 5–6: The Loan/Deposit Ratio and the Credit Cycle

Source sections: THE CORPORATE EXAMPLE, PAINFUL REALITY. Includes Figure 1.1.

## Core Idea
The loan/deposit ratio (LDR) measures how much of a country's savings its banks have lent out. Schulte uses it as the single gauge of where a country stands in its credit cycle. An LDR of 1 with a current account of zero is the "natural resting place".

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Loan/deposit ratio (LDR)**: bank loans divided by bank deposits. Loans are bank assets and represent borrowing. Deposits are bank liabilities and represent savings.
  - Causal chain as stated: savings of people and companies create credit, and credit creates money supply.
  - When to use: to place a country in its credit cycle before reading earnings, GDP or valuation data.
  - How: compute system-wide loans over deposits. Include non-deposit-funded lenders. Schulte adds the US government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs) to the US figure and says economics "forgot" them.
- **The LDR cycle map**: see the threshold table below. The cycle starts near 0.5, rests at 1.0, turns dangerous above 1.2 and reaches its endgame at 1.2–1.4.
- **LDR and current account link (Figure 1.1)**: across countries, a fitted curve of LDR against current account as a percent of GDP crosses an LDR of exactly 1 where the current account is 0.
  - Interpretation given: a country at that point borrows from no one and has lent out all its savings. It funds itself and does not depend on wholesale funding from foreign banks.
- **Pro-cyclical foreign wholesale funding**: international bank inflows amplify the cycle. They do not balance it.
  - Mechanism: (1) domestic savings run out as the LDR nears 1, (2) domestic bankers look abroad and treat foreign funds as "other people's money", (3) foreign bankers see a liquid country as "low risk, easy pickings", (4) the inflow creates a boom and extends the cycle beyond its natural length, (5) foreign lenders pull money together when trouble shows.
  - Timing: foreign money usually arrives late, when the cycle is ageing. Schulte calls it "steroids for an aging athlete".
- **Prudential LDR policy**: four regulatory moves Schulte says are gaining ground.
  - (a) restrict the absolute LDR to below 1,
  - (b) restrict a bank's lending to its capacity to raise deposit liabilities in the country ("subsidiarization"),
  - (c) restrict the funds used in leveraged mortgage activity,
  - (d) force banks to capitalise in-country and keep that capital there for an extended period.
  - Schulte says this is prudent banking policy, and is neither capital controls nor autarky. He names South Korea, Hong Kong, Singapore and China as countries capping the LDR at 1.

## Key Concepts
- **Income statement as "outcome statement"**: corporate income derives from underlying credit trends, which make or break the balance sheet first.
- **Virtuous cycle**: at a low LDR, savings turn easily into loans, and loans become new deposits, provided central banks prevent sudden spikes in credit growth.
- **Wholesale banking**: excessive foreign borrowing by domestic banks. Schulte calls it a "folly".
- **Stealth rise in the LDR**: lending funded by securities, not deposits, which a deposit-based count misses. The GSEs are the example.
- **Asset/liability mismatch (GSEs)**: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac funded mortgages with mortgage-backed securities, some sold to overseas institutions, mostly foreign central banks.
- **Foreign central banks as the "foreign banks"**: they bought dollar assets to keep their currencies undervalued and their exports cheap.
- **Danger zone**: the text's phrase for an LDR of 1.2–1.3. See the inconsistency note below.
- **Endgame**: an LDR of 1.2–1.4, where the text says ratings downgrades, financial scandals, excessive greed and simultaneous foreign withdrawal tend to appear.

## Mental Models
- Think of the LDR as a fuel gauge for future credit. At a low reading "it is hard for companies to lose money", because plenty of future credit is available.
- Think of an LDR of 1 as a closed system. Every step above 1 is funded by someone who can leave.
- Use the "half-off sale" model for rate convergence. A sudden fall in borrowing cost sends the LDR up fast.
- Read P/E and EPS as outputs of the credit cycle. Schulte calls them "meaningless" on their own and says later chapters treat them as a possible contra-indicator.

## Anti-patterns
- **Focusing on earnings and EPS without credit**: this misses how credit moves national liquidity and company balance sheets.
- **Relying on GDP, money supply, leading indicators and fiscal positions alone**: Schulte calls it "a waste of time" without a view on how stretched credit and savings are.
- **Counting only deposit-funded banks**: this hides securities-funded lenders such as the GSEs.
- **Treating foreign inflows as a stabiliser**: they make good developments very good and bad ones very bad.
- **Calling LDR caps capital controls**: Schulte rejects this reading.

## Worked Example: three ways the LDR overshoots
**United States, via the GSEs.** Deposits did not fund Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Mortgage-backed securities did, and foreign central banks bought many of them. The text says this stealth LDR peaked "above 115". Figure 1.1 shows why the count matters: the US plots near 0.6 without the GSEs and near 1.65 with them.

**Southern Europe, via rate convergence.** Rates had been above 10% for years. Entry into the European Union (the text's wording) forced them to 3% to match Germany. LDRs rose far past the danger zone and reached 1.8 at the worst. The text links this to a severe depression and youth unemployment of 50 percent.

**Spain, via offshore borrowing.** Bankers escaped local limits by borrowing from countries with excess savings. Spain's LDR reached 1.7 before 2008. The text says Spain then used one tonne of cement per person for real estate. Per capita, that was more than a third above China and multiples of the United States, Japan or Korea (source given: 2004 World Cement Report).

**Brazil and Australia at the time of writing.** Both sit at 1.2–1.3 with a tapped-out savings pool. Schulte credits Australia with better prudential regulation. For Brazil he cites Petrobras: USD 91 bn of dollar debt, twice the average dollar debt of the seven largest oil companies.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compute the LDR for the whole credit system, including securities-funded lenders.
2. Read the LDR together with the current account. Above 1 means foreign money funds the margin.
3. Late-cycle foreign inflows extend a boom and raise the cost of its end.
4. A sudden drop in a country's rate structure is a trigger for an LDR overshoot.
5. Between 1.2 and 1.4, expect downgrades, scandals and synchronised foreign withdrawal.
6. Liquid systems named at the time of writing: the Philippines, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore, Hong Kong, China and much of Africa.

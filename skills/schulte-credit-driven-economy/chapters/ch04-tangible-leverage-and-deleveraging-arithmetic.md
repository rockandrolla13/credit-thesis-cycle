# Chapter 1, Sections 8–9: Tangible Leverage and the Arithmetic of Deleveraging

Source sections: TANGIBLE LEVERAGE IS THE OTHER VITALLY IMPORTANT FACTOR, WHEN DEBT BECOMES A DIRTY AND DANGEROUS WORD. Includes Figure 1.3.

## Core Idea
Tangible leverage is the second number, after the LDR. An LDR near 1.3 together with tangible leverage of 30–40× means a banking break within months. Leverage can fall in two ways only: cut assets or raise capital. Both hurt, and both happen while loan losses eat the capital.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Tangible leverage**: (assets − intangibles) ÷ (capital − intangibles).
  - Why strip intangibles: they arise when a bank pays more than cash value for an acquisition. The text sets their real value at zero and calls them "a filler".
  - When to use: to judge how thin a bank's or a system's loss buffer is.
  - How: a reading of 10× means $10 of capital per $100 of assets. A reading of 50× means $2 per $100.
- **The combined trigger (LDR about 1.3 and tangible leverage 30–40×)**: Schulte states four consequences.
  1. Corporate earnings "do not matter one whit".
  2. The stock market is "doomed". All stocks fall. Better companies outperform worse ones, and few rise.
  3. "The fuse has been lit and the macroeconomic explosion is only a few months away."
  4. Governments are out of power "within 24 months or so" of the banking explosion.
  - Schulte writes "I submit (and will prove it)". The proof is promised for later chapters.
- **Two ways to lower the LDR**: lower loans (default, liquidation, non-renewal) or raise deposits (save more, spend less).
  - Who bears it: the bank and the public. The bank forces property prices down and repossesses assets. The public must save more "just to stay even".
- **Two ways to lower tangible leverage**: "Assets have to fall and/or capital needs to rise. There is no other way."
  - Who bears it: bank managers and shareholders, often with secret deals between government and bank.
  - Asset route: turn away past customers, write off bad debt, close the loan book to new customers, add low-return cash, or liquidate securities.
  - Capital route: restore confidence and find buyers for new capital.
- **The shrinking-capital trap**: at the height of most crises, about 8 to 10 percent of a bank's balance sheet must be written off against capital. Capital falls while managers, shareholders and officials search for new capital.
  - Why loans go bad: banks stop lending customers the money to repay old debt. Schulte asks whether the world is anything but "an ever-greening of old loans by new debt".
- **Debt-deflation loop**: outright shrinkage of debt brings deflation, which feeds on itself.
  1. Asset values fall and the real value of debt rises.
  2. Real debt payments rise as home values and incomes fall. People spend less.
  3. Wages fall. People feel less safe and spend less.
  4. People expect lower prices, delay purchases and spend less.
  5. The government becomes the only economic actor and builds up debt.
  6. That debt needs low interest rates to service.
  7. Low rates give governments an incentive to produce low growth and policies that reinforce deflation.
  - Example named: Japan "in the last few years".

## Key Concepts
- **Intangibles**: balance-sheet value above the cash price of an acquisition. Deutsche Bank is the named example of a bank with a great deal of it.
- **Healthy leverage**: 10×, the level given for Thai and Indonesian banks in 2013.
- **Suicidal leverage**: 50×, the level given for "the Swiss bank" in 2008.
- **Sustainable leverage**: a minimum fall to 20× was needed for the world system to become sustainable again.
- **Systemically important institution**: the label under which public funds bail out a bank.
- **Backstop ("the dam")**: the public support needed when a 3 percent buffer faces 8–10 percent losses.
- **Ever-greening**: repaying old loans with new debt.
- **Deflation**: falling prices and wages that raise the real value of debt.

## Mental Models
- Think of the LDR as the liquidity gauge and tangible leverage as the solvency gauge. The text reads both together.
- Think of LDR repair as a public event and leverage repair as a private one. The first falls on households. The second falls on managers and shareholders, with the state behind them.
- Use buffer versus expected loss. Capital per $100 of assets must exceed the 8–10 percent write-off, or the system needs a backstop.
- Think of rate transmission as leverage export. Western rates at zero forced lower rates on Asian countries with dollar-linked exchange rates, which sped up their own leverage build-up.

## Anti-patterns
- **Picking stocks on earnings when both gauges are red**: the text says earnings stop mattering.
- **Counting intangibles as capital**: "You cannot buy a cup of coffee with an intangible asset."
- **Treating debt shrinkage as harmless**: the text calls it "very dangerous" because of deflation.
- **Letting leverage reach 30×**: the text says it should never have been allowed.
- **Delaying the adjustment**: Europe "is taking its time", and its large banks kept high leverage seven years on.

## Worked Example: the 2009 deleveraging problem
1. Start: global leverage is about 30×. Banks hold about $100 of assets per $3 of capital.
2. The buffer covers losses of 3 percent. The coming write-offs are 8–10 percent of the balance sheet.
3. The system must be backstopped, and leverage must fall to at least 20×.
4. Route one, capital: reaching 20× needs about $600 billion of new capital. Reaching 15× needs about $1.5 trillion, assuming no more losses.
5. Route two, assets: reaching 15× needs about $22 trillion of assets removed.
6. The United States chose capital. The federal government's TARP program recapitalised the banks, bad debt was written off, and leverage fell below 15× quickly.
7. Europe chose assets. Banks could get capital neither from governments nor from the ECB. The ECB did not take the debt onto its own balance sheet, which the text says would be against the law. It guaranteed trillions of dollars of debt and left it on bank balance sheets across Europe.
8. Result in Europe: Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas and Société Générale still carry high leverage seven years into the crisis. Deutsche Bank's assets in the first half of 2014 exceed the 2009 level.
9. Political cost: the text says TARP was a major reason the Republicans lost in 2008, and that every major European government fell except Germany's.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compute tangible leverage with intangibles removed from both assets and capital.
2. An LDR near 1.3 with tangible leverage of 30–40× overrides company fundamentals.
3. Compare the capital buffer with an 8–10 percent crisis write-off.
4. Classify a deleveraging by route: capital (fast, politically costly) or assets (slow, deflationary).
5. Watch for the deflation loop wherever debt is shrinking outright.
6. Low-leverage, low-LDR systems get "a free ride" while others deleverage. The text says Indonesia's wave "may be cresting" seven years after the crisis.

# Chapter 1, Sections 10–11: Bailouts, Public Debt, and the Rule of 1 and 20

Source sections: EUROPEAN BANK WOES, FOLLOWING THE NUMBERS, the Learning Tools note and the endnotes. Includes Figures 1.4 and 1.5.

## Core Idea
Bank losses move onto the public balance sheet, and governments that fund bailouts fall. The chapter closes on a two-number rule: a maximum LDR of 1 and tangible leverage of 20. Above 20×, bank share prices tend to peak and fall.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Rule of 1 and 20**: maximum LDR of 1 and tangible leverage of 20. The name comes from the Figure 1.5 caption.
  - LDR 1.0 is the "natural resting place" for liquidity (see ch02).
  - Tangible leverage of around 20× is the historical resting place for leverage.
  - Evidence claimed: "in our statistical work", bank share prices keep peaking and falling at about 20×. The market judges that each extra dollar of income earned above 20× "is not worth the risk".
  - When to use: as a two-number screen for banks and banking systems.
  - Failure mode: the excerpt shows one cross-section (Figure 1.5, a 2012 snapshot of 11 institutions). It gives no test statistics. See the Extraction Notes on where the fitted curve peaks.
- **Three routes out of a banking crisis**: the chapter's cases sort into three.

  | Route | Country | Tools named | Stated outcome |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Recapitalise | United States | TARP, write-off of bad debt | Leverage below 15× quickly |
  | Shrink or guarantee assets | Europe | ECB guarantees, debt left on bank balance sheets | High leverage seven years on |
  | Midpoint | United Kingdom | Nationalisation, moral suasion, higher public debt, central bank purchases of government debt | Years of deleveraging still ahead |

- **The crisis fiscal rule (attributed to Rogoff, *This Time Is Different*)**: each financial crisis raises government debt/GDP by an average of 50 points of GDP.
  - Schulte's addition: on average the LDR reaches 1.2, snaps back to about 0.75, and government debt/GDP rises by about 50 points.
  - Extreme case: an LDR of 1.5 to 1.7 causes "an inordinately large" rise in debt/GDP. The text says later chapters show this.
- **Bailout to government-fall link**: the public concludes that government is "in bed with the 'banksters'". Bankers keep their wealth and the middle class pays higher taxes. The distrust spreads to parliaments and to the central bank. "Governments fall after they support bailouts."
- **The post-crisis universal bank pattern**: a bank forced from high leverage toward 20× performs badly while it adjusts.
  - It must cut assets (surrender businesses, shrink loans, leave regions) or raise capital (costly rights issues that anger shareholders).
  - Some replace lost revenue with questionable business lines and then pay penalties.

## Key Concepts
- **Nationalisation**: the UK government took an 80 percent stake in Royal Bank of Scotland after its collapse and forced it to close businesses and shrink.
- **Moral suasion**: government pressure without ownership. The UK used it to push Barclays out of unprofitable investment banking.
- **Wholesale-funded lender**: Northern Rock, a real estate lender "mostly funded from wholesale borrowing".
- **Rights issue**: the capital-raising form Deutsche Bank used in mid-2014, which the text says alienated investors.
- **Resting place**: the level a ratio returns to historically. It is 1.0 for the LDR and about 20× for tangible leverage.
- **Return on equity (ROE)**: the Y axis of Figure 1.5, plotted against tangible leverage as assets over equity.

## Mental Models
- Think of a banking crisis as a transfer. Private leverage becomes public debt, roughly 50 points of GDP on average.
- Use 20× as the level where extra leverage stops paying. Past it the market prices risk above the added income.
- Read a universal bank's exits, rights issues and regulatory fines as symptoms of forced deleveraging.
- Treat a wholesale-funded lender as a fiscal risk. The text puts Northern Rock's cost at 6 points of government debt to GDP.

## Anti-patterns
- **Running a bank above 20× for the extra income**: the text says the market stops paying for it.
- **Funding a mortgage lender in wholesale markets**: Northern Rock is the cautionary case.
- **Bailing out banks without visible cost to bankers**: the text links this to electoral defeat.
- **Replacing lost revenue with questionable business lines**: the text lists fraud, high-frequency trading (which it calls tantamount to front running), tax evasion, smuggling and trading with the enemy.

## Worked Example: three banks after the crisis
**Northern Rock.** Wholesale borrowing funded most of its real estate lending. Its collapse cost 6 points of government debt to GDP, which the text equates to tens of billions of dollars of bailout funds. The text says this caused Labour's heavy electoral defeat by the Tories.

**Deutsche Bank.** Leverage was almost 50 in 2008. The bank cut it to the 20s by surrendering businesses, shrinking loans, leaving regions and raising capital. It was one of the worst-performing banks globally over the period. Its mid-2014 capital increase was a rights issue that alienated investors.

**Barclays.** The bank performed badly while it cut costs and leverage. It was forced to "essentially give up" its investment banking business and reduce its geographic reach. Its balance sheet is still one of the largest globally, with "years of painful deleveraging" ahead. The text forecasts that John McFarlane, taking charge in 2015, will cut many divisions as he did as chairman of Aviva. It calls this a foretaste for many global universal banks.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen every banking system on two numbers: LDR against 1 and tangible leverage against 20.
2. Add about 50 points of GDP to public debt in a baseline banking-crisis scenario. Add more where the LDR reached 1.5–1.7.
3. Sort a crisis response into recapitalise, shrink assets, or midpoint. The route sets the speed of repair.
4. Expect the government that funds a bailout to lose power.
5. Expect a bank deleveraging toward 20× to underperform while it does so.

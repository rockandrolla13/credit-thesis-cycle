# Patterns: Risk and Business Cycles (Cowen)

All patterns are the author's reasoning, reported as his. The book gives no numeric trading rules.

## Sort the cheap-money claim
**When to use**: someone argues that low rates have caused overinvestment. (Ch 1, Ch 4)
**How**: (1) Ask which claim is made. "Investment is riskier" is the risk-based claim. "Investment is systematically excessive" is the traditional Austrian claim. (2) For the second, check the thirteen requirements in Ch 4. (3) Ask what entrepreneurs are supposed to have misread and why they misread it in the costly direction.
**Trade-offs**: the risk-based claim is easier to defend and predicts less. It says the spread of outcomes widened. It does not say a bust follows.

## Trace the easy-money-to-risk chain
**When to use**: judging whether an easing raised the economy's risk. (Ch 2, Ch 3)
**How**: check four links. (1) Substitution: did investment rise against safer allocations? (2) Wealth: did higher asset values raise risk tolerance? (3) Composition: did the mix move to longer, harder-to-reverse projects? (4) Credit allocation: did credit share move to riskier or smaller borrowers?
**Trade-offs**: links 3 and 4 do not show up in aggregate investment or in measured real rates.

## Level versus volatility test
**When to use**: any forecast of long-term investment from a policy change. (Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 4)
**How**: split the policy change into two parts. A lower real rate or easier finance pushes long-term investment up. Higher volatility of loanable funds supply pushes it down. State which part is larger.
**Trade-offs**: the author says the first comparison "has received no empirical investigation". When both operate, tests of either alone tend to fail.

## Five-cause scan for an investment boom or contraction
**When to use**: explaining why risky investment moved. (Ch 2)
**How**: check each cause and its sign. (1) Risk preference. (2) Loanable funds supply and the real rate. (3) Finance constraints: collateral values, monitoring, covenants. (4) Resolution or rise of uncertainty. (5) Retained earnings.
**Trade-offs**: the theory does not care which cause acts. It cannot rank them.

## Composition over aggregate
**When to use**: an aggregate investment or elasticity number looks flat. (Ch 2, Ch 5)
**How**: look at the mix. Compare long-lived against short-lived, durable against non-durable, and small-firm against large-firm borrowing.
**Trade-offs**: capital-intensity has no single metric (Ch 4, Appendix C). Rankings of sectors by term or risk are rough.

## Regime audit
**When to use**: judging whether a policy regime stabilizes investment sectors. (Ch 3)
**How**: (1) Name the regime's target. (2) Name the shocks that hit the target: productivity for a price rule, money demand for rate smoothing, velocity for a nominal GNP rule. (3) Those shocks now drive loanable funds supply. (4) Ask how volatile they are.
**Trade-offs**: no regime removes the mechanism. A fixed base rule removes only the central bank's own volatility.

## Follow the injection
**When to use**: asking whether a monetary change has real sectoral effects. (Ch 3)
**How**: (1) Find where the new money enters. (2) Compare the first recipients' spending and saving pattern with the economy's. (3) If they lend it, investment sectors gain. (4) Ask whether the injection continues. A one-time injection reverses. An ongoing one persists.
**Trade-offs**: needs knowledge of recipients' marginal spending. For gold money the sign can invert.

## Which variable is more volatile?
**When to use**: asking what a market signal will be confused with. (Ch 4)
**How**: list the possible causes of the observed price move. The confusion goes to the more volatile cause. For a lower real rate: saving is stable and investment demand is volatile.
**Trade-offs**: Cowen calls this "intuition". Expectations formation "remains a black box".

## Term structure read
**When to use**: judging how long lenders expect cheap funds to last. (Ch 4)
**How**: a fall in the short rate alone signals a temporary rise in loanable funds. A fall in the long rate signals expected permanence.
**Trade-offs**: ex ante long real rates are hard to measure (Ch 5). The expectations theory has a mixed empirical record (Ch 3, footnote 19).

## Flow against stock
**When to use**: sizing the possible damage from a boom. (Ch 4)
**How**: only investment made during the boom is at risk under the Austrian claim. Compare that flow with the existing capital stock.
**Trade-offs**: the Austrian reply uses "secondary deflation", which borrows from other theories.

## Cross-border transmission check
**When to use**: asking whether one country's monetary shift moves another's investment. (Ch 3)
**How**: (1) Identify the exchange rate regime. (2) Under a hard peg treat the area as one economy. (3) Under floating, ask whether the passive country's nominal rate or the forward exchange rate adjusts. (4) A rate adjustment transmits the investment effect. An exchange rate adjustment transmits a trade shock.
**Trade-offs**: "indeterminate outside of a fully specified model".

## Evidence discrimination test
**When to use**: citing a statistic as support for the theory. (Ch 5)
**How**: (1) Ask which rival theories predict the same result. (2) Check whether the test used money growth or price inflation. (3) Check for controls on real shocks and on inflation variability. (4) Check whether rates are ex ante or ex post.
**Trade-offs**: by the author's standard almost nothing discriminates.

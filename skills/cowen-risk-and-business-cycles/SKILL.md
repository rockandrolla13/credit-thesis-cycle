## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Two theories of the investment cycle (Ch 1, Ch 4)
- **Traditional Austrian theory**: "positive rates of nominal money growth induce unsustainable increases in long-term investment." It needs entrepreneurs to be fooled.
- **Risk-based theory** (Cowen's): money growth or other shifts "may make an economy riskier". Sometimes a sustainable boom follows. Sometimes a boom/bust follows. "Entrepreneurs will not err on average, but policy may make the economy riskier and thus more cyclical."
- Use the first sorting question on any cheap-money argument: does it claim *more risk* or *systematic excess*?

### 2. How cheap money leads to riskier, longer-term investment (Ch 2, Ch 3)
Risky investment is "long-term, costly to reverse, high-yielding" and information-sensitive. A lower real rate moves firms toward it through five links:
1. **Substitution**: investment gains against consumption and safer uses.
2. **Wealth**: higher portfolio values lower risk aversion.
3. **Composition**: firms switch from safe existing lines to new risky ventures. This can be large "even when the measured interest elasticity for aggregate investment is zero".
4. **Present value**: cheaper borrowing favors long-dated payoffs.
5. **Credit allocation**: under credit rationing, easing "induces a higher percentage allocation of credit to riskier firms".

A "real rate decline" includes relaxed finance constraints. Investors are not tricked. They accept the risk knowingly. Average returns and aggregate risk both rise.

### 3. The six assumptions (Ch 2)
1. Investment is riskier than consumption.
2. A lower real rate raises investment and each investor's risk.
3. Individual risk increases add up to aggregate risk.
4. Riskier implemented projects have higher expected returns.
5. Near-term returns are more certain than far-term returns (informational decay).
6. An exogenous rise in real risk contracts investment.

The first four are essential. Risk does not diversify away because sectors are few, projects are complements, and entrepreneurs forecast the same variables.

### 4. Two scenarios, opposite signs (Ch 2, Ch 3)
- **Money growth scenario**: lower real rates or easier finance. Boom first. Higher cyclicality after. Outcome uncertain.
- **Volatility scenario**: higher volatility of money or loanable funds. Immediate contraction of long-term investment. No boom first.
- **Stagflation**: both at once, with the volatility effect dominating.
- Five causes work in both directions: risk preference, loanable funds supply, finance constraints, uncertainty, retained earnings.

### 5. Booms and busts share a source (Ch 2)
- Too little risk gives slow growth and no cycle. Too much risk gives a cycle.
- Busts arrive "sharply and without prior warning" when new information disappoints. Growth rates change gradually.
- "The costs of business downturns will be correlated inversely with their frequency."
- Sizes of booms and sizes of busts are correlated.

### 6. Credit rationing channel (Ch 3)
- Banks ration because higher rates worsen borrower risk-taking and the borrower pool (Stiglitz and Weiss 1981).
- Policy then works through availability. Measured real rates need not move.
- Volatility bites harder. Borrowers face the same rate or "infinity".

### 7. Policy regime audit (Ch 3)
Any regime that uses money to stabilize another target lets shocks to that target drive loanable funds. Price rules import productivity shocks. Nominal rate smoothing imports money demand shocks. Nominal GNP rules import velocity shocks. Gold, 100 percent reserves and securitized money change the conduit only. Verdict: "no automatic remedy". The one prescription: "attempt to minimize monetary volatility".

### 8. Why money is non-neutral (Ch 3)
New money enters at specific points (injection effects). A real open market operation is a fiscal act. The central bank spends money created below its value. Money's liquidity yield cannot be split off and refunded. A one-time injection reverses. Ongoing inflation keeps the real rate effect.

### 9. The thirteen requirements of the Austrian claim (Ch 4)
Systematic overinvestment needs, among others: naive and elastic expectations, errors in the costliest direction, volatile saving, confusion of inflation with thrift and not with weak investment demand, real rates as good predictors of consumer demand, a term structure that fails to inform, and persistent arbitrage profits. Cowen: the claim is "no more than a conceivable but incomplete scenario" (quoting Yeager). Reconciled view: blame *volatile* money growth.

### 10. Evidence status (Ch 5)
Seven predictions tested against other researchers' work. Verdict: "decidedly mixed". Relatively strong: money and short real rates, non-neutrality across sectors, credit and lending channels, cyclicality of long-term goods. Weak: money and long real rates, money and measured market risk. Nothing "discriminates decisively" for the theory.

## Use for Credit and Macro Questions

- **Debt-financed investment booms**: the core use. It gives the mechanism from cheap funding to riskier, longer-lived projects, and a discipline against calling every boom malinvestment (Ch 2, Ch 4).
- **Credit spreads**: little. No model of spread levels. It reports that the T-Bill/commercial paper spread and paper issuance lead output, and that the low-grade corporate versus government return spread is a priced risk factor (Ch 5).
- **Inflation and Fed regimes**: the regime audit, the stagflation rule, and the level-versus-volatility split (Ch 3). All Fed evidence is pre-1997.
- **Issuer screening**: nothing at issuer level. Only sector and size results: durable, construction-linked and capital goods sectors and small firms respond most to money (Ch 5).
- **Loan quality**: one direct result. Loose policy raises the ratio of risky to safe loans (Lang and Nakamura 1995, Ch 5).

## Method or Case

- **METHOD**: ch01, ch02, ch03, ch04, ch05. Every chapter teaches how to reason about or test the investment cycle.
- **CASE**: none. The book has no episode chapter and no interviews. Ch 5 states that it excludes the historical and case study literature, including Austrian accounts of the Great Depression.
- Note on ch05: it is method (a survey of tests), but its findings are dated evidence from pre-1997 samples. Treat the numbers as historical results, not as current parameters.

## Trust Notes

- **Second-hand evidence**: Cowen runs no tests. Every number in ch05 is his report of another paper. Check the original before relying on one.
- **Lost in extraction**: the body of Table 1.1 (capital durability by industry), the body of Table 4.1 (reswitching, rebuilt from the text), and all five figures (2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 3.1, 3.2). The chapter files describe the figures from the surrounding text only.
- **Text defects**: line-break hyphens are dropped ("highyielding"). "Cyclicality" often appears as "cyclically". The bibliography and index were not converted into the skill.
- **Scope**: theory is verbal, with no formal model. The author says the theory "cannot be judged as empirically superior" to rivals.

# Chapter 4: Business Cycles Without Rational Expectations: The Traditional Approach of the Austrian School

## Core Idea
The claim that cheap money causes *systematic* overinvestment in long-term projects needs thirteen special assumptions about how entrepreneurs misread signals. Cowen does not rule the claim out. He shows it is "a considerably more contingent proposition than its advocates have claimed". Volatile money growth, not positive money growth, is the more likely culprit.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The traditional Austrian claim** (section "The traditional Austrian claim"): "Positive rates of nominal money growth induce unsustainable increases in long-term investment."
  - Rothbard's version (1975): businessmen "are misled by the bank inflation into believing that the supply of saved funds is greater than it really is".
  - Lending the new funds is not enough to start a cycle. Entrepreneurs could reinvest in safe, short-term processes or repay debt. The cycle needs them to be *fooled* by the rate signal.
- **Two expectational assumptions behind the claim** (same section):
  1. *Naive expectations*: entrepreneurs underestimate the chance that monetary inflation explains the data.
  2. *Elastic expectations*: entrepreneurs overestimate the permanence of observed price and interest rate changes.
  - Both give "too much weight" to visible signals.
- **Three signal extraction problems** (same section):
  1. Confusing real and nominal price changes. (This is the Lucas 1972 problem.)
  2. Reading interest rates as information about forthcoming expenditure streams.
  3. Misjudging the permanence of price or rate changes.
  - The Austrian claim rests on problems two and three.
- **The eight-part critique** (section "Outline of the critique"). None of the eight needs rational expectations.
  1. *Especially costly errors.* The claim has entrepreneurs err in the most costly direction. A "mirror image" theory is as plausible: entrepreneurs fear inflation, read a true savings rise as inflation, and underinvest. Growth is low and no cycle occurs. A "winner's curse" defense fails. It would work without inflation and does not favor long-term projects.
  2. *Inflation versus inflationary volatility.* Inflation signals a volatile environment. Volatility raises option value and cuts long-term investment. McDonald and Siegel (1986): moderate uncertainty "may more than double the required expected rate of return". Evans (1991) finds inflation and inflation volatility positively correlated.
  3. *Savings must be volatile.* The confusion with a savings rise is plausible only if savings is volatile and unpredictable. Empirically savings is among the most stable variables. If savings were volatile, real cycles would occur without any monetary cause. The claim is "caught in a bind".
  4. *Investment demand is the more volatile curve.* A lower real rate has three possible causes: more saving, a monetary injection, or lower investment demand. Entrepreneurs more likely confuse inflation with the more volatile variable. Investment demand is "far more volatile" than savings. A naive reader then sees pessimism, not thrift.
  5. *Real versus nominal confusion works the other way.* Naive entrepreneurs miss the coming inflation. They overestimate real borrowing costs and borrow too little. Unexpected inflation "of even a few percent" makes ex post real rates zero or negative (Summers 1983). One signal problem insures against another.
  6. *Rates say little about consumer demand.* Consumption is stable through time and with respect to the real rate. Real rates do predict demand for debt-financed durables such as homes and cars. That is a different chain of reasoning from the Austrian one. Rates also say nothing about *which* goods will be demanded.
  7. *The term structure tells investors.* The short rate signals how much is saved now. The long rate signals how long savings will last. Money growth moves short real rates more than long real rates. Banks know they received new reserves and will lend short. If entrepreneurs still misread, traders could earn systematic profits by shorting medium-term bonds.
  8. *Sustainability.* A constant money growth rate adds a predictable amount of loanable funds each period. So why should the new investments fail? See the next framework.
- **Sustainability under constant money growth** (section "Are the new investments sustainable?"):
  - *First, second and third-round recipients.* Banks receive the reserves. Borrowers invest them. Factor owners receive the spending and consume at their normal propensities.
  - The Austrian case needs entrepreneurs to "overestimate the correlation between observed first-round spending effects and subsequent latter-round spending effects".
  - Three hypotheses that could rescue it. Cowen calls each "plausible, but none is necessarily compelling":
    1. Inflation breaks the link between the loan rate and consumers' marginal rate of substitution.
    2. Money growth makes the wealth distribution volatile.
    3. Constant nominal growth gives a falling real supply of loanable funds.
  - *Frictions of adjustment.* Prices adjust faster as inflation continues. Cowen's illustration: capital goods prices rise 2 percent in year one, 3 percent in year two, 4 percent in year three. Real loanable funds increments shrink and real rates rise.
  - *The policymaker's dilemma.* Rising money growth can hold real effects constant for a while. Inflation "cannot rise forever". At some point it stabilizes or ends in hyperinflation.
  - *Size of the damage.* Only investment made during the boom is misallocated. If new investment is small relative to the capital stock, the distortion "may not be large enough to generate cyclical behavior".
- **The natural rate, restated** (section "The 'natural rate of interest'"):
  - The market rate always clears the loanable funds market. In that sense it is always "natural".
  - Discoordination arises "not when interest rates are at 'incorrect' levels, but when entrepreneurs misinterpret the information contained in interest rates".
  - *Minimum-noise interest rate*: the rate set by a process that is easy to predict. It is a property of a policy regime, not of a rate level.
- **The thirteen requirements** (section "Concluding remarks on the traditional Austrian theory"). The Austrian claim needs all of these:
  1. Naive expectations about inflation.
  2. Overestimated permanence of rate changes.
  3. Systematic errors in especially costly directions.
  4. The low-rate effect outweighs the volatility and option value effect.
  5. Volatile, unpredictable saving.
  6. Inflation confused with savings, not with weaker investment demand.
  7. The real rate is a significant predictor of future demand curves.
  8. Problems two and three outweigh the real versus nominal problem.
  9. Consumption varies a lot through time.
  10. Persistent profit opportunities in term structure trades.
  11. The term structure fails to transmit banks' view of monetary policy.
  12. Overestimated correlation between first-round and later-round spending.
  13. Constant nominal money growth gives a volatile real supply of loanable funds.
- **Risk-based answers to the thirteen** (section "Comparison with the risk-based theory"): rational expectations hold. Errors go in all directions. Entrepreneurs "need not be confused by monetary inflation at all". No term structure arbitrage exists. Positive constant money growth raises cyclicality "but they do not necessarily bring a business downturn or a decline in welfare".
- **Reconciliation** (section "Can the two approaches be reconciled?"): shift the Austrian emphasis from positive to *volatile* money growth. Hayek can be read either way (Laidler 1994). The difference that remains: under the risk-based view, volatility brings an immediate contraction, not an expansion of malinvestment.

## Key Concepts
- **Mirror image theory**: entrepreneurs are too cautious about inflation and underinvest.
- **Forced savings** (Appendix A): inflation bids resources from consumption to capital goods. The contested second-round version has consumer prices rising faster than incomes. Cowen finds it "an unlikely second-order effect" and "inessential". If it occurred it would partly *validate* the new investment.
- **Ricardo effect** (Appendix B): Hayek's argument that lower real wages reverse the move to capital. Cowen rejects it. It confuses a shift of the demand curve with a movement along it. The relevant price is (w/p)/(k/p), which a change in p leaves unchanged.
- **Capital-intensity** (Appendix C): has a value dimension and a time dimension. No single metric ranks projects at all interest rates.
- **Capital reswitching**: a technique is most profitable at two distant rates and not in between.
- **Capital reversing**: a lower real rate lowers the value of the capital stock. Same phenomenon in a different metric.
- **Paradox of information** (Grossman and Stiglitz 1980): not everyone can read prices naively, or prices carry no information.
- **Secondary deflation**: the Austrian oral-tradition device for a general fall in investment values.

## Mental Models
- Use the question "which variable is more volatile?" to decide what a price move will be confused with.
- Think of the term structure as the channel by which the best-informed lenders tell everyone else how long cheap funds will last.
- Read "multiple equilibria" as "unmodeled causal factors".
- Think of every monetary misperception theory as implying a real cycle theory. Money can only be confused with a real shock that itself moves the economy.

## Anti-patterns
- **Blaming the level of the interest rate**: the error is in the reading of the signal, not in the rate.
- **"Investment rose without saving, so it is malinvestment"**: new lending is an act of saving by the new money recipients. Long-term investment "ought to increase by some degree".
- **Counting only the signal problems that point one way**: "we cannot rule out the contrary forces which induce distortions in other directions".
- **Assuming more signal extraction problems means worse forecasts**: welfare losses "do not increase monotonically" with their number.
- **Using capital-intensity as if it were one number**: the Cambridge critique applies. The risk-based theory "sidesteps" it.

## Worked Example
Capital reswitching (Appendix C, Table 4.1). The table body was lost in extraction. The text describes it:
- Two techniques each produce one unit of output after three periods.
- Technique 1 applies seven units of input in period two.
- Technique 2 applies two units in period one and six units in period three.
- The equiprofitable rate solves 7(1+i) = 2(1+i)^2 + 6.
- Roots: i = 0.5 and i = 1.0.
- Technique 1 is more profitable below 50 percent and above 100 percent. Technique 2 is more profitable in between.
- Cowen's reading: this is a partial equilibrium result. It varies the rate alone. In general equilibrium each rate comes with different saving, spending and relative prices (Garrison 1979). Mayer (1960) finds the time and value dimensions of capital positively correlated in practice.
- Clean case: project 1b invests one unit for one period. Project 2b invests five units for five periods. 2b is more capital-intensive at every rate.

The term structure trade (section on the term structure): if entrepreneurs overestimate how long short rates stay low, short rates end higher than expected. A trader shorts medium-term bonds and may buy long-term instruments as a hedge. Cowen uses this as a *reductio*. Persistent profits of this kind should not exist, so the Austrian misreading should not persist.

## Key Takeaways
1. "Rates are low, so there is overinvestment" skips thirteen steps.
2. Low rates plus volatile policy may shrink long-term investment.
3. Real rates do predict demand for debt-financed durables such as housing. That is the one demand-signal channel Cowen accepts.
4. Money growth moves short real rates more than long real rates.
5. Only boom-period investment is at risk. Compare its flow with the capital stock.
6. Constant money growth sustains new long-term investment "to some degree".
7. The reconciled view blames volatile money growth.

# Chapter 5: Empirical Evidence

## Core Idea
Cowen surveys other researchers' statistical tests against seven predictions of the monetary scenarios. The evidence is "decidedly mixed". Many results are consistent with the risk-based theory. None discriminates decisively in its favor.

All tests are second-hand. Cowen runs none of his own. The data are mostly US, twentieth century or post-war, and all pre-1997.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Seven falsifiable predictions** (section "Introduction"):
  1. Do increases in the money supply lower real interest rates?
  2. Does monetary policy affect bank lending?
  3. Does monetary policy induce sectoral shifts?
  4. Does unexpected money growth increase expected stock returns and stock return volatility?
  5. Does monetary policy have predictive power over investment?
  6. Does money supply or interest rate volatility discourage high-yielding investments or predict output declines?
  7. Are capital-intensive sectors more cyclical than other sectors?
- **Why the tests are weak by construction** (sections "Introduction" and "Money, credit, and the bank lending channel"):
  - Monetary shocks do not bring the same result each time. Averages may show no effect.
  - Money need not predict turning points. It sets vulnerability. A real shock sets the date.
  - When the money growth and volatility scenarios both operate, a test of either one alone "will likely fail".
  - Real shocks are harder to isolate than monetary shocks. So only the monetary scenarios get tested.
- **Evidence scorecard** (author's verdicts, by section):

  | Question | Verdict in the text | Key results |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1. Money and short real rates | "partial but not complete support" | Mishkin (1981b), Summers (1983), Shiller (1983), Christiano and Eichenbaum (1992b) find effects. Fama (1975) and others find a pure Fisher effect |
  | 1b. Money and long real rates | "less support" | Mishkin (1981a) cannot reject no correlation |
  | 2. Bank lending and credit | Consistent, not definitive | Kashyap et al. (1993), Bernanke and Blinder (1992) for. Romer and Romer (1990), Ramey (1993) against |
  | 3. Sectoral shifts | "some of the most promising evidence" | Kretzmer (1989), Thorbecke, Lang and Nakamura (1995) |
  | 4. Stock returns and risk | Returns: consistent. Risk: "partial and ambiguous" | Marshall (1992), Rozeff (1974), Kaul and Seyhun (1990). Against: Boudoukh et al. (1994) |
  | 5. Money and investment | Consistent, not decisive | Wainhouse (1984), Gordon and Veitch (1986) |
  | 6. Volatility | Mixed | For: Mascaro and Meltzer (1983), Evans (1984), Ferderer (1993). Against: Brainard et al. (1980), parts of Leahy and Whited (1995) |
  | 7. Capital-intensive cyclicality | Supported, does not discriminate | Murphy et al. (1989), Wainhouse (1984), Mack (1956) |

- **Tests on cheap money and the riskiness of lending** (sections on bank lending and sectoral shifts). These are the direct tests of the "easy money, riskier investment" link:
  - *Lang and Nakamura (1995)*: when monetary policy is loose, "the ratio of risky loans to safe loans rises". The reverse holds when policy is tight. The ratio Granger-causes GDP and inventory investment. Risk is measured as the loan's mark-up over the prime rate. Limit: this is loan risk, not project risk.
  - *Oliner and Rudebusch (1995)*: tight money does not change the bank versus non-bank debt mix for small or large firms taken separately. It reallocates "all types of credit from small firms to large firms".
  - *Gertler and Gilchrist (1993, 1994)*: expansionary policy raises the borrowing share of small firms. Contractionary policy lowers it.
  - *Kashyap and Stein (1994b)*: tight money hits small banks hardest.
  - Cowen's inference: if small firms are riskier, monetary expansion raises aggregate risk. Footnote 9 caveat: not if small firms' marginal projects are much safer than their inframarginal ones.
- **Tests on longer-term and durable sectors** (sections on sectoral shifts and investment):
  - *Kretzmer (1989)*: unanticipated money and hours worked in thirty industries. See Worked Example.
  - *Thorbecke (1995a)*: stock returns by industry. Most affected by monetary policy: lumber, clay, glass and stone, primary metals, transportation equipment, furniture, rubber, then capital goods industries. Least affected: food, textiles, utilities, tobacco, apparel, leather.
  - *Taylor and Yücel (1996)*, Texas: most industries are insensitive to real rates. Construction, apparel, non-electrical machinery and primary metals are sensitive. Real rates move the *composition* of employment, not the total.
  - *Ceglowski (1989)*, US: construction and related sectors are highly sensitive. Transportation equipment, chemicals, textiles, rubber and plastics are moderately sensitive.
  - *Gordon and Veitch (1986)*, US 1919 to 1983: the monetary base and the multiplier both predict investment. The link is strongest for durables.
  - *Kashyap et al. (1993)*: the commercial paper to bank loan mix explains investment. Strongest for inventories and equipment. Weaker for non-residential structures.
  - *Greenwald and Stiglitz (1988a)*: residential construction and inventories fluctuate most. Producers' durable equipment fluctuates less than total investment. Business construction fluctuates less again.
- **Credit as a leading indicator** (section "Is credit pro-cyclical?", after Friedman and Kuttner 1993a):
  1. Prices and quantities in short-term credit markets both lead output.
  2. They carry independent information.
  3. Much of the price information is in interest rate *spreads*.
  4. Commercial paper volume is a better leading indicator than bank loan volume.
  - The T-Bill/commercial paper spread: a wider spread predicts output declines. Two readings: default risk, or the stance of monetary policy.
  - Bernanke's case for the policy reading: the spread can swing "as much as 300 basis points" while high-grade paper defaults are rare. The spread is "nearly uncorrelated" with the Baa-Aaa corporate spread.
  - Commercial paper volume is *countercyclical*. Firms may issue paper to cover cash flow shortfalls before the downturn (Friedman and Kuttner 1993a).
  - Result four says: reject "narrow, bank-centered accounts". Much of the credit fluctuation is in non-bank markets.
- **Interpreting the VAR anomaly** (section "Money, credit, and the bank lending channel"): Litterman and Weiss (1985) find money has no power over output once the nominal rate is included, and they reject a real rate mechanism. Cowen's three ways out:
  1. Nominal rate changes proxy for unmeasurable ex ante real rates.
  2. Policy works through credit rationing, where the real rate need not move.
  3. Nominal rates proxy for the real shock at the turning point. Money created the vulnerability. The rate only forecasts the bust.
- **Why real rates look procyclical** (section "Cyclical behavior of real interest rates"): the supply of loanable funds shifts out first and the rate falls. Then the expected boom shifts demand out and the rate may rise. A falling real rate can precede a boom (money scenario) or a bust (volatility scenario).
- **Four rival explanations for cyclical capital-intensive sectors** (section "Are capital-intensive investments more cyclical?"):
  1. Accelerator models.
  2. Consumption smoothing leaves investment to absorb demand swings.
  3. Increasing-returns real business cycle models (Murphy et al. 1989).
  4. Stickier prices in higher-order goods (Basu 1995).

## Key Concepts
- **Liquidity effect**: money growth lowers interest rates, at least for a time. Cochrane (1989): up to a year for nominal rates.
- **Fisher effect**: nominal rates rise one for one with expected inflation. Summers (1983) finds they rise "systematically less".
- **Tax-adjusted Fisher effect**: nominal rates would need to rise about 1.3 points per 1 point of inflation. "The data refute this prediction unambiguously."
- **Romer shocks**: six episodes of deliberate Fed disinflation used as exogenous monetary shocks.
- **Non-borrowed reserves**: Christiano and Eichenbaum's preferred measure of Fed policy.
- **Relative price variability**: rises with inflation and money growth. Evidence from Argentina, Germany, Israel, Mexico, the Netherlands, the UK and the US.
- **Tobin's Q and user cost**: explain little of investment. Sales and profits explain more.
- **Bad news principle** (Bernanke 1983a): the value of waiting rises with the spread of bad outcomes.

## Mental Models
- Think of money as loading the dice and a real shock as rolling them.
- Use the money supply, not the price index, when you test the theory. Price inflation carries negative real shocks. Money growth mostly does not.
- Use variance, not Beta, as the risk measure for irreversible investment. Variance hurts even risk-neutral investors.
- Treat stock volatility as a poor proxy for real volatility. Leverage alone moves it (Christie 1982).

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading "inflation hurts stocks" as evidence against the monetary scenario**: the negative link comes from negative real shocks and from relative price variability. After Kaul and Seyhun (1990) control for variability, it disappears.
- **Reading "cyclical sectors respond most to money" as proof of the risk-based theory**: every theory that links money to output predicts it.
- **Using ex post real rates as if they were ex ante**: every such test is a joint hypothesis.
- **Assuming one inflation rate for everyone**: it biases measured real rate volatility downward.
- **Relying on long-rate tests**: ex ante long real rates are hard to measure. "We therefore know much less about long rates."
- **Expecting cash-flow significance to settle the theory**: cash flow may proxy for prospects or for funds availability. The data cannot tell.

## Worked Example
Kretzmer (1989), as Cowen reports it (section "Does monetary policy induce sectoral shifts?"):
- Design: split money into anticipated and unanticipated parts. Measure the effect of unanticipated money on hours worked in thirty industries.
- Lag pattern of the effect:

  | Lag | Effect on hours |
  |---|---|
  | Contemporaneous | Positive, small |
  | 1 year | Positive, large |
  | 2 years | Positive, small |
  | 3 years | Negative, large |
  | 4 years | Negative, small |

- Cowen: "Kretzmer's data fit a classic boom/bust cycle."
- Cross-section: effects are weakest in agriculture, forestry and fisheries and relatively strong in machinery. Seven of the ten strongest effects are in durable manufacturing.
- Durability test: assign each industry the expected life of its final product. Durability is positive and significant at the 1 percent level. The regression explains almost half the variation in the elasticities.

Other numbers reported in the chapter:
- Huizinga and Mishkin (1984): a 1 point rise in inflation goes with a fall in the ex ante real rate of 28 basis points on T-Bills, 65 on long T-Bonds and 192 on common stocks. Not controlled for real shocks or inflation variability.
- Hamilton (1985): post-war US recessions are associated with ex ante real rates "twice as high as average".
- Wainhouse (1984): in six post-war US credit expansions, credit growth went with higher investment goods output in 102 of 120 cases. Prices of goods further from consumption rose more in 213 of 300 cases.
- Romer and Romer (1990): the effect of loans on production peaks after five months and is zero after seventeen.
- Leahy and Whited (1995): 772 firms. A 10 percent rise in return variance goes with a 5 percent fall in Q. Variance loses significance for investment once Q is included. Covariance is never significant.
- Brainard et al. (1980): 187 firms. Risk and Q show both positive and negative links.
- Christie (1982): leverage and interest rates explain 16 percent of stock volatility over time, up to 48 percent in sub-samples.
- Fama (1991): expected price inflation explains about 3 percent of return variation.
- Chen et al. (1986): the two strongest priced risk factors are industrial production growth and the return spread of long-term low-grade corporates over long-term governments. Unexpected inflation and the government term structure matter less.
- Size of investment: business fixed investment is about 10 percent of US GDP. Investment swings are at least three times output swings in developed countries.
- Poitras (1995): rises in the long nominal rate predict lower output. The effect vanishes when the differenced 90-day T-Bill rate is added. "The short rate appears to be a more potent predictor."
- Ferderer and Zalewski (1994): US interest rate volatility was at unprecedented levels between 1931 and 1934.
- Pindyck and Solimano (1993): volatile marginal product of capital lowers investment and growth, mostly in developing countries. The mean inflation rate predicts that volatility better than inflation variability does.

## Key Takeaways
1. The author's own verdict: the theory "cannot be judged as empirically superior to competing business cycle explanations".
2. The author lists six results with "relatively strong evidence": real rate effects of monetary policy, real effects of interest rate volatility, non-neutrality of money, a bank lending channel, the importance of credit in fluctuations, and the greater cyclicality of long-term investment goods.
3. The text flags two weak spots: money and long real rates ("less support"), and money growth and aggregate investment risk in stock data ("partial and ambiguous").
4. The one direct test of "loose money, riskier loans" in the book is Lang and Nakamura (1995).
5. Small firms gain credit share in easings and lose it in tightenings.
6. Durable and construction-linked sectors respond most to money and to real rates.
7. Spreads and commercial paper volume lead output. Bank loan volume leads less well.

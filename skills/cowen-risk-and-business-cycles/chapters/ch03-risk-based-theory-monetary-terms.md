# Chapter 3: A Risk-Based Theory in Monetary Terms

## Core Idea
Monetary policy is a real sectoral shock. New money enters through the loanable funds market, lowers real rates or eases finance constraints, and moves the economy to a riskier mix of investment. No policy rule removes the mechanism. Only lower volatility in loanable funds supply reduces it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two basic monetary scenarios** (section "Two basic monetary scenarios"):
  1. *Money growth scenario.* Faster base money growth lowers real interest rates. A boom follows. Cyclicality rises.
  2. *Monetary volatility scenario.* Higher monetary uncertainty causes a contraction "without any prior boom".
- **Eight stylized facts the theory must fit** (section "What stylized facts does this chapter seek to explain?"):
  1. Higher nominal money growth stimulates the economy in the short run.
  2. Monetary tightenings bring immediate output declines (Romer and Romer 1989).
  3. Higher inflation does not improve welfare in the long run, on average.
  4. The long-run result of any one instance of money growth cannot be predicted.
  5. Money appears not to matter once the nominal interest rate is in a vector autoregression (Litterman and Weiss 1985).
  6. Expected and unexpected inflation do not bring fundamentally different results (Mishkin 1982).
  7. Higher volatility of nominal money lowers the growth rate (Mascaro and Meltzer 1983).
  8. Stagflation is possible but does not always occur.
- **Stagflation rule** (same section): stagflation arises when high money growth and high monetary volatility occur together *and* the volatility effect on growth outweighs the level effect on returns. So "we should expect neither a stable Phillips curve, nor a stable inverse Phillips curve".
- **Expected versus unexpected money growth** (section "Expected vs. unexpected money growth"):
  - Both lower real rates. They differ in timing.
  - Expected money growth brings the boom *sooner*. Entrepreneurs adjust before the money arrives.
  - In the long run the two have "roughly equal potency".
  - Author's inference: "the Fed may wish to announce its intentions when it wishes to engage in short-run stimulation."
- **Credit rationing channel** (section "How money affects investment when banks ration credit"):
  - Why banks ration (Stiglitz and Weiss 1981): expected profit on a loan is non-monotonic in the rate. A higher rate (1) pushes borrowers to take more risk and (2) worsens the borrowing pool. "Dead-beats" do not mind high rates.
  - So banks cap loan size and turn away some observationally identical borrowers.
  - Monetary policy then works through the *availability* of funds. For an excluded borrower the real rate falls "from infinity to the rate observed in the market".
  - Measured real rates need not move. Investment still rises.
  - Banks do not hold excess reserves in the basic model. "If the last loan was profitable to make, the next one will be profitable too."
  - Volatility bites harder here. The shock lands entirely on quantities. Borrowers face a binary cost: the same rate or infinity (or a "loan shark" rate).
- **Credit rationing and risk** (section "Credit rationing and risk"): an independent reason easy money raises risk.
  - Risky borrowers, or borrowers with new risky projects, are the most credit constrained.
  - Safe borrowers ("blue chip corporations") have higher ratings, securitization access and more to lose from "go for broke strategies".
  - So an easing "induces a higher percentage allocation of credit to riskier firms".
  - This holds whether or not investment is riskier than consumption.
- **Policy regime audit** (section "Monetary policy issues"). Rule: any regime that uses the money supply to stabilize another target lets shocks to that target drive loanable funds supply.

  | Regime | What drives money supply | Effect on investment sectors |
  |---|---|---|
  | Fixed base money growth rule | Nothing | Removes central bank volatility. Does not remove private loanable funds volatility |
  | Broad aggregate target | Offsets private shocks | Smooths loanable funds if private volatility is unwanted |
  | Loose real interest rate target | Market pressure on real rates | Possible smoothing. Never an absolute peg |
  | Price level rule | Productivity shocks | Volatile productivity gives volatile money (Hayek 1967, Gray 1976) |
  | Nominal interest rate smoothing | Money demand shocks | Volatile when money demand is unstable |
  | Nominal GNP rule | Velocity shocks | Stabilizes aggregate demand. May raise sectoral volatility |
  | Commodity money | Gold supply and demand | Same logic if gold enters through banks |
  | 100 percent reserves | Base only | Moves the channel to non-bank lenders |
  | New Monetary Economics | Securities as exchange media | Moves the cyclical impetus to commercial paper markets |

  Author's conclusion: "no automatic remedy". If a simple policy fix existed, the market would already have provided a proxy for it.
- **Transmission across currencies** (section "Transmission of cycles across currencies"):
  - Permanently fixed rates: one monetary area. Single-economy analysis applies.
  - Managed fixed rates: central banks move nominal rates to steer money flows. Cyclical forces start in both countries.
  - Floating rates: covered interest parity holds. Real rate equalization does *not* hold (Mishkin 1984a). Either the passive country's nominal rate moves or the forward exchange rate moves. Transmission is partial.
  - If the exchange rate adjusts instead, the shock arrives through export and import sectors.
- **Why money matters: the fiscal decomposition** (section "Foundational issues: why does money matter?"):
  1. *Injection effect.* New money enters at specific points. A counterfeiter shifts demand toward his basket even with flexible prices.
  2. *Three reasons banks shift to private lending after an open market purchase*: portfolio spillover from higher T-Bill prices, binding reserve requirements, and eased bank liquidity constraints (the "bank lending channel", Kashyap et al. 1993).
  3. *Persistence.* A one-time operation has no lasting real rate effect. Ongoing inflation raises the flow demand for T-Bills permanently, so the real rate effect persists.
  4. *Fiscal decomposition.* A real-world open market operation is not balanced-budget. The central bank spends fiat money "at a value higher than its marginal cost of production". It runs a surplus against the private sector and spends it in one direction.
  5. *Indivisibility.* Money's liquidity yield cannot be separated from money and refunded through taxes. This is "the ultimate microfoundations for why monetary policy matters".
- **Short rates matter for long-term investment** (section "Do long rates or short rates matter?"):
  - The expected change in the long rate is given by the current short rate.
  - Investment should depend on all parts of the term structure (Altug 1993).
  - Short-term working capital and long-term capital are complements. Short-term borrowing is "especially important for small firms".
  - Theory cannot say whether investment responds more when both rates fall or when only the short rate falls.

## Key Concepts
- **Wicksell effect**: expansionary monetary policy lowers the real interest rate.
- **Injection effects**: real effects from where new money enters.
- **Bank lending channel**: monetary policy eases banks' own liquidity constraints.
- **Romer shocks**: episodes of deliberate Fed disinflation (used in Ch 5).
- **Balanced budget open market operation**: one that reverses the initial asset exchange. It is neutral. Hoover's example: two five dollar bills for a ten.
- **Velocity and persistence** (footnote 13): high velocity reverses the real effects of a one-time injection quickly.
- **Trade velocity** (footnote 4): Haberler's reading of Hayek's velocity concept. It excludes financial transactions.

## Mental Models
- Think of monetary expansion as the government buying cheese every period. The price of cheese rises permanently. Replace cheese with T-Bills.
- Think of monetary policy as moving the economy between Pareto points with different risk. It is not good or bad in itself.
- Use "where does the new money enter?" for commodity money too. Gold that enters through miners with representative spending brings no sectoral shock. Miners with above-average spending rates *invert* the story.
- Think of a rule that targets anything other than loanable funds as holding money supply "hostage" to the shocks on that target.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Banks will just hold excess reserves"**: this needs special corner assumptions (Stiglitz 1992). In the general case all funds are lent.
- **"Credit rationing means sticky interest rates"**: rates are flexible but set at non-market-clearing levels.
- **"Ricardian equivalence makes open market operations neutral"**: it covers changes in finance for given spending. It does not cover a central bank surplus spent in one direction.
- **"Only the long rate matters for investment"**: the short rate carries the expected change in the long rate and funds working capital.
- **"A gold standard or 100 percent reserves ends the cycle"**: they change the conduit, not the logic.
- **"Nominal GNP targeting minimizes investment cycles"**: money and velocity have the same effect on aggregate demand and different effects on relative prices.
- **Tobin and Mundell effects**: Cowen reports that both "have fallen out of favor". Currency demand is inelastic and the capital stock is large.

## Worked Example
Germany and Japan under floating rates (section "Floating exchange rates"):
1. Germany has an unexpected rise in inflation. German real rates fall. German long-term investment and cyclicality rise.
2. Case A: German prices adjust at once. German nominal rates *fall* (liquidity effect, as in Dornbusch 1976). Demand for mark assets falls relative to yen assets.
3. Equilibrium needs some mix of two moves. Japanese nominal rates fall, or the forward rate prices mark appreciation.
4. Japan's monetary policy and price path are unchanged. So if Japanese nominal rates fall, Japanese real rates fall too. Japan becomes more cyclical.
5. Case B: German prices rise with a lag. German nominal rates *rise* through the Fisher premium. Japanese nominal and real rates rise, or the forward rate prices mark depreciation. Japan becomes less cyclical.
6. If the exchange rate adjusts instead of rates, Japan gets an export and import shock.
7. Volatility version: a rise in German monetary volatility cuts German long-term investment. Japan receives part of the rate effect and also a traditional real shock.

The gold standard version: demand for gold rises in Germany relative to the UK. The UK central bank raises rates to stop the gold outflow. Germany may lower rates. Both moves are monetary policy, so "cyclical forces may be set off in both countries". Footnote 8: this needs imperfect capital mobility.

## Key Takeaways
1. The author's central bank prescription is modest: "attempt to minimize monetary volatility".
2. Easy money shifts credit share toward riskier firms. That is a separate channel from the real rate.
3. Under credit rationing, do not look for the effect in measured real rates. Look at quantities.
4. Announced easing moves the boom earlier.
5. Stagflation needs both high money growth and high monetary volatility.
6. Cycles cross borders through nominal rate links, and only partly.
7. Removing banks from the story moves the cyclical impetus to other credit markets.

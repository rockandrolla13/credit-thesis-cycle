# Chapter 1: Introduction

## Core Idea
Business cycles are failures of intertemporal coordination: entrepreneurs commit resources to long, hard-to-reverse projects, and later spending does not validate those choices. Cowen rebuilds the Austrian investment-based cycle theory on risk and rational expectations instead of on systematic entrepreneurial error.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two statements of investment-based cycle theory** (section "New and old Austrian perspectives"):
  1. *The traditional Austrian theory*: "positive rates of nominal money growth induce unsustainable increases in long-term investment."
  2. *The risk-based theory*: increases in the money supply, or other sectoral shifts, may make an economy riskier. Sometimes a sustainable boom results. Other times a boom/bust cycle results. "Entrepreneurs will not err on average, but policy may make the economy riskier and thus more cyclical."
  - When to use: as the first sorting question for any cheap-money argument. Ask which of the two claims the argument makes.
  - How: the two theories agree that money growth lowers real interest rates. They diverge after that point. The traditional theory says investment expands *too much* ("The Austrian Claim"). The risk-based theory says the size of the shift "cannot be determined a priori".
- **Risky versus safe investment** (section "The problem"):
  - Risky investments are "long-term, costly to reverse, high-yielding, and having returns highly sensitive to the arrival of future information".
  - Safe investments are "more liquid, lower yielding, and less sensitive to the arrival of new information". Consumption usually counts as a safe allocation.
  - The cycle mechanism in Chapters 2 and 3 is a sectoral shift between these two classes.
- **Two cycle drivers and their signs** (section "The problem"):
  - A decline in real interest rates tends to *increase* aggregate risk and cyclicality.
  - An increase in expected volatility does the opposite. It penalizes risky, information-sensitive investment and causes a downturn.
  - The effects are asymmetric. Busts "arrive suddenly and unpredictably as discrete events". Changes in the growth rate come slowly.
- **Cost accounting for investment error** (section "Some assumptions"):
  - *Adjustment costs* come from moving factors to other uses.
  - *Misallocation costs* come from leaving resources in inferior uses because adjustment is too costly.
  - A *malinvestment* is an investment where these two costs push the return below the risk-free (or minimum-risk) rate.
- **Rational expectations as discipline, not description** (section "Expectations"):
  - Definition used: forecasts are efficient and unbiased, so forecast errors are serially uncorrelated.
  - It does *not* imply small forecast errors.
  - It does *not* imply everyone has the same information. Cowen assumes two asymmetries: entrepreneurs do not know demanders' future spending and saving, and they do not always know future monetary policy.
  - Use: it forces any claimed coordination failure to name its informational asymmetry.

## Key Concepts
- **Intertemporal coordination**: the match between investment plans and later product purchase plans.
- **Capital goods**: inputs to market production, taken broadly. Consumer durables, human capital and land development all count.
- **Time to build**: the lag from decision to completed investment. Mayer (1958) surveyed 276 US companies building new plant and found an average of "up to thirty months".
- **Durability of capital**: Bizer and Sichel (1991) put the average at eighteen to thirty years across industries. Table 1.1 lists the industries (table body lost in extraction).
- **Injection of loanable funds**: the route by which new money lowers real rates in the Austrian model. New money enters "through the banking system".
- **Cyclical theory versus growth theory**: cyclical theory covers the short run, where risk and return are positively correlated. Growth theory covers a longer run where risk may fall as returns rise.

## Mental Models
- Think of modern finance theory as "the proper substitute for the books which Hayek and Marget never wrote". Austrian capital theory studied the abstract properties of capital. Finance studies its payoff streams in different world-states.
- Think of monetary policy as a real sectoral shock that works through portfolios. It is not a trick played on investors.
- Use the theory as a complement to neo-Keynesian and real business cycle theory. It explains how much vulnerability to shocks agents accept at the start.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating every inflation-induced reallocation as a "distortion" or "malinvestment"**: Cowen says these conclusions "must be demonstrated rather than assumed". Some shift toward capital goods is a correct response to changed demand.
- **Postulating one particular kind of mistake**: this "overcommits" cycle theory. Inflation may raise errors of many kinds, not one systematic kind.
- **Assuming homogeneous information**: it would make central planning feasible and removes the reason for a price system.
- **Reading rational expectations as "small errors"**: variance of outcomes around forecasts can be large.

## Worked Example
The three stylized facts the monetary version must explain (section "The problem"):
1. Monetary policy is often potent in the short run.
2. Monetary policy has unpredictable long-run effects.
3. Monetary policy cannot improve welfare on average.

Cowen's reconstruction: the central bank lowers real rates or relaxes finance constraints. Entrepreneurs accept more risk and shift to long-term, higher-yielding projects. Before the uncertainty resolves, expected returns are higher and "the economy will appear wealthier". Later the extra risk "sometimes turns into systematic economic busts". So the same policy produces a lasting boom in one episode and a boom/bust in another.

The second case: the central bank raises monetary *volatility* instead of producing an inflation surprise. Entrepreneurs shift from riskier to less risky investment. The downturn is immediate. If the level and the volatility of money growth are positively related, the result is stagflation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Cheap money does not prove malinvestment. It proves higher risk-taking.
2. The author's claim is about the *spread* of outcomes, not their mean.
3. A lower real rate and a higher volatility push long-term investment in opposite directions.
4. A "real rate decline" includes easier finance constraints, not only lower observed market rates.
5. Name the informational asymmetry before you claim a coordination failure.

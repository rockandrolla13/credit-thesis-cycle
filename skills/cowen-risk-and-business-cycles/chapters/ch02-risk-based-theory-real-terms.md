# Chapter 2: A Risk-Based Theory in Real Terms

## Core Idea
A fall in the price of risky investment makes entrepreneurs choose longer, riskier, higher-yielding projects by rational choice. The economy gains expected growth and gains cyclicality at the same time. A rise in volatility does the reverse and causes a downturn with no boom before it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The six assumptions** (section "Framework and assumptions"). The first four are essential. Five and six are "working hypotheses".
  1. Investment involves greater risk than consumption.
  2. A decline in the real interest rate increases investment and increases risk for each investor.
  3. Increases in investment risk for each entrepreneur translate into increases in aggregate risk.
  4. Riskier projects, at least those implemented in equilibrium, yield higher expected pecuniary returns.
  5. Entrepreneurs have greater certainty about returns in earlier periods than in later periods (*informational decay*).
  6. An exogenous increase in the real economic risk of investments induces a contraction of investment.
  - What Cowen does *not* assume: that real rate changes trick investors, or that investors pick systematically wrong projects.
- **How cheap money leads to riskier, longer-term investment** (assumptions 2 and 4, section "Induced increases in risk"):
  1. *Substitution effect.* A lower real rate favors investment over consumption. Investment is the riskier allocation.
  2. *Wealth and portfolio effect.* A lower real rate raises portfolio values. "Risk aversion declines with wealth", so risk-taking rises again.
  3. *Composition effect.* Entrepreneurs switch from "an existing and relatively safe line of production" to "a new and risky venture". The economy offers a "term structure" of project returns. Riskier long-term projects pay more.
  4. *Present value effect.* More loanable funds lower the price of borrowing and change present value calculations, "thereby encouraging risky, long-term investment".
  5. *Result.* Average gross returns rise. Risk-adjusted marginal returns fall to match the new, lower real rate. Aggregate risk rises.
  - A "real rate decline" is general. It includes relaxed finance constraints. A borrower who cannot borrow faces a rate that is "prohibitively high or infinite".
  - Footnote 1: the risk term structure in the real economy should be steeper than on financial assets. A 30-year plant is much riskier than a 30-year Treasury bond.
- **Five expansionary causes** (section "Induced increases in risk"). Any of them starts the boom. Reverse them for the contraction.
  1. Preference shifts toward risk (Keynesian "animal spirits").
  2. Supply-side shifts in loanable funds: higher savings or monetary policy lower the real rate.
  3. Easing of finance constraints: higher collateral values, better monitoring, lower moral hazard, new contracts and covenants.
  4. Resolution of uncertainty: option value of waiting falls (Dixit and Pindyck 1994). Strongest for long-term investment.
  5. Positive shocks to retained earnings (Myers and Majluf 1984).
- **Why risk does not diversify away in the aggregate** (assumption 3):
  - Economies have few independent sectors. Long and Plosser (1983) use six.
  - Projects are complements. The track supplier bears the locomotive supplier's risk (Lachmann 1978).
  - Entrepreneurs forecast *common* variables. Under rational expectations all expect the same mean. When the world misses that mean, all err together.
  - The Law of Large Numbers covers *subdivision* of a risk, not *multiplication* of it (Samuelson). Fifty coin flips are riskier than one.
- **Three objections and the author's answers** (section "Induced increases in risk"):
  1. *Interest elasticity of investment is too small* (Lucas 1981). Answers: the real rate is one of five causes. The first shock only starts the boom, and income and expectation effects carry it. The shift is in the *composition* of investment, which aggregate elasticities miss.
  2. *Irreversibility makes rates irrelevant* (Pindyck and Solimano 1993 report hurdle rates of "three or four times the cost of capital"). Answer: the real choice is tripartite. Investors choose among investing, consuming and waiting. Rates matter little for *when* to commit. They still matter for *what kind* of commitment.
  3. *Booms should not lift all sectors.* Answer: see comovement below.
- **Sources of boom comovement** (section "What generates the comovement of the boom?"):
  - Higher expected wealth raises demand for all normal goods. Higher-yield projects supply the capacity.
  - Informational decay: realized returns are high early because the information behind the projects is fresh.
  - Capital and labor are complements.
  - Initially unemployed resources.
- **Sources of bust clustering and persistence** (section "The bust and the cluster of errors?"):
  - Resolution of project risk on common variables.
  - Misallocation costs persist for the life of irreversible capital.
  - Time to build and time to plan.
  - Durability of capital.
  - Consumption smoothing and autocorrelated shocks.
- **The volatility scenario** (section "Volatility and immediate business cycle contractions"):
  - The five contractionary causes: lower risk tolerance, higher time preference and real rates, tighter finance constraints, negative retained-earnings shocks, higher uncertainty or volatility.
  - Two things happen. The economy moves to lower-yielding investment. Earlier investments are revealed as malinvestments.
  - Result: "a form of stagflation, with no boom, an initial downturn, and a lower rate of economic growth".
  - Aggregate risk afterward may rise or fall. It depends on whether the *switching effect* (to safer projects) outweighs the *increase in riskiness effect*.
  - If switching is strong, the return gap between risky and safe investment widens. Those who can forecast earn extraordinary profits, "just as gold buyers did in the 1970s".
- **Three channels from volatile inflation to volatile real rates** (section "Monetary sources of real economic volatility"):
  1. Volatile loanable funds supply.
  2. Inflation is harder to forecast, so the Fisher effect is less reliable.
  3. Individuals face different price levels, so one nominal rate means many real rates.
- **Booms and busts are symmetric in size, asymmetric in form** (section "Are business cycles and changes in growth rates essentially the same?"):
  - Too little risk gives a slow-growth economy with no cycle. Too much risk gives a cycle.
  - "The costs of business downturns will be correlated inversely with their frequency."
  - Efficient pricing implies "a correlation between sizes of booms and sizes of busts".
  - A smaller risk differential between risky and safe commitments smooths the cycle and raises its frequency.

## Key Concepts
- **Information-sensitivity**: the dependence of an investment's value on future news. It defines a risky investment.
- **Informational decay**: the value of the information behind an investment falls over time. Cowen calls it "the least essential" assumption.
- **Malinvestment**: the wrong *kind* of investment. "The problem is not overinvestment per se."
- **Switching effect**: the move to safer projects after a volatility shock.
- **Tripartite option**: invest, consume or wait.
- **Voluntary downturn**: downturns are accepted ex ante and regretted ex post.
- **Procyclical productivity**: explained by willingness to implement risky innovations, not by technology shocks.

## Mental Models
- Think of a small shock that changes the level of risk as a cheap ticket to Las Vegas. The ex ante change is small. The ex post change in family income can be large.
- Use the hula hoop factory to picture informational decay. The fad pays at once. The long-run return is very uncertain.
- Think of Keynes and Hayek as two special cases of one theory. Both describe a move from risky, high-yield investment to safe, low-yield investment. Keynes blames inadequate market signals. Hayek blames false government signals.
- Think of risk as the "ghost" variable in neo-Keynesian models. Rigidities bind only sometimes. Agents chose their exposure knowing that.

## Anti-patterns
- **Citing a low aggregate interest elasticity as proof that rates do not matter**: "small interest rate changes may induce large shifts in the composition of investment even when the measured interest elasticity for aggregate investment is zero."
- **Citing the Law of Large Numbers against aggregate error**: it confuses multiplication with subdivision of risk.
- **Assuming hedges remove the problem**: interest rate futures shift pecuniary risk. They do not remove the real cost of a wrong allocation. A hedging market may *raise* cyclicality by raising risk tolerance.
- **Expecting every downturn to follow a boom**: "downturns are not always preceded by supra-normal booms."
- **Treating cycles and growth-rate changes as the same thing**: they share a cause and differ in form.

## Worked Example
Hercowitz (1986) simulation, as Cowen reports it (section on comovement):
- Setup: an artificial economy calibrated on Canada, with time to build. The representative period of production is fourteen to sixteen months. Real rate shocks come from US data.
- Mechanism: when production takes time, a lower real rate raises the productivity of capital. Investment, capital utilization and labor demand rise.
- Result with the real rate defined on the producer price index: a 1 percentage point rise in the real rate lowers output by 0.53 percent and labor input by 0.28 percent.
- Result with the real rate defined on the consumer price index: output falls 0.91 percent and labor input falls 0.39 percent.
- Use: consumption, investment, labor supply and production "all comove conversely with the real interest rate".
- Contrast in footnote 8: Merrick (1984), with no time to build, gets countercyclical new investment, which contradicts the data.

## Key Takeaways
1. Cheap money raises the chosen level of risk. It does so through substitution, wealth and composition effects.
2. The boom is real in expectation. The bust is one of two possible resolutions.
3. Look at the mix of investment, not the total.
4. A volatility shock contracts long-term investment at once and needs no prior boom.
5. Errors cluster because entrepreneurs forecast the same variables.
6. Big booms and big busts come from the same risk differential.
7. The US downturn "lasts for one to three years" in the author's stylized description.

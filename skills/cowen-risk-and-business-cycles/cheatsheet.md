# Cheatsheet: Risk and Business Cycles (Cowen)

Every line is the author's position. Evidence is pre-1997 and second-hand.

## Decision rules

| When | Conclude | Because | Cite |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real rates fall or finance constraints ease | Risk-taking and cyclicality rise. A bust is possible, not certain | Substitution, wealth and composition effects | Ch 2 |
| Loanable funds supply turns volatile | Long-term investment contracts at once. No boom first | Option value of waiting rises | Ch 2, Ch 3 |
| Money growth is high and volatile together | Stagflation, if the volatility effect dominates | Two opposing effects on long-term investment | Ch 3 |
| Easing is announced in advance | The boom comes sooner | Entrepreneurs adjust before the money arrives | Ch 3 |
| Banks ration credit | Watch loan quantities, not measured real rates | Policy works through availability | Ch 3 |
| Policy eases under rationing | Credit share moves to riskier firms | Risky borrowers were the constrained ones | Ch 3 |
| Only the short rate falls | Lenders see the funds as temporary | The long rate signals permanence | Ch 4 |
| Someone says "low rates, so malinvestment" | Ask for the misread signal | The claim needs thirteen assumptions | Ch 4 |
| A regime targets prices, nominal rates or nominal GNP | Shocks to that target now drive loanable funds | Money supply is "hostage" to them | Ch 3 |
| A statistic uses price inflation | Do not apply it to money growth | Price inflation carries negative real shocks | Ch 5 |

## Opposing forces on long-term investment

| Force | Direction | Cite |
|---|---|---|
| Lower real rate | Up | Ch 2 |
| Easier finance constraints | Up | Ch 2, Ch 3 |
| Higher retained earnings | Up | Ch 2 |
| Uncertainty resolved | Up | Ch 2 |
| Higher risk tolerance | Up | Ch 2 |
| Higher volatility of money or real rates | Down | Ch 2, Ch 3 |
| Lower real rate read as weak investment demand | Down or flat | Ch 4 |

## Traditional Austrian versus risk-based

| Point | Traditional Austrian | Risk-based (Cowen) |
|---|---|---|
| Expectations | Naive and elastic | Rational |
| Error | Systematic, toward too-long projects | In all directions |
| Culprit | Positive money growth | Volatile money growth, or any risk-raising shift |
| After easy money | Unsustainable boom, then bust | Boom. Then either a lasting boom or a bust |
| After volatile money | Malinvestment expands | Immediate contraction |
| Welfare | Inflation distorts | A move between Pareto points |
| Capital-intensity | Required concept | Not required |

## Tells

- Credit share shifting to small or risky borrowers: easing is raising aggregate risk (Ch 3, Ch 5).
- Ratio of risky to safe loans rising: loose policy, and it leads GDP (Lang and Nakamura 1995, Ch 5).
- T-Bill/commercial paper spread widening: output decline ahead (Ch 5).
- Commercial paper issuance rising while bank loans lag: tight money or cash flow strain (Ch 5).
- Durable, construction-linked and capital goods sectors moving most: the monetary channel is active (Ch 5).
- Large booms: expect large busts if the bets fail. The same risk differential drives both (Ch 2).
- A wide return gap between risky and safe investment after a volatility shock: high payoff for anyone who can forecast (Ch 2).

## What the theory will not tell you

- Whether a given boom ends in a bust (Ch 1, Ch 3).
- The turning point. Money sets vulnerability. A real shock sets the date (Ch 5).
- Whether the current level of risk is too high. That needs a view on externalities (Ch 3).
- Whether the low-rate effect or the volatility effect is larger (Ch 4).

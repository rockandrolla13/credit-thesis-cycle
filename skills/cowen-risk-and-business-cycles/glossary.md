# Glossary: Risk and Business Cycles (Cowen)

**Accelerator model** — investment demand is a multiple of consumer goods demand. Needs initially unemployed resources. The data "do not unambiguously reject" it (Ch 2, Ch 5)
**Adjustment costs** — costs of moving factors of production to other uses (Ch 1)
**Austrian Claim, the** — expansionary monetary policy induces capital-intensity to expand too much (Ch 1, Ch 4)
**Bad news principle** — the value of waiting rises with the spread of potential negative results. Bernanke 1983a (Ch 5)
**Balanced budget open market operation** — one that reverses the initial asset exchange. Fully neutral (Ch 3)
**Bank lending channel** — monetary policy eases or tightens banks' own liquidity constraints and so their loan supply (Ch 3, Ch 5)
**Capital goods** — inputs used for marketplace production, broadly defined to include consumer durables, human capital and land (Ch 1)
**Capital-intensity** — a multi-dimensional idea combining how much capital is invested and for how long. No globally transitive metric exists (Ch 4, Appendix C)
**Capital reswitching** — a project is favored at two distant interest rates and not in between (Ch 4, Appendix C)
**Capital reversing** — a decline in the real rate lowers the value of the capital stock. Reswitching in another metric (Ch 4, Appendix C)
**Comovement** — most sectors rise and fall together. The classic problem for investment-based theories (Ch 2)
**Credit rationing** — banks set rates below market-clearing levels, cap loan sizes and turn away some identical borrowers. Stiglitz and Weiss 1981 (Ch 3)
**Elastic expectations** — entrepreneurs overestimate the permanence of observed price and interest rate changes (Ch 4)
**Fisher effect** — nominal interest rates rise with expected inflation. The data show a less than one-for-one rise (Ch 4, Ch 5)
**Forced savings** — inflation entering through banks bids resources from consumption to capital goods. The second-round version is "inessential" (Ch 4, Appendix A)
**Informational decay** — the value of the information behind an investment falls over time. Assumption 5 (Ch 2)
**Information-sensitivity** — dependence of an investment's value on future revelations about the world (Ch 1, Ch 2)
**Injection effects** — real effects that arise because new money enters at specific points in space and time (Ch 3)
**Intertemporal coordination** — the match between investment plans and later purchase plans (Ch 1)
**Liquidity effect** — money growth lowers interest rates for a time (Ch 3, Ch 5)
**Loanable funds theory** — supply and demand for loans jointly set real and nominal rates (Ch 3)
**Malinvestment** — an investment whose adjustment and misallocation costs push its return below the risk-free rate (Ch 1)
**Minimum-noise interest rate** — the rate set by a process that is easy to predict. A property of a regime, not of a level (Ch 4)
**Mirror image theory** — entrepreneurs fear inflation, misread real savings as inflation, and underinvest (Ch 4)
**Misallocation costs** — costs of keeping resources in non-ideal uses because adjustment is too costly (Ch 1)
**Monetary scenario** — unexpected money growth lowers real rates or eases finance constraints and raises risky investment and cyclicality (Ch 3, Ch 5)
**Monetary volatility scenario** — higher money supply volatility raises risk, shifts investment to lower-yielding uses and causes a downturn with no prior boom (Ch 3, Ch 5)
**Naive expectations** — entrepreneurs underestimate the probability that monetary inflation explains the data (Ch 4)
**Natural rate of interest** — Wicksell's concept. Cowen calls it "incomplete" because the market rate always clears the loan market (Ch 4)
**New Monetary Economics** — systems that separate the medium of exchange from the medium of account. Hall 1982 (Ch 3)
**Option value of waiting** — the gain from delaying an irreversible commitment. Rises with volatility (Ch 2, Ch 4)
**Rational expectations** — forecasts are efficient and unbiased. Errors are serially uncorrelated. Errors need not be small (Ch 1)
**Ricardo effect** — substitution of labor for machinery as real wages fall. Rejected as a cycle mechanism (Ch 4, Appendix B)
**Risk-based theory** — policy or other sectoral shifts may make the economy riskier and more cyclical. Entrepreneurs do not err on average (Ch 1)
**Risky investment** — long-term, costly to reverse, high-yielding, information-sensitive (Ch 1)
**Romer shocks** — six episodes of deliberate Fed disinflation used as exogenous shocks (Ch 3, Ch 5)
**Rounds of fund recipients** — first: banks. Second: borrowers who invest. Third: owners of the factors the borrowers hire (Ch 4)
**Safe investment** — more liquid, lower yielding, less information-sensitive. Includes consumption and T-Bills (Ch 1, Ch 2)
**Signal extraction problems (three)** — real versus nominal. Rates as forecasts of expenditure streams. Temporary versus permanent (Ch 4)
**Stagflation** — high inflation with low growth. Arises when high and volatile money growth occur together and volatility dominates (Ch 2, Ch 3)
**Switching effect** — the move toward safer projects after a volatility shock (Ch 2)
**Time to build** — the lag from investment decision to completion. Kydland and Prescott 1982 (Ch 1, Ch 2)
**Traditional Austrian theory** — positive nominal money growth induces unsustainable increases in long-term investment (Ch 1, Ch 4)
**Tripartite option** — the choice among investing, consuming and waiting (Ch 2)
**Wicksell effect** — expansionary monetary policy lowers the real interest rate (Ch 3)

# Chapter 3: The Wizards of Oz

## Core Idea
Intelligence from insiders and experts is necessary background but a poor forecasting method on its own. Use the constraint framework to judge which expert input is diagnostic. "You cannot outsource your fiduciary duty."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Five limitations of intelligence-based forecasting**:
  1. *Not statistically significant.* The consultant is not in actual intelligence and lacks the volume of cross-checked signals an agency has. US intelligence spending is roughly $60 billion, about a third to the CIA. Even the CIA errs.
  2. *The questions moved from the frontier to the core.* In G20 systems the constraints on power are too great. A connected contact can mislead by reinforcing your bias and giving "artificially high conviction in an inaccurate forecast".
  3. *Funneling.* A handful of sources, or one, gets oversimplified and mass-distributed. If the insight is right, the market already priced part of it. If it is wrong, "the result of funneling is carnage".
  4. *Reflexivity.* Policymakers use markets to force action from counterparts and "talk their own book". Treat an official like a CEO on a quarterly call.
  5. *The false expert.* The person on air may be the most available person, not the best.
- **The humility test for a consultant**: a high-conviction view "without a scenario analysis or a probability decision tree" signals overstated, statistically insignificant insight. Real experts give scenarios plus data points to monitor, or a decision tree with probabilities. They downplay their sources. Subpar analysts stress their personal network to seem indispensable.
- **Three kinds of expert worth seeking**:
  - *Academics*: someone who spent 20 years on the topic, not the op-ed-writing stars. Put their view into your own net assessment. Never let them tell you what the market will do.
  - *Technocrats*: second- and third-tier former officials, not "top dogs", who are often ideological, legacy-driven and cut off from the analysts who made them useful.
  - *Regulators*: former agency staff for sector-specific questions. Avoid firms claiming to predict regulation with big data.
- **Three-level game**: an extension of Putnam's two-level game. Boris Johnson negotiated with the EU, faced the domestic opposition, and had to settle an internal Tory split between deal and no-deal Brexiters.

## Key Concepts
- **Funneling**: see above.
- **Selling / talking their book**: stating a preference because the statement itself pays, whatever the later action.
- **Nondiagnostic statement**: one whose benefits to the speaker hold "regardless of whether his later actions matched" it.
- **Misery index**: unemployment plus inflation. "A crude measure of political risk". Its flaw is that some countries run chronically high readings.
- **Iterative versus one-off polls**: repeated polls by agencies with a country track record beat single polls from data aggregators. Campaign polls "almost always overstate" the candidate.
- **Mosaic**: the cross-checked body of human, electronic and signals intelligence that makes agency forecasts statistically meaningful.
- **First-past-the-post**: each district goes to the plurality winner. A split on one side of the spectrum hands seats to the other.

## Mental Models
- Treat an insider as a prisoner of their own material reality. The author believed Downing Street insiders sincerely expected no-deal. "I just ignored what they thought was going to happen."
- Use a leader's loudest hawkish line as a possible buy signal when the hawkishness serves a domestic audience.
- Think of a missing data series as information. Pollsters "going dark" in Argentina was a warning.
- "All policymakers are" hypocrites. "They push the envelope as far as their constraints allow them."

## Anti-patterns
- **Forecasters in the media**: citing Tetlock, experts who appear often in news media forecast especially badly. Media wants "pithy, glib, extreme, and highly confident".
- **Forecasters with a weekly column**: they are unlikely to write that a crisis is irrelevant.
- **Forecasters who prescribe policy**: "The moment forecasters pollute their analysis with policy prescription, I no longer take their forecasts seriously."
- **Linking discrete events**: investors cut the odds of tax reform because Obamacare repeal failed, "even though the two were discrete events".
- **Name-dropping consultants**: three names before the next breath means stop listening.
- **Ideological attachment to a story**: the Macri reform story fit investors' supply-side priors, so they needed "little convincing".

## Worked Example: betting against no-deal Brexit (2019)
1. **Market price**: no-deal odds near 45% in summer 2019.
2. **Intelligence**: uniformly bearish, sourced close to or inside Downing Street.
3. **Reframe**: Johnson faced a three-level game. The binding level was internal. The Brexit Party, founded six months earlier, led the polls in June. Under first-past-the-post, a Brexit Party near 20% would wreck the Tories. UKIP's best was 12.6% in 2015.
4. **Inference**: at most about a quarter of the electorate backed severing ties (Brexit Party peak of 26%). Governing on a 26% policy would be folly, but campaigning on it to reunite the right was rational.
5. **Diagnosticity check**: the bravado paid off for Johnson whether or not he followed through. So it was nondiagnostic.
6. **Call**: bet against the rise in no-deal probability. The "dead in a ditch" line was, in retrospect, a buy signal.
7. **Result**: election majority on 12 December 2019, Brexit Party at 2%, deal passed.

Background from footnote 20: May's deal failed because she lost her majority in 2017 and depended on the DUP's 10 MPs.

## Key Takeaways
1. Buy expert knowledge to learn "the stuff you don't have time to learn". Do the forecast yourself.
2. Demand scenarios, probabilities and monitoring variables from any provider.
3. Ask whether a statement would be made regardless of intent. If yes, discard it as evidence.
4. Ask who else has the same "insight". If everyone does, you face funneling risk on both outcomes.
5. Public, crude, observable data (a misery index, a caucus chair's public remark) beat private access in the cases given.
6. Complex political systems "cannot be analyzed as if they are banana republics".

# Chapter 7: Constitutional and Legal Constraints: The Constraint-Preference Hybrids

## Core Idea
Law binds citizens but is malleable in policymakers' hands. Legal and constitutional constraints sit on "the bottom rung" of the hierarchy and often behave like preferences. Two things still make them worth study: an *absent* legal constraint is itself a risk, and a procedural rule can remove a political constraint.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The hierarchy of constraints (most explicit statement in the book)**: "On the hierarchy of constraints, constitutional and legal issues take the bottom rung. If these are the fulcrum constraints of your forecast, you are probably wrong." A net assessment "never begins with a legal analysis. It begins with an understanding of the first-string constraints: political, economic, financial, and geopolitical. Only at the end do constitutional and legal matters come to the forefront."
- **Constraint-preference hybrid**: laws shift between two states, constraint or preference, "depending on the environment" (the author's image is a non-Newtonian fluid). "Like preferences, for policymakers they are subject to all other constraints."
- **Rule for when to use legal analysis**: "only when all other constraints fail to elucidate the situation", meaning when they do not eliminate any hypothesis.
- **Rule for weak-rule-of-law states**: where norms are loosely followed, the legal constraint "is irrelevant, and I treat it as a pure preference". Example given: Russian presidential term limits.
- **Absence of a legal constraint as the risk**: on US trade, "The absence of a legal and legislative constraint to trade war was the risk." With no constraint in the way, the outcome rests on a preference, and "betting on preferences, therefore, was folly".
- **Law as an enabler that removes a political constraint**: reconciliation let a 52-seat Senate majority pass tax cuts. "In this special case, legal constraints eliminated both interparty and partisan political constraints."
- **Retroactive normalization**: policymakers break or stretch the rule first and legalize it later. EFSF → ESM. Nixon's 1971 surcharge → Trade Act of 1974.

## Key Concepts
- **"The constitution is not a suicide pact"**: other constraints come first. If the political capital exists and the stakes are large, "policymakers will scrap or rewrite the rules. Quickly."
- **Budget resolution**: passed by both houses, not sent to the president, not law. It sets guidelines. Due by 15 April under the timetable in effect since 1987.
- **Appropriations bill**: the "budget bill" that funds departments. Due by the fiscal year start on 1 October. Congress faces no penalty for missing either date.
- **Continuing resolution**: extends prior appropriations at the same level.
- **Budget reconciliation**: introduced by the Congressional Budget Act of 1974. Limits Senate debate to 20 hours, so no filibuster. The bill must change federal revenue or spending.
- **Cloture**: ending debate. Needs 60 Senate votes.
- **Byrd rule**: strikes extraneous non-budget provisions, and forces any provision to "sunset" if it raises the deficit beyond the years the bill covers. Waiving it needs three-fifths of the Senate (60 votes).
- **Dynamic scoring**: revenue estimation that counts the "macroeconomic feedback" of tax cuts, on the theory that they pay for themselves.

## Mental Models
- Treat a legal red line in a crisis as the weakest link. "Compared to the strength of all other constraints pushing against the euro's failure, a flimsy legal mandate was most easily broken."
- Map the executive's delegated powers before assuming the legislature will check it.
- Read a procedural rule for what it reveals about political constraints. Reconciliation mattered "because it allowed the investor to gauge the scenario's political constraints".
- "Be wary of any macro investment view that is overly technical, legal, or reliant on constitutionality."

## Anti-patterns
- **Getting hung up on technicalities**: the 2016 UK referendum was nonbinding and consultative, yet "legal constraints bent to its much stronger political constraints".
- **Taking "revenue neutrality" rhetoric at face value** in 2017.
- **Remembering half the rule history**: clients recalled the Democrats' post-2006 ban on deficit-raising reconciliation but not the Republican reversal in 2015.
- **Assuming Congress checks trade policy**: it delegated that power from the 1930s on.

## Worked Example: forecasting the trade war (November 2016 to 2017)
1. **Consensus after the November 2016 election**: a pro-trade Congress would check Trump's trade threats.
2. **Constraint check, top down**: many Trump priorities "would die in Congress" for lack of a filibuster-proof Senate majority. That pushed him toward foreign policy and trade, where the executive acts alone.
3. **Legal check**: a table of statutes (compiled by Matt Gertken) showed no legal or legislative constraint on presidential tariffs. History agreed: Nixon in 1971, and Obama used selective tariffs from his first month.
4. **Call**: "Trump had no constraints to pursue a trade war with China."
5. **Apparent refutation**: the April 2017 Mar-a-Lago summit put the trade war on hold. Critics said the view was wrong.
6. **Author's reply**: at Mar-a-Lago a preference for deal-making prevailed. The risk was the missing constraint. If the preference changed, nothing stood in the way.
7. **What happened, per text**: Trump used Section 301 against China and Section 232 against others, allies included. Ch 2's event table dates "Trump declares trade war" at 2018-03-01.

## Worked Example: the 2017 tax cut (political first, legal last)
1. **Political constraints**: near zero. Republican voters accept Republican deficits and the Freedom Caucus signalled consent (Ch 3). This did not eliminate the remaining doubt, so the author turned to law.
2. **Legal question**: could reconciliation pass a deficit-widening bill with 52 Senate seats?
3. **Findings**: reconciliation bars the filibuster. The 2015 rule change allows deficit increases. The Byrd rule only forces a sunset. Dynamic scoring supplies the claim that cuts pay for themselves. "At worst, the CBO's score would force the Republicans to 'sunset' tax reform legislation, but not scuttle it."
4. **Offsets on offer** (border-adjustment tax, ending business interest deductibility, ending the state and local income tax deduction) were "either draconian or insufficient".
5. **Path of least resistance**: bust the budget, sunset the provisions, invoke dynamic scoring.
6. **Why this differed from Obamacare repeal**: strong political constraints blocked repeal. Law built to bypass those constraints enabled the tax cut.
7. **Outcome**: passed 51-48 on 19 December 2017 (Ch 3).

## Forward call recorded in the chapter (written January to June 2020)
A President Biden with a Democratic House and a slim Senate majority, even a 50-50 tie broken by the vice president, can use reconciliation to raise corporate taxes, build on Obamacare, and raise capital gains taxes. "And he most certainly will do that." The book does not report the outcome.

## Key Takeaways
1. Run legal analysis last. Use it to break a tie the stronger constraints leave open.
2. In a crisis, assume legal limits give way if political, economic and geopolitical constraints push against them.
3. Check for *missing* legal constraints. They tell you where preference alone decides, which is where forecasts are least reliable.
4. Procedural detail can be decisive, and it needs "immensely boring" research. Most clients in 2017 had the reconciliation rules wrong.
5. US shutdowns cited: three days in January 2018, and 35 days from late 2018 to early 2019, the longest on record.

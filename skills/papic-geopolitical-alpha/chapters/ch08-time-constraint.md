# Chapter 8: The Time Constraint: When Preferences Run Down the Clock

## Core Idea
The constraint framework has a blind spot. Collective psychology can delay a constraint-based outcome for an unknowable time. The framework tells you *what* must happen, not *when*. Terrorism (2014-2016) and COVID-19 (2020) are the two "constraint wild cards" used to show this.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Time as the Achilles' heel**: "The constraint framework has a blind spot: the power of market participants' collective psychology to run down the clock on constraint-based forecasts." Alpha depends on whether the forecast event comes too early, too late or on time.
- **The weakest link is the median voter's zeitgeist**: political constraints rest on the median voter. That works when the median is moderate. In a panic "the outlier becomes the median". It is "a median voter preference gone wild".
- **Hysteria as a material constraint**: "fear creates its own reality". "A hysterical society becomes a material constraint in itself." The median voter reins in policymakers, but nothing reins in the median voter quickly. "The time it takes for an entire society to return to sanity is unknowable, and impossible to forecast."
- **Near versus far constraints**: mass fear is "an imminent constraint". A depression is "further down the risk curve". A rational policymaker can be forced to obey the nearer one even at disastrous long-run cost.
- **The tactical/strategic rule**: "React as a tactical investor to the narrative and as a strategic investor to the constraints."
- **The Unit Cost of Fear**: "Fear is cheap, until it isn't." Fear-led behaviour buckles as its socioeconomic cost rises. The binding cost is money: "The real constraint is money and, in particular, the lack of it." The author expected it to bite "with the first round of layoffs".
- **Desensitization**: populations get used to a repeated, bounded threat. Each later terror attack had a lower "return on investment". Its limit: an airborne virus with a 30% mortality rate would not allow it.
- **Rational policymaker objection, answered**: clients say policymakers are irrational. "Even an irrational policymaker cannot run through a wall." The real flaw is that the *collective* can be irrational.
- **Social media as "a narrative accelerant"**: it amplifies the most extreme narratives, but their "half-life is shorter". Markets react sharply and can recover as fast.
- **Numerator/denominator rule for early outbreak data**: deaths are recorded in hospitals and so are near reality. Infections are badly undercounted early. So early mortality rates are overstated. Precedents cited: H1N1 in 2009, Ebola in 2014.
- **Order-of-effects reasoning**: doctors are bound by the Hippocratic Oath to first-order effects. Policymakers are constrained by second-, third- and fourth-order effects. "Doctors don't make public policy. Policymakers do."

## Key Concepts
- **Constraint wild cards**: terrorists and pandemics (named in Ch 2).
- **Seeing "around the curve"**: the framework looks past the prevailing media narrative. Markets trade the narrative first.
- **Flatten the curve**: keep hospitalizations under capacity by prolonging distancing, perhaps for the 18 months a vaccine needs. The author calls its supporters "Flattenistas".
- **Demand hysteresis**: consumers retrench permanently after a shock impairs balance sheets, as opposed to labor hysteresis.
- **Vertical distancing**: isolating the elderly from the rest. The author expected it to be legislated. It happened through behaviour instead (Figure 8.8).
- **I-neck recovery**: steeper than a V. Durable goods consumption regained its high in two months, against six years after the 2008 crisis.
- **"G thang"**: the rally was fiscal. GDP = C + G + I + NX. "Monetary stimulus has been subservient to the fiscal."

## Mental Models
- Treat a leader's dismissive early statement as possibly right on the material facts and still wrong for forecasting, because "the material reality did not matter" over the forecastable horizon. Obama's "J.V. team" remark (January 2014) and Trump's "hoax" remark (28 February 2020) are the paired cases.
- Be wary of official models in nonlinear settings. They miss behavioural change, and some are built to scare. The CDC's 2014 Ebola forecast was 1.4 million infected and 100,000 dead within months. The outcome was 28,646 infected and 11,323 deaths.
- Check a chart's categories and units before trusting it. The CDC's 20-44 "young adult" bracket spanned 25 years against 10 for the others and showed absolute counts, not rates (Figure 8.5).
- Watch the policy response, not the shock. "The main story of 2020 is not COVID-19, but the gargantuan stimulus that has followed it."

## Anti-patterns
- **Focusing "too much on the material reality, as opposed to the zeitgeist of market participants"**: the author's own error, twice.
- **Trading daily new cases** once deaths and hospitalizations had decoupled from them.
- **Sizing sectors by headlines**: the sectors "going to zero" (airlines, restaurants, cruise lines, car rental and similar) were only 5.4% of GDP.
- **Linear extrapolation from limited data**: the Imperial College study and one blog post drove the harshest measures.
- **Calling the rally "money printing" or "retail led"**.

## Worked Example: is COVID-19 a depression? (March 2020)
1. **Admit the timing miss.** The framework did not say when panic would start or end.
2. **Ask the question constraints can answer**: depression or not. That decides between another 30-50% equity fall and a bottom.
3. **Three constraints that make the crisis manageable**:
   - *COVID-19 targets the elderly.* Concentrated risk permits less damaging management, by policy or by behaviour.
   - *The cost of fear is not constant.* The Unit Cost of Fear rises.
   - *The median voter leans left.* So the policy response is "much larger and faster than investors anticipate" (Ch 4).
4. **Price the alternative.** Indefinite lockdown ends business investment and triggers mass layoffs. The US consumer is about 15% of global GDP. A large share of the US has a negative savings rate.
5. **Count second-order deaths.** A 2016 *Lancet* study attributes over 260,000 excess OECD cancer deaths to the 2008 recession. A 2018 *Lancet* study finds Greek mortality rose 17.8% between 2010 and 2016, three times Western Europe's rise. The author scales a 20% rise to "about 2.5 to 3 million extra deaths annually" in the US.
6. **Count third- and fourth-order effects.** The 1930s analogue: multipolarity, an isolationist US, challenger powers. The chain he draws is "deglobalization, populism, jingoism, and finally a world war".
7. **Conclusion**: "An economic depression is such a massive constraint on policymakers that the trajectory of flatten-the-curve policy will ultimately have to change."
8. **Stated falsifier**: "If pursued to its ultimate conclusion, it will cause a depression and thus prove me wrong."
9. **Why cases and deaths diverged by June** (his three guesses): more testing, lower viral load, changed behaviour by the elderly. If severity stays blunted, "daily new cases become irrelevant from the market's perspective".

## Key Takeaways
1. A correct constraint view can lose money for a long time. Size and time positions for the narrative, hold the strategic view from the constraints.
2. Ask what will raise the cost of the prevailing fear, and when ordinary households feel it.
3. Check whether a threat's features allow desensitization. A bounded, concentrated risk does. A 30% mortality rate would not.
4. In a crisis, forecast the policy reaction function. In 2020 it was set by the Buenos Aires Consensus, not by the virus.
5. The most important 2020 chart, in the author's view, is Figure 8.12: after 2008 monetary stimulus met fiscal austerity, after 2017 fiscal stimulus met hawkish monetary policy, and in 2020 the two moved "in lockstep".
6. The author says he can forecast a society's behaviour with the framework, "just not the behavior's end date".

# Chapter 4: Politics

## Core Idea
Political constraints come first. "Power is the most important constraint", above wealth, the economy, markets, geopolitics and demographics. Measure it in the short term with an ad hoc reading of political capital, and in the long term with the median voter theorem.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Political capital (ad hoc gauge, six factors)**. No accepted index exists. The author reads six factors case by case:
  1. *Popularity.* Public support is capital in the bank. Authoritarian regimes care even more because the cost of lost credibility is worse than a lost election.
  2. *Time in power.* Capital decays with mileage. New leaders have a "honeymoon period", the time for unpalatable policy. A 2019 IMF study found reforms early in a term do not affect election prospects. Leaders turn risk averse as elections near.
  3. *Legislative math.* Without a majority the leader must buy coalition partners or the opposition. A minority coalition is "the weakest option in a parliamentary democracy".
  4. *Economic context.* Tailwinds help incumbents. A crisis hurts the incumbent but helps a *new* leader push reform (Bolsonaro's pension reform; Rajoy after late 2011). Macri, an incumbent, did not benefit.
  5. *Special interest group support.* Organized groups solve the free-rider problem (Olson) and are costly opponents.
  6. *Global momentum.* Policies trending globally are easier to pass (Huntington's "regional contingency factor").
  - Scope: this gauge forecasts policy "over the course of a policymaker's term but not beyond".
- **The J curve of structural reform** (Figure 4.1): unpopular policy drains political capital first and pays later. The trough is the "danger zone", "a moment of truth when the populace may reject the policy". Macri failed to exit it in 2019.
- **Fulcrum constraint** (footnote 17): "When a constraint is so powerful that it bends all other factors to it." If it changes at all, the whole analysis may have to pivot. It is "the most diagnostic of all variables".
- **Median Voter Theorem (MVT)**: to win or keep power, parties and politicians approximate the policy choices of the median voter. The author uses it as a quantifiable proxy for political capital over long horizons.
  - Three assumptions, each of which the author doubts: (1) voter preferences are single-peaked, (2) preferences lie on a single policy dimension, (3) politicians want political capital above all else.
  - The three-step process it implies for a politician:
    1. Identify the central issue of the day.
    2. Ascertain the median voter's position on that issue.
    3. Asymptotically approach that position, outflanking opponents.
  - Forecasting rule: "to forecast policy, focus on the median voter, not the policymaker." If the median voter moves, *all* parties move.
  - Time rule: MVT "allows for policy to deviate from the median voter in the short term, but not in the long term". Coalitions, gerrymandering and party prerogatives mask it in the short run.
- **The Buenos Aires Consensus**: the author's name for the move away from the Washington Consensus. The Washington Consensus "sought to remove democracy from economic policy" through independent central banks, counter-cyclical fiscal policy and laissez-faire regulation. Now "democracy is finding its way back into economic policy". The mentality is "growth above all else".
- **The median citizen (MVT without votes)**: in China the proxy for the median voter is "popular middle-class support". One tenet does not carry over: there is no short-term competition from an opposition, so leaders are "less subject to the 'time-in-power' constraint".

## Key Concepts
- **Power**: "a force that compels others to do something they would prefer not to do."
- **Traveling with events**: letting news coverage set what you analyze.
- **Third Way**: left parties adopting center-right economics in the late 1980s and 1990s, read as the median voter moving right.
- **The Three Represents** (Jiang Zemin): the party represents advanced productive forces, advanced culture, and "the interests of the vast majority of the people". The author reads this as approaching the median citizen.
- **Middle-income trap**: the growth stall the CCP fears, because Latin American regimes and the USSR did not survive theirs.
- **Price maker / price taker**: "The median voter is the price maker in the political marketplace. Politicians are price takers."
- **Tetlock's high scorers**: those who "know many small things" and practice flexible "ad hocery", against those who "know one big thing".

## Mental Models
- Think of the median voter as "the political zeitgeist of the nation". Buchanan's 1992 platform lost and Trump's similar 2016 platform won because the zeitgeist moved.
- Watch the opposition as a second reading of the median voter. In 2020 the US and UK left did not move to the center to outflank Trump and Johnson.
- Read a party-line vote as a forecast. The strict party-line ARRA vote in 2009 "suggested that no future stimulus bills would be forthcoming".
- Treat a one-party state as having abundant short-term political capital but a real long-term popularity constraint.

## Anti-patterns
- **Counting only the first domino**: forecasters priced Johnson's rhetoric and not its purpose.
- **Blaming COVID-19 for the end of laissez-faire**: "the zeitgeist had already turned".
- **Assuming a "right-wing" label means laissez-faire policy**: the author asks which laissez-faire tenet Trump practices and answers none.
- **Using MVT where there is no accountability at all**: "In assessing the policies of North Korean ruler Kim Jong-un, MVT is useless."
- **One-theory forecasting**: Tetlock's worst forecasters.

## Worked Example: the Buenos Aires Consensus forecast (written Q1 to late June 2020)
1. **Central issue and median voter**: the middle class deteriorated and the Debt Supercycle ended in 2010. Footnote 25 adds a generational driver: in 2020 the US median voter is, for the first time, a Millennial.
2. **Evidence the median voter moved left on economics**: Trump and post-Cameron Tories abandon fiscal prudence, free trade and central bank independence. The left does not tack to the center. Biden's platform would have been called socialism eight years earlier.
3. **Short-run deviation and its price**: the December 2017 tax cut did not match the median voter and was "a disastrous electoral pitch" in the November 2018 midterms.
4. **Fulcrum**: "the left-moving median voter will be the fulcrum constraint limiting policymakers' response options." Trump won in 2016 with unemployment at 4.7%, so even a brief recession gets a 2008-scale response.
5. **Forecasts made** (dated by the author):
   - Late June 2020: a further $1.5-$3 trillion of US stimulus by end-2020, taking the budget deficit "to as high as 30% of GDP".
   - Do not fear the 2021 fiscal cliff. Policymakers will fill it.
   - Over the next decade: more unorthodox fiscal and monetary policy, more antitrust cases, selective regulatory pressure, higher taxes on capital gains and high incomes.
   - The transition "will dominate markets over the next decade", more than US-China rivalry, European integration risk or technology.
6. **Investment view stated**: the end of US equities' cyclical outperformance. Underweight US assets on a multi-year horizon. "The dollar has probably peaked in 2020." Stay bullish US equities in absolute terms, but reposition toward European, Japanese and EM equities "on a decade-long strategic asset allocation". The author says this holds "no matter who wins the 2020 election".
7. **Outcome**: the book was finalized in mid-2020. It does not report how these calls resolved.

## Worked Example: China and the median citizen
- **Constraint**: as of 2017 the middle class was over 50% of the population (Figure 4.9). Coastal wealth already exceeds Korea's and Taiwan's when they left authoritarian rule (Figure 4.10). GDP per capita is just under $10,000, right below where most effective democracies sit (Figure 4.11, Freedom House scale 1 best to 7 worst).
- **Evidence the constraint binds**: the self-imposed 2017-2020 slowdown, deleveraging, the crackdown on shadow banking and provincial revenue raising, anticorruption, pollution control, changes to the one-child policy and Hukou. Xi's "housing is for living, not speculation" is kept even at the cost of a slower COVID-19 recovery, because rising coastal property prices would build a vocal opposition, as in Hong Kong.
- **Out-of-consensus forecast**: China's middle class "will force China away from geopolitical excitement. It will retreat inward and back off from its competition with the US." Evidence cited from 2019-2020: restraint in Hong Kong, the Phase One deal (called "a capitulation"), fewer South China Sea incidents.
- **Stated risk to the view**: "the greatest risk to global order and peace is American miscalculation of Chinese intentions." If the US threatens China's escape from the middle-income trap, "Beijing would have to respond with aggression".
- **Second stated risk**: centralizing power removes the provinces as a scapegoat.
- **Litmus test the author sets**: whether the reform effort "survives beyond 2020".

## Key Takeaways
1. Start every forecast with domestic politics. It sets the maneuvering room in international conflicts as well.
2. Run the six-factor audit and look for the one factor that bends the rest.
3. Use MVT for multi-year policy direction and the political-capital audit for within-term calls.
4. A higher-than-consensus probability is enough. You do not need to call the event.
5. Leaders with strong political capital can pay for unpopular reform. That matters for EM currencies, since relative productivity is one of the few long-run currency indicators.
6. The author wants "a back-tested, actionable, political capital index" and says none exists.

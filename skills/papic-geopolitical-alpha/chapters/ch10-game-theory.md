# Chapter 10: Game Theory - It's Not a Game!

## Core Idea
Use game theory only after a full net assessment of every player. "Setting the gameboard is as important as playing on it." Payoffs built from material power alone give precise, wrong answers. The author says he does not use game theory often and wrote the chapter as a list of dos and don'ts.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Setting the gameboard**: "The key to game theory is in assessing which actor holds the greater capability to change its rival's expected utility function." Expected utility is the weighted average of all possible outcomes. Footnote 2 gives the assumed form U(w) = ln(w), logarithmic utility, "most often the case where material outcomes are concerned".
- **Three factors of relative capability**:
  1. **Material balance of power**: who is less exposed, and who is the customer.
  2. **Risk tolerance**: "The actor willing to risk more is the one with greater interests at stake."
  3. **Credibility**: reputation for follow-through.
- **Two-level game (Putnam)**: domestic politics defines acceptable "win-sets" that are carried to the geopolitical arena. "Politicians cannot conclude foreign agreements that are outside of those domestic win-sets." Figure 10.3 (recovered from PDF p. 259; book p. 239) is a four-circle Venn diagram. Each player has a *Geopolitical Arena* circle and a *Domestic Arena* circle. Their overlap is that player's "Geopolitical/Domestic Win Set". The small central region where both players' win-sets overlap is labelled **WIN-WIN**. "For a win-win outcome, all four arenas must align."
- **Competing tolerance curves** (Figure 10.4, recovered from PDF p. 260; book p. 240): axes are PAIN (vertical) against TIME (horizontal). Two concave curves rise from the origin. Two horizontal lines mark each side's pain threshold. A side capitulates where its curve meets its own threshold.
  - *Pain threshold* (height of the line): "The difference between pain thresholds is determined by material power." America's line is drawn above China's.
  - *Pain tolerance function* (shape of the curve, its "half life"): set by domestic politics. America's curve rises faster. China's rises more slowly.
  - Result as drawn: America's capitulation point comes *earlier in time* than China's, even though China's threshold is lower. The figure's annotation: "China may have a lower pain threshold, but its internal politics and focus on the real economy (as opposed to the markets) allows it to delay when it reaches that capitulation point."
- **Game of chicken, regular and constraint-adjusted**: see the two matrices below. "Game theory is useless without first correctly assessing each actor's relative power."

## Key Concepts
- **Precision versus accuracy**: "Precision is not the same as accuracy, and the presence of one (mathematical precision) does not guarantee the presence of the other."
- **Win-set**: the set of international outcomes a leader's domestic politics will accept. A lost win-set becomes a constraint.
- **Capitulation point**: the time at which accumulated pain reaches the pain threshold.
- **Game of chicken**: both drive straight at each other. Each expects the other to be rational and swerve. "The most dangerous form of game theory", since mutual crash "has a high probability of occurring".
- **Three-dimensional chess**: working out which side is more constrained at home, including interest groups.

## Mental Models
- Ask what vehicle each player is driving before you draw the matrix. Merkel drove "a G-Class Mercedes-Benz SUV" and Tsipras "a tricycle". Italy drives "a finely crafted Italian supercar", safer than a tricycle but "no match for the G-Class Benz".
- Separate how much pain a side can bear from how fast its politics turns pain into surrender. Elections and equity-market sensitivity steepen the curve.
- Treat a leader's past deals as their credibility score. From Beijing's view Trump's "bark appeared stronger than his bite".
- Two-level game theory supports the hierarchy: political constraints "play a key role in determining an actor's capability on the global stage" (footnote 4).

## Anti-patterns
- **Material-power-only games**: "I have read too many game theory-reliant reports on investment that go little beyond material power."
- **Formalizing a topic you do not know**: mathematically elegant, empirically useless.
- **Symmetric payoffs by default**: the standard chicken matrix assumes equal cars.
- **Taking false confidence from the math**: "The mathematics can produce a false sense of confidence in the forecast."

## Worked Example: the US-China trade war (2018-2019)
1. **Consensus through 2018 and 2019**: the US had enough material leverage to force onerous terms.
2. **Gameboard**: score the three capability factors (table above). Then add the two-level game. China has no election calendar and focuses on the real economy, not equities, so it has "fewer domestic political constraints than the US".
3. **Tolerance curves**: China's threshold is lower, but its capitulation point comes later.
4. **Outcome as the text states it**: "The Phase One deal, concluded in late 2019, was not much different from the May 2018 agreement that Beijing offered Washington." The extra 18 months "did not really budge Beijing".

## Worked Example: Greece versus Germany (2015)
1. **Setup**: SYRIZA won in early 2015. Tsipras appointed Yanis Varoufakis, a game theorist, as finance minister. Commentators called it a game of chicken headed for a crash.
2. **Greek constraints** (from the Ch 5 net assessment):
   - *Political*: polls through 2015 showed over 60% of Greeks backing euro membership.
   - *Economic*: exit means a collapsing drachma and surging prices because energy is imported. Any export boom would arrive after "the angry median voter forced Tsipras out".
   - *Geopolitical*: no alternative ally. When Tsipras floated Russian money for an alliance, "Putin rebuffed him".
3. **German position**: by 2015 the ECB had ring-fenced the Mediterranean economies and Greek debt "had been cleansed from Europe's banking sector". Berlin "could play hardball" without risking the Euro Area.
4. **Adjusted matrix**: Figure 10.6.
5. **Call**: Greece was bluffing.
6. **Outcome**: Greece stayed and reformed (Ch 5). "Those who invested based on this dire Euro Area prediction lost the game."

## Forward application: Italy versus the EU (2020-2021)
- Expect Italian populists to "consistently bend their Euroscepticism once they come to power", as Lega did in office between June 2018 and September 2019.
- The difference from Greece: "Germany will have to accommodate Italy more." The COVID-19 recession is "likely to see Germany cross a number of red lines that it never did with Greece, including the adoption of some form of debt mutualization".

## Key Takeaways
1. Do the net assessment of each actor first. The game only arranges what the assessments found.
2. Score material power, risk tolerance and credibility separately. Material power is one factor of three.
3. Add the domestic level. The side with the tighter election calendar and more market-sensitive politics capitulates sooner.
4. Rewrite the crash payoff for each player from their constraints. If one side's crash payoff is ruinous and the other's is minor, the game is not really chicken.
5. Distrust a formal model more as it gets more precise, unless the empirical base is strong.

# Chapter 5: The Economy and the Market

## Core Idea
Macroeconomic fundamentals and market pressure are two distinct constraints that bend policymaker preferences. In the Euro Area crisis they worked as a *centripetal* force, not a centrifugal one. In a multipolar world they also cap how far a trade war can go.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two constraints, reflexively linked (Soros)**:
  - *Macroeconomic constraints.* Read fundamentals as a doctor reads health markers. One red flag is manageable. Several off-course markers call for a deeper investigation of root cause. The author's diagnostic questions:
    1. What are the main drivers of growth?
    2. Is productivity high, or is growth mostly labor-force growth?
    3. Are there structural imbalances (long-term unemployment and underemployment, over-regulation, protection of domestic industry, wealth inequality)?
    4. Is the current account balanced? If not, is the imbalance from export dependency or from too much imported goods and capital?
    5. Does domestic policy encourage creative destruction and competition?
  - *Finance (market) constraints.* The market's job is "to discipline and constrain policymakers". Investor inflows can mask poor fundamentals for a long time, "like ibuprofen masking a fever". Markets are narrative-driven (Shiller), so they amplify both panic and mania. "Bond yields can be more powerful than missiles."
- **The German Question and the euro's logic**: unified Germany (1871) is too big for its neighbors and too small to beat them united. Integration is Germany's non-military answer. A common market cannot last without a common currency because the temptation to devalue is too great. The sequence was the "snake in the tunnel" dollar peg, then a deutschmark peg, then the euro, whose aim was to constrain the Bundesbank's power, not to benefit Germany. "Germany benefited anyway."
- **Thucydides Trap (Graham Allison)**: status quo power versus challenger. War in 12 of 16 cases surveyed. Of the four peaceful cases, three involved deep cultural affinity (Spain after Portugal, US after UK, Germany's rise in twenty-first-century Europe) and one was the Cold War. The hegemon "does not react to intentions, it reacts to capabilities".
- **Relative versus absolute gains by system polarity** (realism; Snidal 1991; Gowa and Mansfield 1993):
  - *Bipolar*: relative gains dominate. A geopolitical externality raises the marginal social cost of imports, so the state imposes a tariff t (Figure 5.9).
  - *Unipolar*: the hegemon cares about absolute gains and small states about relative gains, so small states win "bad deals" from the hegemon. That speeds relative hegemonic decline until the hegemon demands a bigger share. Snidal's dynamic "took 30 years to materialize".
  - *Multipolar*: "States that do not cooperate fall behind other relative gains maximizers that cooperate among themselves." Sensitivity to relative gains falls as the number of players rises above two.
- **The lost-trade fulcrum (Figure 5.10)**: in a multipolar world the tariff-imposing state hands the forgone trade to other great powers. "The US and China's lost trade in a multipolar world is the fulcrum constraint preventing both from an all-out trade war."
- **Why multipolarity is "the least ordered and the most unstable"**, three reasons:
  1. *Math*: more potential conflict dyads, and no permission needed from a rule-setter.
  2. *Lack of coordination*: more veto players.
  3. *Mistakes*: more dice rolled at once, so the system is accident-prone.
- **Three ways forecasters misuse economics and markets**:
  1. *Outdated knowledge base.*
  2. *Assumed 1:1 constraint-policymaker relationship*: linear extrapolation from a single constraint.
  3. *No perception of subtlety*: using only what charts easily on a Bloomberg screen.

## Key Concepts
- **Reflexivity**: investor perceptions alter fundamentals. Example given: currency rally → room to cut rates → growth → more appreciation.
- **Currency smoothie**: the euro blended the deutschmark with weaker currencies, devaluing it "by roughly 20%" against competitors (Figure 5.2).
- **Hartz IV reforms**: German labor reforms of the early 2000s. Unemployment fell from a peak of 11.5% in January 2002 to 4.9%.
- **Ceteris paribus world**: where Grexit advocates lived. Political risk neither precedes nor follows exit and every policymaker has an economics PhD.
- **TARGET2**: the Euro Area's real-time gross settlement system. The imbalance rose from €0.3 trillion in 2010 to nearly €1.5 trillion in 2020.
- **Imperial overstretch**: a declining hegemon keeps defending a sphere sized for its zenith.
- **Silicon Curtain**: the hypothetical tech bifurcation between the US and China.
- **Keynes adage and corollary**: owe a hundred pounds and you have a problem, a million and the bank has, a billion and "everybody has a problem".

## Mental Models
- With a large imbalance "the lender - not the debtor - has the most to lose". So TARGET2 is "one of the sinews that binds European countries together".
- "Unions out of weakness are often the most sustainable." Europe integrates "out of weakness and fear", like the 13 colonies and the Swiss cantons.
- Look for systemic reasons before calling leaders incompetent. Allies traded with Germany before 1914 because each feared losing absolute gains *to each other*.
- "On a long enough time line, the ignorance rate for policymakers falls to zero."
- "Material constraints force policymakers down the path of least resistance." The path is not always visible in advance and does not always lead somewhere good.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading peripheral debt stress as a breakup force** without the core's export dependence on the periphery.
- **Blaming the currency for a structural problem**: "The currency is the straw man."
- **Borrowing an analogy without its macro context**: Argentina 2001 for Greece 2015.
- **Assuming red lines hold** "when Euro Area death is on the line" (Figure 5.14).
- **Acronym doom-hunting**: confirmation bias finds the next MBS or CDO in any technical mechanism.
- **Using the Cold War as the template** for the US and China. Most of history is multipolar.

## Worked Example: the Euro Area call (2010 to 2020)
1. **Consensus (2010-2015)**: German austerity preferences and peripheral debt would break the euro. British media projected its own Euroscepticism onto Germany.
2. **Constraint analysis**: Germany's export model depends on the union (table above). A Greek exit could unravel the union and so the German model. The German median voter saw this quickly (Figure 5.5) and policymakers followed, approving "one bailout after another".
3. **Out-of-consensus calls the author lists**: in 2010, that Berlin would let the ECB buy government debt directly. In 2015, that Greece would adjust its budget balance "by an extra 5% of GDP".
4. **What happened, per text**: the ECB turned dovish and German policymakers "basically crossed every red line they ever set". Merkel pushed debt mutualization. The trigger for her urgency was the May 2017 G7 summit in Sicily and her Munich beer-hall speech ("The era in which we could fully rely on others is over"). Berlin conceded greater fiscal integration "in summer of 2020".
5. **Forward calls (written 2020)**: the Macron-Merkel European Recovery Fund "is likely to pass at some point in 2020" despite the Frugal Four (Austria, Denmark, the Netherlands, Sweden). "I do not see risks to European integration over the course of the next decade." Allocate to Europe over the next decade. Europe will also adopt Buenos Aires policies, but less hard and fast than the US.

## Worked Example: is the trade war sustainable? (2018 to 2020)
1. **Preference**: both capitals are turning confrontational. The author himself published "Power and Politics in East Asia: Cold War 2.0?" in September 2012.
2. **Constraint**: multipolarity. Allies will not hand back the lost trade. Bipolar-era alliances moved trade flows strongly, and that link weakens under multipolarity.
3. **Two scenarios**:
   - *Attempted Damage Control*: leaders recognize the constraint and trade continues with limits. Trade does not reduce tension. "The probability of military conflict may increase even as trade between China and the US remains steady."
   - *Doubling Down*: leaders ignore multipolarity and give the trade to Europe and Japan, making both the US and China poorer.
4. **Call**: "The constraint framework strongly favors the first scenario."
5. **Evidence cited**: the Phase One deal, "a giant concession to the multipolar nature of the world". Trump's 18 February 2020 remarks against curbing jet engine sales to China ("They'll make those chips in a different country").
6. **Conclusion**: "the trade war between China and the US is unsustainable." It is not on the author's list of 2020s worries. Neither side gets full exclusion or Xi's "self-sufficiency".

## Worked Example: Yugoslavia 1988-1991 (constraints leading to ruin)
Foreign borrowing replaced adjustment after the 1970s oil shock and Asian competition. Lending stopped as Yugoslavia's Cold War value faded. Nominal wages rose 5% a month in Q4 1988. Real wages rose 20% in eight months to September 1989. Annual CPI inflation hit 13,000% in Q4 1989. On New Year's Eve 1989 Prime Minister Marković pegged a new currency to the deutschmark, imposed austerity and wage freezes, and stopped the printing. Inflation fell to zero in 1990, but output collapsed and Milošević had the Serbian central bank print for his campaign. The author's point: the macro context "limited how much political capital was available" to the federal reformers.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask who depends on whom before reading a crisis as a breakup. Dependence is the constraint.
2. Polls of the median voter beat op-eds by eminent economists. Greeks and Germans both moved toward the euro as the crisis deepened.
3. Even "Trotskyite communists like Tsipras turn into Thatcherite supply-side reformers when faced with constraints".
4. In a multipolar system economic constraints on trade war are "more restrictive than in any other system".
5. Both of the chapter's cases ended with crises resolving, not deepening. The author chose them to counter the doom bias.
6. Constraints do not guarantee good outcomes. Missing market feedback delays correction and worsens the next crisis.

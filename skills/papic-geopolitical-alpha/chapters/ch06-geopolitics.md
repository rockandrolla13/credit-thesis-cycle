# Chapter 6: Geopolitics

## Core Idea
Geography-based geopolitics is a real constraint but the author ranks it below politics, the economy and finance. Used alone it is "analytical fast food". Used with the other constraints it explains US grand strategy and where Russian military action stops.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Position in the constraints hierarchy**: the author says his choice "puts it below politics, the economy, and finance in the constraints hierarchy". Reason: politics is "the most powerful - and therefore predictive - constraint" but needs contextual knowledge. Economics and finance need specialist knowledge. Geopolitics gives "the most value for the least work put in", so generalists overstate it.
- **Grand strategy**: the "implicit but influential geopolitical imperatives, rooted in geography, from which a country derives its day-to-day foreign policy". Two founding theorists:
  - *Mahan* (sea power): dominate the oceans, the global "commons", because they carry trade and so hard power.
  - *Mackinder* (land power): Eurasia, the "World Island", holds enough resources (Russia), population (China), wealth (Europe) and buffer to be self-sufficient. A power that dominates it becomes a superpower by default.
- **Central tenet of American grand strategy**: "Prevent any one power from dominating Eurasia."
- **The Trump Doctrine (three lines of thought, as of 2017)**:
  1. *Transactionalism*: alliances must show a clear, immediate, calculable benefit. "Pay up."
  2. *Mercantilism*: no permanent allies, only trade balances that must be positive.
  3. *Nationalism*: "it is the right of all nations to put their own interests first."
  - Shared premise with Obama: the world is multipolar and the US is in relative decline. Obama sought leverage through allies. Trump seeks to "geopolitically deleverage".
- **Russia's three "membership perks" for client states**:
  1. *Geography*: clients sit between a mountain chain and Moscow, so trade's path of least resistance runs through Moscow. True for Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan and Azerbaijan, "far less for Ukraine".
  2. *Natural resources*: cheap energy at a high political price.
  3. *Protection racket*: security for governing elites against external, domestic and neighboring threats.
- **The "value-add" sphere imbalance**: membership of Russia's sphere is inferior to the EU's offer of market access, investment and a "good-housekeeping seal of approval". The author calls this Russia's fulcrum geopolitical constraint: "Moscow has very little to offer to the members of its sphere." "Russia had no carrot, so it had to use the stick."

## Key Concepts
- **Geopolitics**: commonly a catchall for interstate relations. In the strict sense, analysis from natural endowments, demographics and geography.
- **Hard power**: power "characterized by a coercive approach to international relations, often involving military action". Soft power relies on cultural or economic persuasion.
- **World Island**: Mackinder's name for Eurasia.
- **Geopolitical deleveraging**: retrenching from alliances and global commitments.
- **Eurasian Union**: Russia's customs union turned institutional sphere. Without Ukraine it is "Russia and a collection of Lilliputian economies".
- **Confounding variables in polls**: Ukraine's 2010 vote was not a clean East/West split. Yanukovych won on a pragmatic platform that included EU integration.

## Mental Models
- "Geography is not destiny. And it is not immutable." The Channel in 1588 versus 1940-1941. The US before and after the Erie Canal (1825). Plague-stricken Portugal launching the Age of Exploration within a century.
- The Afghanistan/Bosnia versus Switzerland test: similar terrain and heterogeneity, opposite outcomes. Geography alone under-determines.
- Relying on geopolitics in isolation "is like driving a car with a rearview mirror, a GPS system, and a pitch-black windshield".
- Focus on the system, not the quality of the players. The author learned this from football handicapping and from George Friedman at Stratfor.
- "Geopolitical constraints do not discriminate between party labels." Friedman's late-2008 forecast was that Obama would largely follow George W. Bush on Iraq, Russia and Guantanamo.
- When a power turns to force, ask what it could not get by attraction.

## Anti-patterns
- **Happy-meal forecasts**: Poland is always paranoid because of the North European Plain. The US is a permanent safe haven because of two oceans. China can never gain global influence because it is hemmed in.
- **Capability-spread reasoning**: concluding that Russia could easily defeat Ukraine from relative military size "betrays a lack of military strategy acumen". Supply lines, terrain and a hostile population decide.
- **Blaming NATO enlargement for Crimea**: footnote 16 says Germany vetoed Ukrainian accession at the 2008 Bucharest summit, six years earlier, and Putin knew it.
- **Reading foreign policy through party ideology**: both conservative and liberal clients sent hate mail over the Obama forecast.

## Worked Example: will Trump end the transatlantic alliance? (2017, reviewed 2020)
1. **Preference**: under the doctrine NATO and the EU are "detrimental to US interests". NATO costs money for little material return. The EU runs a trade surplus with the US.
2. **Where the preference leads**: allies hedge. Japan, South Korea and Germany move closer to China. Eurasia drifts toward the integration Mackinder feared.
3. **Fulcrum constraint**: "This potential World Island." Geography has not changed. The US intervened in Europe twice and Asia three times in the twentieth century to stop one power unifying the continent. The last united-Eurasia scare was the 1939 Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, and about 400,000 Americans died in the war that followed.
4. **Call**: there is "a significant probability" Trump pursues the doctrine to its end, especially in a second term free of re-election constraints. "However, it is unlikely because geopolitical constraints on the doctrine are too vast."
5. **Evidence cited**: Trump endorsed Article V in Poland (6 July 2017). The December 2017 National Security Strategy called NATO "one of our great advantages over our competitors" and reaffirmed Article V.
6. **Caveat the author records**: three years later most authors of that strategy had been removed. He still expects that "Material constraint - geopolitics - will ultimately win out over ideology - nationalism."

## Worked Example: where will Russia stop? (2014-2015)
1. **Event**: Russia annexed Crimea in February-March 2014. The Donbas war began a month later.
2. **Why force in 2014 but not after the 2005 Orange Revolution?** Then Moscow played a long game and got Yanukovych elected in 2010. By 2014 the pull of the EU showed that Russia's sphere could not compete (Figure 6.1 compares Ukraine with Poland and Romania). Force was "its only remaining choice". It also showed other client elites that Moscow protects them.
3. **Why Ukraine matters to Moscow**: a top market for Russian non-energy exports in 2014, demographic similarity, a buffer against NATO, the Black Sea Fleet base in Crimea (overstated, given Novorossiysk).
4. **Consensus**: Russia would build a land bridge to Crimea and push on. "The Baltics are next" (Ch 2).
5. **Constraint read**: see the table. Either Russia was incompetent, "or Moscow made the only decision its constraints allowed".
6. **What happened, per text**: Russia stopped at under 5% of Ukrainian territory. On 30 September 2015 it began its Syria intervention, pivoting to a theater where it was not constrained.
7. **Scope note**: the book was finalized in 2020 and reports nothing after that date.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use geopolitics as one constraint among five. "Geopolitical alpha is rarely a product of only the geopolitical constraint."
2. For US foreign policy, check any presidential preference against the Eurasia imperative.
3. For a regional power, compare what its sphere offers with what the rival bloc offers. A weak offer predicts coercion and also limits it.
4. Estimate the force required for a feared military scenario. If the number overcommits the actor, lower the probability.
5. Expect continuity across administrations where geography binds.

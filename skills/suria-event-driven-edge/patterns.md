# Patterns: The Event-Driven Edge in Investing

These are the author's techniques, stated as his. Each carries the chapter it comes from.

## Annualize the spread on a conservative date (Ch 2)
**When to use**: comparing merger deals with different spreads and timelines.
**How**: (1) Spread = (deal value - price) / price. (2) Take the last day of the company's stated closing window. (3) Annualized = spread x 12 / months to close. (4) Add dividends the target will still pay. (5) Adjust the date for sector and regulators.
**Trade-offs**: simple scaling assumes you can redeploy at a similar spread. A delay from four months to six cuts 12% to 8%.

## Lock in a stock-deal spread (Ch 2)
**When to use**: all-stock or cash-plus-stock deals.
**How**: short the acquirer at the exchange ratio. One Lemonade share per 19 Metromile shares. Nine Goodyear shares per 10 Cooper shares.
**Trade-offs**: borrow fees and dividends owed on the short. If the deal fails, the target falls and the acquirer rises.

## Regulatory path check (Ch 2)
**When to use**: before entering any deal.
**How**: list the approvals in the merger agreement. Mark HSR status (early termination, expiry, pull-and-refile, second request, suit). Mark CFIUS, state regulators, and each foreign authority, above all SAMR. Note the outside date.
**Trade-offs**: the author says experienced arbitrageurs skip cross-border, banking, insurance and utility deals. That removes many of the widest spreads.

## Free CVR (Ch 2)
**When to use**: a deal pays cash plus a contingent value right.
**How**: enter only if the cash spread is positive and closing risk is low. The CVR is then "a free or nearly free lottery ticket".
**Trade-offs**: not all CVRs pay.

## Option overlays on a deal (Ch 2)
**When to use**: to add income or cap the loss on a deal.
**How**: sell covered calls past the close date. Buy puts on high-risk deals. Buy calls instead of stock. Sell puts just below the deal price.
**Trade-offs**: each gives up part of the spread or of the bidding-war upside. A delay can leave bought options worthless. The author says premiums have been "very thin in recent years".

## Rebound trade after a broken deal (Ch 2)
**When to use**: a deal fails and arbitrage funds sell.
**How**: analyze why the deal failed. Wait several days for forced selling to end. Hold longer than feels natural. The author says he entered and exited Rogers too early.
**Trade-offs**: "Not all failures are created equal." Rite Aid kept falling for years.

## Insider purchase filter (Ch 3)
**When to use**: screening Form 4 filings for ideas.
**How**: keep open-market purchases that fit one of five types: same-industry buying, clusters, long-serving independent directors (best if investment professionals), cycle-tested chairs, founder CEOs. Drop the eight low-signal types (same-price clusters, same-day matched trades, late filings, new-hire requirements, annual routines, contractual buys, exercise-and-sell, tax sales). Read the footnotes.
**Trade-offs**: insiders are early and can be anchored. The idea still needs full research.

## Sell/buy ratio at extremes (Ch 3)
**When to use**: judging whether a market sell-off is near an inflection.
**How**: divide the week's aggregate insider selling by aggregate insider buying. Compare with the median of 19.03. A reading under 1 is rare (March 2020: 0.98, then 0.88).
**Trade-offs**: not a timing tool by itself. Quiet periods distort it. Large single sales skew it. Insiders are early, so scale in over weeks or months.

## Cluster selling into weakness (Ch 3)
**When to use**: you hold a stock and insiders are selling.
**How**: ignore routine sales, exercises and tax sales. Act when several insiders make regular sales at once and keep selling as the price falls. "Revisit your thesis."
**Trade-offs**: most insider selling carries no signal.

## Double Dipper (Ch 4)
**When to use**: looking for buybacks that management believes in.
**How**: require a real fall in share count from the 10-Q or 10-K, plus open-market purchases by insiders.
**Trade-offs**: an idea source only.

## Signaling screen for buybacks (Ch 4)
**When to use**: a company announces a large buyback.
**How**: be suspicious if sales are declining, the stock has fallen sharply, analysts are cutting estimates, and management pay is heavy in options. Prefer buybacks made while analysts raise estimates. Avoid multi-division "black boxes".
**Trade-offs**: the cited study found suspected signalers were well under 10% of the sample.

## Uber cannibals screen (Ch 4)
**When to use**: a systematic buyback portfolio.
**How**: Pabrai's rule. Top five repurchasers of the prior year. Market cap at least $100m. No insurers. Growing revenue. Price/sales under 2.5. Buyback percentage more than 2% above the dividend yield. Rebalance each 18 March.
**Trade-offs**: the names turn over a lot. The author says the largest cannibals "delivered bad results" in 2022.

## Odd lot tender (Ch 4)
**When to use**: a tender offer has an odd lot clause and the stock trades below the tender price.
**How**: buy up to 99 shares and tender them.
**Trade-offs**: rare now. The price moves fast. The profit is small.

## SPAC redemption with a kept warrant (Ch 5)
**When to use**: a SPAC before its business combination.
**How**: buy units after the IPO and before the 52-day split. At the vote, redeem the shares for about $10 plus interest if the target is weak. Keep the warrants.
**Trade-offs**: the company can redeem warrants early. Bowlero paid $0.10 per warrant.

## De-SPAC short list (Ch 5)
**When to use**: looking for short ideas.
**How**: start from completed SPAC combinations. Test each for free cash flow, revenue trend, net debt and capitalized leases. Check borrow cost.
**Trade-offs**: hard to borrow. Meme rallies. The author's WeWork short went from $6.40 to $2.76 in the model portfolio.

## Parent-or-child debt test (Ch 6)
**When to use**: a spinoff is announced.
**How**: read the Form 10-12B and the presentations. Find which entity carries the debt and which gets the cash. Apply Howe's three checks: growth history, cost of debt, competitive position.
**Trade-offs**: the author sometimes changed his mind "right before the spinoff, as more information became available".

## Spinoff entry clock (Ch 6)
**When to use**: timing a purchase of the spun-off company.
**How**: choose one of three rules in the chapter. Wait until 50% of shares outstanding have traded (about seven to eight trading days). Wait six months. Or wait for the second year.
**Trade-offs**: the three rules disagree. The author does not pick one.

## Spinsider (Ch 6)
**When to use**: after a spinoff starts trading.
**How**: look for open-market purchases by the spinoff's insiders.
**Trade-offs**: Douglas Elliman insiders bought below $6 and the stock went to about $2.

## New-management audit (Ch 7)
**When to use**: a CEO change, or any new position.
**How**: (1) Record at the prior company: revenue, margins, capital allocation, ROE. (2) ROE trend against industry peers, with 15% as the author's screen. (3) Proxy statement: pay, pay ratio, grant size against profit. (4) Insider buying or cashing out. (5) Item 5.02 history for sudden or clustered exits.
**Trade-offs**: a strong record does not fix a weak business (Mayer at Yahoo!). Turnarounds at large companies take years (GE).

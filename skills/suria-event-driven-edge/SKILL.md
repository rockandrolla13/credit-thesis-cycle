## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

All rules below are the author's. This is an equity special-situations book. It is not a credit or macro text.

### 1. The stance (Preface, Introduction, Conclusion)
- If your ideas come from the sources everyone uses, expect mediocre results.
- Six strategies: merger arbitrage, insider transactions, stock buybacks, SPACs, spinoffs, management changes.
- **The trifecta**: idea generation, macro signals, and patterns where one company appears in several screens.
- Every screen is an idea source. "There is no substitute for deep due diligence."
- Each strategy has a "dark side". In a crisis "almost all correlations go to one".

### 2. Merger arbitrage (Ch 2)
- **Spread** = gap between the target's price and the deal price. It pays for deal risk and for time.
- **Base rates** (InsideArbitrage data, 2010 to 2022): 95% of deals with a definitive agreement close. Mean time to close is 131 days, median 107, on 2,320 deals.
- **Asymmetry**: a broken deal with 5% upside can cost 30% to 50%.
- **Annualize** on a conservative closing date. 4% in four months is 12%. The same 4% in six months is 8%.
- **Three killjoys**: regulators, dissenting shareholders, missing financing.
- **Regulatory path**: HSR 30-day clock, second request, suit. Then CFIUS, state regulators, foreign authorities. SAMR approvals can take 12 to 15 months.
- **Deal types**: all cash (buy and wait), all stock (short the acquirer at the ratio), cash plus stock, cash or stock, collar, special conditions and CVRs.
- **Walk-away law**: no "on hold" after signing. The acquirer must prove a Material Adverse Change. Cases settle at the price or a small discount (Tiffany under 3%, Forescout 12.12%, Twitter nil).
- **Spread as signal**: failed deals start with wide spreads that widen before failure (Mitchell and Pulvino). Spreads widen and terminations rise in bear markets (Cretin et al.).
- **Rebound trade**: arbitrage funds are forced sellers after a break. Wait for them to finish.
- **Bond alternative**: spreads widen when rates are high. Large-spread deals are like junk bonds.

### 3. Insider transactions (Ch 3)
- Purchases carry the signal. Sales mostly do not.
- **Keep five types**: same-industry buying, cluster purchases, long-serving independent directors (best if investment professionals), cycle-tested chairs, founder CEOs.
- **Drop eight types**: same-price clusters, same-day matched trades, late filings, new-hire requirements, annual routines, contractual buys, exercise-and-sell, tax sales (code F).
- **Sell/buy ratio**: weekly aggregate insider selling over buying. Mean 30.10, median 19.03 (August 2010 to July 2022). It fell to 0.98 and 0.88 in March 2020. Use only at extremes, with other indicators. Not a timing tool.
- **Selling that matters**: several insiders making regular sales at once while the price falls (First Solar 2011, Carvana 2021).
- Insiders are early and can be anchored. Analysts beat managers when macro drives the business.
- A purchase announced by press release is normally a red flag.

### 4. Stock buybacks (Ch 4)
- Track the fall in share count, not the announcement.
- **Cyclical trap**: stocks look cheapest and cash is highest at the cyclical peak, so companies buy at the top.
- A buyback near a top can still be sound when the industry is holding back supply (frackers after the OPEC price war).
- A leveraged company buying back stock instead of paying down debt: "the simple answer to this question is no."
- **Double Dipper**: buybacks plus open-market insider buying.
- **Signaling marks**: declining sales, falling stock, analysts cutting estimates, option-heavy pay.
- **Uber cannibals** (Pabrai): top five repurchasers, price/sales under 2.5, growing revenue. 6.3% a year above the S&P 500 in his backtest.
- **Capital allocation audit**: when did they issue or retire debt and stock, and did past M&A pay?

### 5. SPACs (Ch 5)
- Units at $10 split into shares and warrants after 52 days. Holders can redeem for about $10 plus interest at the vote and keep the warrants.
- De-SPACs perform badly. March 2023, 315 combinations: 8% positive, mean loss almost 65%, median loss 78%.
- Test the target on free cash flow, revenue trend, and enterprise value including net debt and capitalized leases.
- EBITDA and "adjusted" EBITDA hide interest, tax and recurring "one-time" costs.
- "Turnaround situations rarely turn around", and less so with leverage.

### 6. Spinoffs (Ch 6)
- Funds with mandates sell the spinoff. Forced selling creates the entry.
- **Four types**: spinoff, split-off, carve-out, Reverse Morris Trust.
- **Four sources of outperformance**: parent recovery, parent performance, long-run child returns, acquisition of either.
- **Debt test**: leveraged parents "often load the spinoffs with a disproportionate amount of debt". Viatris got $24.5bn of debt and fell 36% while Pfizer rose 34%. The new Biohaven got $275m of cash and rose 142%.
- **Howe's three checks**: growth history, cost of debt, competitive position.
- **Entry clocks**: 50% of shares traded (Howe), six months, or year two (Cusatis). The chapter does not settle on one.
- **Spinsider**: spinoffs with insider buying. It failed for Douglas Elliman.

### 7. Management changes (Ch 7)
- Stock price is a poor yardstick. Use ROE (net income / shareholder equity) inside one industry. The author screens for 15% or more.
- Check a new CEO's record at the prior company (Culp at Danaher, then GE).
- A good manager does not rescue a bad business (Mayer at Yahoo!).
- Read the proxy statement for pay, the pay ratio, and stock grants. Low-pay CEO firms beat high-pay firms by nearly 39% over 10 years (MSCI).
- **Departures**: sudden or clustered exits are "strong sell signals". Read 8-K Item 5.02. Bad news lands on Friday evenings.
- Footnote and risk factor changes can warn of bankruptcy risk.

This includes the year-by-year deal success table (Image 2.4), the Cannae sum-of-the-parts table (Image 6.3), and every price chart. Each chapter file lists its losses under "Extraction Notes".

- **Publication year is not in the extracted text.** The author writes from points between late 2022 and late 2023. Outcomes after that are not in the book and are not supplied here.
- **No missing chapters.** All seven chapters, the Preface, Introduction and Conclusion were read in full. Three primers (options, bonds, futures) were left out of the chapter files.
- **Single-author, practitioner view.** The author sells a newsletter and screening tools on the same strategies.

## Use for Credit and Macro Questions

- **Credit spreads**: nothing direct. The book prices merger spreads, not bond spreads. The one bridge is Ch 2: deal spreads widen with interest rates and in bear markets, and large-spread deals are compared to junk bonds.
- **Debt-financed investment booms**: little. Ch 4 gives the shipping capex cycle and the fracker capital burn as cyclical-peak examples. Ch 5 gives SPAC issuance counts for 2018 to 2021 as an issuance boom. Neither is a method for sizing a debt-financed boom.
- **Inflation and Fed regimes**: nothing as method. The 2022 rate rises appear only as background to deal breaks, preferred share prices and spinoff leverage.
- **Issuer screening**: this is where the book helps. Equity-side warning signs with credit meaning: debt pushed into a spinoff (Ch 6), buybacks by a leveraged company (Ch 4), debt-funded rollups (Ch 5), adjusted EBITDA against free cash flow (Ch 5), capitalized leases added to enterprise value (Ch 5), debt that "appeared to have few takers" and was placed at 10.25% (Carvana, Ch 3), cluster insider selling (Ch 3), and sudden executive departures and risk factor changes (Ch 7).
- **Market stress signal**: the insider sell/buy ratio at extremes (Ch 3) is a sentiment reading at a market low. The book gives two data points below 1.

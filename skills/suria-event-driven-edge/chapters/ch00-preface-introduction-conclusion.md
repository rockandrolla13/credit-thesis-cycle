# Preface, Introduction and Conclusion

## Core Idea
Event-driven investing means investing in companies that are going through a specific event. The author uses six strategies as a source of fresh ideas, as a source of returns he calls uncorrelated with the market, and as an occasional macro signal. He treats every strategy as an idea filter. None replaces due diligence.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The idea-source argument** (Preface): if your ideas come from the same sources that millions of other investors use, your performance "is likely to be mediocre". You would do better in a broad index.
- **Six event-driven strategies** (Preface, Introduction): legal insider transactions, merger arbitrage, stock buybacks, SPACs, spinoffs, management changes. Each strategy chapter carries a "dark side" section on when it fails.
- **The trifecta** (Introduction): the three things the author says the strategies give him.
  1. *Idea generation.* Companies he would otherwise have missed.
  2. *Macro signals.* A strategy can show "the market may be at an inflection point". His example is unusually strong insider buying near the end of a bear market.
  3. *Unique patterns.* "The same companies start bubbling up across strategies." Insiders buy while the company buys back stock. A merger target plans a spinoff and an insider buys before the close.
- **Flexible mental model** (Introduction): pick the strategy that fits the current market instead of staying "pigeonholed" in one style. Some strategies work in falling markets. Others work coming out of a bear market.
- **Three-pile triage** (Conclusion): each idea goes to one of three places. Invest, watchlist, or "the rejection pile". Deep due diligence decides which.

## Key Concepts
- **Event-driven investing**: investing in companies experiencing a specific event. Also called special situations investing (Ch 1).
- **Dark side**: the author's label for the failure modes of each strategy.
- **Correlation caveat**: "at times of extreme market stress most asset classes tend to become correlated and move in unison" (Introduction).
- **Catalyst**: the event that tips a watched idea into a purchase. In the Twilio example it was the CEO's open-market purchase.

## Mental Models
- Treat years of reading as a search cost. Munger read Barron's for 50 years and found one idea. The author read Fortune for over a decade and found one (Twilio).
- Treat a quick loss as a skill. "Taking a loss quickly and avoiding an even larger one down the road is an investing superpower."
- Markets move in cycles. The author notes that spinoff effects found four decades ago still persist, and that merger arbitrage is used "decade after decade" (Conclusion).

## Anti-patterns
- **The "safe blue chip" that fell by half**: the author lists three red flags he missed in 2001. A stock that declined rapidly. A cyclical company in a recession. A CEO "cheering the stock on".
- **Holding and hoping**: he rode Enron from about $30 to 30 cents, a 99% loss, on advice to "stay the course".
- **Optimizing a working system into a style bet**: his experimental insider fund returned 23% in year one. He then overlaid a value framework "just as value stocks started significantly underperforming growth stocks". The underperformance lasted years and he wound the fund down.
- **Momentum without research**: his first 30% in a few weeks was "beginner's luck".

## Worked Example: the Twilio purchase (Introduction)
1. A Fortune article about founder Jeff Lawson appeared before the IPO. The author kept the company in mind.
2. The IPO priced at $15 per share. The stock rose 92% on day one.
3. The stock then declined after the usual IPO pop.
4. The CEO bought shares on the open market at the same time. The author calls this legal insider purchase "the catalyst" for his own purchase.
5. Stated result: the stock went up more than 1,600%.

## Worked Example: the experimental insider fund (Introduction)
- Inputs: more than a decade of insider transactions and several decades of FactSet fundamental data. Traditional data analysis plus machine learning.
- Finding: "a series of algorithmic trading strategies that outperformed the market in our backtests".
- Build: an automated process that picked, bought, and sold at the end of a holding period. The author registered as an advisor in California and Washington and raised a seed fund.
- Result: 23% in year one, ahead of the benchmark. Then the value overlay failed and the fund closed.
- The book does not give the strategy rules, the holding period, or the backtest numbers.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use event-driven screens to find ideas other investors do not see.
2. Expect the strategies to diverge from the market in normal times and to correlate with it in a crisis.
3. Look for the same company appearing in two or three screens at once.
4. Read the "dark side" of each strategy before using it.
5. Sort every idea into invest, watchlist, or reject after due diligence.

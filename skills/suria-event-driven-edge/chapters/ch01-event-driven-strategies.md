# Chapter 1: Event-Driven Strategies

## Core Idea
The six strategies share one feature: "a significant event that changes the nature or trajectory of a business". This chapter defines each strategy in a few paragraphs and gives one anchoring example for each. It is a map, not a method.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Merger arbitrage (risk arbitrage)**: buy the target at a discount to the acquisition price and wait for the deal to close. The gap is "the spread". The arbitrageur takes the risk that the deal does not close and the stock falls "sharply to pre-deal levels".
  - Base rate the author gives: he analyzed over 12 years of deal data from 2010 to 2022 and "95% of announced deals close".
  - Logic: the payoff is "a few pennies", so "most deals have to close" for the strategy to work.
- **Insider transactions**: insiders see the business in real time. They must report an open-market purchase or sale to the SEC within two business days. The author admits this is not normally an event-driven strategy, because the transaction does not transform the company. He includes it because the signal can show a stock is "materially cheap or expensive".
- **Stock buybacks**: companies sell shares (a secondary offering) when the stock is expensive and buy them back when it is cheap. The author ties CEO success to capital allocation, "specifically when to issue more shares and when to repurchase them" (citing Thorndike's *The Outsiders*).
- **SPACs**: a pool of money raised first, with a two-year search for a private operating company. If no company is found, the money goes back to investors with interest. That deadline gives the sponsor an incentive to merge with something.
- **Spinoffs**: "initial underperformance followed by subsequent outperformance is a feature and not a bug". Large funds do not want the spinoff and sell it soon after separation.
- **Management changes**: the leader has a "disproportionate impact". A leader with a strong record joining a troubled company can be a signal. Departures can be red flags.

## Key Concepts
- **Spread**: the difference between the target's current price and the acquisition price.
- **Secondary offering**: any sale of new shares to the public after the IPO, no matter how many times it has happened before. It dilutes holders and often leads to a temporary price drop.
- **Blank check company**: the older name for a SPAC.
- **Sponsor**: the party that starts the SPAC and receives a large stake in the combined company.
- **Boomerang CEO**: a founder or former CEO who returns (Schultz, Dell, Jobs, Dorsey).
- **Spinoff**: a division separated into an independent company.

## Mental Models
- Think of a tight spread and a wide spread as two readings of deal risk. Alleghany traded under 1% from its price. Twitter traded 66% from its price.
- Think of buybacks and secondary offerings as one decision seen from two sides: management's view of whether the stock is cheap or expensive.
- Think of spinoff weakness in year one as selling pressure, not as a verdict on the business.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading the SPAC deadline as discipline**: the author says the two-year clock pushed 2020 and 2021 vintage SPACs into companies with "little more than a business plan and no sales or profits". Those stocks "inevitably collapsed post-merger".
- **Assuming a founder-led company keeps its course after the founder leaves**: "companies falter and lose their way when their visionary founder CEO departs".

## Worked Example: two spreads (Ch 1)
| | Alleghany / Berkshire Hathaway | Twitter / Elon Musk |
|---|---|---|
| Deal price | $848.02 per share | $54.20 per share (Ch 2) |
| Announced | 31 March 2022 | Agreed 25 April 2022 (Ch 2) |
| Observation date | 30 September 2022, stock at $840.56 | 11 July 2022, stock at $32.65 (Ch 2) |
| Spread | $7.46, under 1% | 66% |
| Outcome | Closed 19 October 2022 | Closed 27 October 2022 at the original price (Ch 2) |

Side note the author gives on Alleghany: Buffett offered $850 per share. The final $848.02 excludes the financial advisory fee owed to the investment banks.

## Worked Example: Ferrari (Ch 1)
- Fiat raised its Ferrari stake from 50% in 1969 to 90% by 1988.
- Ferrari was spun off in 2015 under the ticker RACE.
- It dropped at first and underperformed the S&P 500 for much of its first year.
- Over its first five years it gained 237% against 67% for the S&P 500.

## Other numbers in the chapter
- Accenture was spun out of Arthur Andersen in 2001 and later became worth over $160 billion. Arthur Andersen ceased to exist after its conviction over Enron.
- The author's first blank check example: two entrepreneurs raised about half a million dollars, then bought a jeweler called Jody Coyote in Eugene, Oregon.

## Key Takeaways
1. An event-driven strategy needs an event that changes the business. Insider transactions are the admitted exception.
2. Merger arbitrage pays small amounts often, so the close rate is the first number to check.
3. The SPAC deadline creates a sponsor incentive that can work against the investor.
4. Expect a spinoff to fall first.
5. Track who runs the company and when that changes.

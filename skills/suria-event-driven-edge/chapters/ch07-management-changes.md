# Chapter 7: Management Changes

## Core Idea
A CEO or management team can have a disproportionate effect on a company, but stock price is a poor measure of them. The author judges new management by its record at the prior company and by return on equity, checks pay in the proxy statement, and treats sudden or clustered departures as a sell signal.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Business versus management** (Buffett, quoted): "When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact." The author's version: turnarounds "rarely turn around". "The magic really starts to happen when you combine a good management team with a strong business."
- **Why attribution is hard**: Bezos said a good quarter was "baked in three years before". The median CEO tenure at a public company is just under four years and the CFO's is 3.5 years.
- **The right yardstick**: the author cites Biggs for the view that two-thirds to three-fourths of stock price movement at any time is driven by sentiment. So measure management on operating metrics.
  - **Return on equity (ROE)** = net income / shareholder equity. Shareholder equity = total assets minus total liabilities.
  - **Return on assets (ROA)** = net income / total assets. The author finds it less useful because many businesses are asset-light.
  - Rules: compare ROE inside one industry. Read the trend, because one-off events move net income. The author screens for "a ROE of 15% or greater".
- **Prior-company test for a new CEO**: at the last company, did revenue grow, did margins expand, was capital allocated efficiently, and was ROE respectable?
- **Founder rule**: the author prefers founders at the helm and watches their open-market purchases. "If part of your investment thesis rests on a founder leading the company, the departure of the founder requires revisiting that thesis." A founder who stays on as executive chair is a softer case.
- **Compensation check**:
  1. Open the proxy statement and read the Summary Compensation Table. It covers the CEO, the CFO and the three other highest-paid officers for three fiscal years.
  2. Read the pay ratio: CEO pay divided by median employee pay. It has been reported since 2018.
  3. Check how grants are counted. A ranking that books a multi-year grant in one year distorts the picture.
  4. Compare stock-based compensation with net income and revenue.
- **Pay does not buy performance**: the author's "big picture answer is no". See the studies below.
- **Departure signals**:
  1. Read Item 5.02 of the 8-K for departures, appointments and pay arrangements.
  2. Sudden resignations are the biggest red flag (Michelle Leder).
  3. Bad news is often filed after the close on a Friday or before a three-day weekend.
  4. A cluster of departures, or "a revolving door" at the CEO's office, needs special attention.
  5. The author's verdict: "Sudden departures and rapid churn, where management team members only last a few months, are strong sell signals for long investors". Short sellers use the same signal.
- **The mosaic**: a prior track record is not enough. Also check whether insiders buy with their own money or cash out their grants, whether the financials match the CEO's optimism, and whether capital allocation is sound.
- **Joly's three imperatives** (Best Buy): people, then business, then financial. Each depends on the one before it. Money is an outcome, not the purpose.

## Key Concepts
- **Proxy statement**: the SEC filing that holds executive pay disclosure.
- **Realized compensation**: stock awards counted when vested and options when cashed in, not when granted.
- **Pay ratio**: CEO pay over median employee pay.
- **Stock-based compensation**: pay in options and RSUs. It shows on the cash flow statement.
- **Item 5.02**: the 8-K item for officer and director changes.
- **Net Promoter Score**: promoters (9 or 10) minus detractors (0 to 6), with passives (7 or 8) dropped. The range is -100 to 100. Some investors use it as a quality indicator.
- **Risk factor changes**: changes in footnotes or in the risk factors from one quarter to the next can warn of bankruptcy risk.

## Mental Models
- Think of hope as a warning. "Once you get to a stage where you start hoping for certain outcomes, your investment is likely in trouble."
- Think of turnaround time as a function of size. "The larger the company and the deeper the hole, the longer it takes."
- Expect a drop after the first excitement. GE rose on Culp's appointment, then fell more than 40% over the next two months.
- "Too many companies would prefer that you not read the footnotes. That should be incentive enough to delve into them" (a former SEC chairman, quoted by Leder).

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging the CEO by the stock price over a short period.**
- **Assuming a star hire fixes a weak business**: Marissa Mayer at Yahoo! (2012 to 2017). Tumblr was bought for $1.1bn in 2013 and sold for $3m in 2019. The author calls it partly "a bad business meeting good management".
- **Accepting a pay ranking without the filing**: Equilar's list booked the full value of grants in the grant year.
- **Ignoring stock-based compensation**: see Qualtrics.
- **Reading a single departure as proof.** People leave for many reasons. The signal is suddenness, weak explanations, and clusters.

## Worked Example: Qualtrics (Case study 1)
1. Carved out of SAP by IPO in 2021 at $30. It closed day one at $45.50.
2. 2021: revenue up 42% to $1.08bn. Net loss $1.06bn. Stock-based compensation "exactly $1.06bn".
3. The CEO's total compensation was $540,513,050, the second highest on Equilar's 2021 list. That is more than half of the company's stock-based compensation.
4. The stock ended 2021 at $35.40 and bottomed at $9.65 in November 2022.
5. March 2023: bought by Silver Lake and CPP Investments for $12.5bn, $18.15 per share, a 73% premium. That is below the IPO price. Management grants usually accelerate in an acquisition.
6. Same list, 10th place: Intel's CEO at $178m, with a pay ratio of 1,711 against median pay of $104,400. In his first two years the stock lost more than half against 18% for the Nasdaq. Net profit fell from $19.87bn in 2021 to $8.01bn in 2022 and the dividend was cut 65%. The author says "the jury is still out".

## Worked Example: General Electric (Case study 2)
1. The starting point: restatements, SEC probes, "well over $200bn of net debt", and a $3bn Buffett investment in 2008.
2. Culp joined the board in April 2018 and became CEO in October 2018.
3. The prior-company record: CEO of Danaher from 2000 to 2014. He raised its market cap and revenue fivefold with over $22bn of acquisitions.
4. Actions: gross debt cut by over $90bn. Lighting and biopharma sold. GE Transportation merged with Wabtec in early 2019. GE Healthcare spun off. Power and renewables to be spun in 2024.
5. The author notes the debt placement: "The mantra with spinoffs is to load the spinoff with a lot of debt." GE loaded GE Healthcare "with plenty".
6. Markers along the way: four insiders including Culp bought in May 2022. Net debt is almost nil in late 2023. The stock is up more than 100% over the last year.
7. The author's reading: the Danaher record signalled "a high probability of a turnaround", and that it would take time.

## Worked Example: Vista Outdoor (departure cluster)
- October 2022: the CFO leaves "to pursue other opportunities".
- 1 February 2023: the CEO resigns at the board's request after a "loss of confidence in his leadership for reasons not involving financial reporting or internal controls". He was to lead the planned spinoff.
- Two weeks later: the general counsel resigns.
- The author infers that something about the spinoff "appears to have gone off the rails". The book gives no outcome.

## Key Takeaways
1. Judge management on ROE and margins over long periods, inside its industry.
2. Check a new CEO's record at the last company.
3. Revisit the thesis when a founder leaves.
4. Read the proxy for pay, the pay ratio, and the size of stock grants.
5. Track Item 5.02 filings. Treat sudden and clustered exits as a reason to sell or to dig.
6. Do not expect a good manager to rescue a bad business.

# Chapter 6: Spinoffs

## Core Idea
Funds that receive a spinoff often cannot or will not hold it, so it is sold without regard to value in its first weeks or months. That forced selling, plus a newly incentivized management team, is why spinoffs as a group have outperformed. The work is to decide between parent and child, and the deciding fact is often where the debt was put.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The corporate life cycle**: young companies grow organically. Middle-aged ones grow by acquisition. Mature ones spin off divisions.
- **Four types of separation**:
  | Type | Mechanism | Example |
  |---|---|---|
  | Spinoff | Holders receive shares of the new company, like a special dividend | Kontoor Brands from VF, 2019 |
  | Split-off | Holders choose shares of the subsidiary in place of parent shares. They cannot keep both | Not given |
  | Carve-out | The parent sells some or all of the subsidiary in an IPO | Chipotle from McDonald's. Match Group from IAC, November 2015, with IAC keeping nearly 85% of class A |
  | Reverse Morris Trust | The parent spins off a unit and merges it with another company at the same time. Tax-efficient | Pfizer's Upjohn with Mylan, forming Viatris, 2020 |
- **Why the child gets sold**: the spinoff does not meet the fund's mandate. A large-cap fund with a $10bn floor will not hold a $1.5bn spinoff.
- **Why spinoffs work** (the author's two reasons): mean reversion after the forced selling, and "newly incentivized management teams" free of the parent's bureaucracy. "You can't win a race by committee."
- **Four sources of outperformance** (from Sergi and Owers and the papers they build on):
  1. *Parent price recovery.* In some cases the parent's price recovers fully after the spin, so "the spinoff almost becomes free".
  2. *Parent performance.* On average the parent does not fall by the full value of what it spun off. Long-run parent returns are also better when it sheds a weak unit and loads it with debt.
  3. *Long-term returns for the child*, after the forced selling ends.
  4. *Acquisition.* The parent or the child becomes a takeover target. Some parents spin a unit off to make themselves one.
- **Parent or child: the debt test.** "Parent companies with leveraged balance sheets often load the spinoffs with a disproportionate amount of debt." A parent can also push its weakest assets into the child. Read the Form 10-12B and the investor presentations for the motive, the pro-forma financials, and the management team.
- **The author's position on leverage, against Greenblatt**: Greenblatt saw leveraged spinoffs favourably because leverage amplifies returns. The author finds leverage "a significant risk", above all when the company's fate is tied to the economy and to rising rates.
- **Rich Howe's three checks** (Stock Spinoffs Investing):
  1. *Historical growth in revenue and earnings.* Avoid secular losers except for quick trades. The author adds that managers who underperformed inside the parent keep underperforming in the child.
  2. *Cost of debt.* Avoid heavily indebted spinoffs when rates are high.
  3. *Competitive position.* Is the spinoff a market share leader?
- **Timing rules** (three views the chapter records):
  | Source | Rule |
  |---|---|
  | Cusatis, Miles and Woolridge (1993) | Outperformance comes in the second year as a public company |
  | Rich Howe | Wait until at least 50% of shares outstanding have traded. The stock bottoms after 40 to 60% have traded, usually about seven to eight trading days |
  | "Other investors" with quantitative work | Six months after the spinoff |
  The author says cheaper trading has shortened the timeline since the early papers.
- **Sum-of-the-parts (SOTP)**: value each business in a holding company and compare the total with the market value. A spinoff is one way to close the gap. The limit: "there may not be a clear catalyst".
- **The Spinsider screen**: spinoffs whose insiders buy on the open market with their own money after trading starts.

## Key Concepts
- **SpinCo**: the new company created by a spinoff.
- **Form 10-12B (Form 10)**: the SEC filing for a spinoff. It carries pro-forma financials and total shares outstanding.
- **8-K**: the current report for major events, due within four business days.
- **Conglomerate discount**: a holding company trading below the value of its parts. The author says Berkshire avoids it because cash flows up to good capital allocators.
- **Ex-dividend analogy**: a parent "should" fall by the value of what it distributes.
- **Stub**: the small leftover entity, as with the new Biohaven.

## Mental Models
- Think of the first weeks of a spinoff as supply from holders who never chose to own it.
- Think of the debt allocation as the parent telling you which entity it wants to be strong.
- "There is a season for every strategy." The spinoff index outperformed the S&P 500 until 2019 and lagged in 2020 and 2021, when technology led the index.
- A turnaround of a large ship takes longer. Expect year two, not month two.

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying the child because the parent's partner was cheap**: the author first liked the Upjohn spin because Mylan was already cheap. The debt changed his mind.
- **Trusting insider buying alone in a spinoff**: Douglas Elliman (spun from Vector Group at end 2021) closed day one over $12. Insiders bought below $6. It missed estimates, posted losses, and traded just above $2 by August 2023.
- **Ignoring the macro setting**: Douglas Elliman was a luxury real estate business in a period of rising rates.
- **Buying on day one.**
- **SOTP without a catalyst.**

## Worked Example: Pfizer, Upjohn and Mylan (Case study 1, parent wins)
1. Background: Mylan raised the EpiPen price by 500%, from $103.50 in 2009 to over $608 in 2016. Lawsuits settled in 2022 for a combined $609m. The generic drug sector also carried debt from "ill-timed and expensive acquisitions".
2. Structure: a Reverse Morris Trust. Upjohn issued $12bn of debt at or before separation. The proceeds went to Pfizer.
3. Result at close: the new company had $24.5bn of total debt, Mylan's plus Upjohn's.
4. Closed November 2020.
5. Two years later: Pfizer up 34%. Viatris down nearly 36%. A gap of 70 points.
6. The author bought Pfizer. Its later acquisitions gave him merger arbitrage trades that closed quickly.

## Worked Example: Biohaven (Case study 2, child wins)
1. 10 May 2022: Pfizer agrees to buy Biohaven for $148.50 cash, $11.6bn, a premium of nearly 79%. Holders also get one SpinCo share per two Biohaven shares.
2. The arbitrage: stock at $141, spread $7.50 or 5.32%. The deal closed 143 days after announcement, over 13% annualized.
3. The spinoff terms: SpinCo is funded with $275m in cash, net of cash and securities it already holds. It also gets tiered royalties at "low- to mid-tens" percentage rates on US sales of two migraine drugs above $5.25bn a year, capped at $400m a year, for years through 2040.
4. The author's view of the royalty: a long shot. Nurtec ODT sold $462.5m in 2021 and global migraine drug sales in 2019 were $1.8bn. He counts it as optionality on top of the cash.
5. Insider signal: a director bought $5.63m at $148.04 weeks before the close, with 46 cents of spread left. The author reads this as buying for the free SpinCo share.
6. After the spin: the new Biohaven closed day one at $6.30. The CEO bought a little over $5m that week and a director $2.46m. A $302m secondary at $10.50 followed within weeks, with insiders taking $41m and $9m.
7. Stated result: up nearly 142% by February 2023.
8. The contrast with case 1: the child was given cash, not debt.

## Key Takeaways
1. Find where the debt goes before choosing parent or child.
2. Wait for forced selling to run its course. The chapter gives three different clocks.
3. Prefer spinoffs with growing revenue, a manageable cost of debt, and a leading position.
4. Count the parent as a candidate. It may recover the value of the spin.
5. Use insider buying in the spinoff as a catalyst check, not as proof.
6. Watch SOTP holding companies for the next spinoff.

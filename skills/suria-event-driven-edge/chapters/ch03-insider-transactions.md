# Chapter 3: Insider Transactions

## Core Idea
Insider purchases carry more signal than insider sales. With thousands of filings a year, the task is to filter for the few purchases that show a real variant perception, and to discard the many that are mechanical. In aggregate, the weekly ratio of insider selling to buying gives a market signal only at extremes.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Who is an insider**: management from the C-suite down to VP level, board members, and anyone owning more than 10% of the stock (including funds). Each files a Form 4 within two business days of a transaction.
- **Lynch's rule, amended**: Lynch said insiders sell for many reasons and buy for one. The author says the selling half is right and the buying half is "only partially right". Insiders can be anchored to a higher price, have "tunnel vision", and be slow to see macro or industry shifts that markets pick up quickly.
- **High-signal purchases (the five to track)**:
  1. Multiple companies in the same industry at the same time.
  2. Cluster buying within one company (more than one insider).
  3. Long-serving independent directors, above all those who are investment professionals.
  4. A chair of the board who has seen multiple cycles, mainly in cyclical businesses.
  5. Founder CEOs.
- **The hallmarks in the Netflix case**: a long-serving board member who had seen the company through two bear markets. A high-growth company that made "a strategic, but not fatal, mistake". An independent director with an investment background.
- **Low-signal transactions (exclude these)**:
  1. A cluster where every insider paid the exact same price. This points to a secondary offering, dividend reinvestment, or a company retirement plan.
  2. A purchase and a sale on the same day for the same shares and price. This is a transfer between accounts or people.
  3. Filings that arrive months or years late.
  4. Purchases by new directors or managers who must meet a holding requirement.
  5. Regular annual purchases.
  6. Contractually required purchases (Pinterest's new CEO had to buy $5m of stock within 60 days to qualify for a $20m grant).
  7. Option exercises followed by an immediate sale.
  8. Automatic sales to cover tax on vested RSUs (code F, sometimes miscoded as S).
- **The sell/buy ratio**: aggregate insider selling in a week divided by aggregate insider buying in that week, across all companies.
  - August 2010 to July 2022: mean 30.10, median 19.03. Very large single sales skew the mean.
  - A reading below 1 means insiders bought more than they sold. In over 10 years of tracking the author had not seen that until March 2020.
  - Use: "valuable input at the extremes", "combined with other indicators". The author "would not advocate using the sell/buy ratio as a market timing tool".
  - Caveat: both buying and selling fall sharply in earnings quiet periods. These usually start two weeks before quarter-end and last one to three days after the earnings release.
- **When selling matters**:
  1. Elevated aggregate selling across companies "can signal trouble ahead".
  2. A cluster of insiders selling at once while the stock price is falling. The sales must be regular sales, not option exercises or RSU vesting.
- **Flip-floppers**: insiders who were selling and then start buying, or the reverse. Under Section 16(b), the Short-Swing Profit rule, a purchase and sale inside six months forces the insider to give the profit back to the company. An insider who accepts that cost is signalling a changed view.
- **Forced selling**: a large holder liquidating for non-fundamental reasons creates an overhang. The stock "is likely to rebound to a more natural level" once the selling ends.
- **Reading a Form 4**: check the transaction date and not only the filing date. Check the transaction code: P purchase, S sale, M option exercise, F tax payment, G gift. Check direct versus indirect ownership. Read Table II for derivatives. Read "Explanation of Responses" for the footnotes. A Form 4/A corrects an earlier filing.

## Key Concepts
- **Cluster purchase**: purchases by multiple insiders of one company at the same time.
- **Variant perception**: a view of the stock that differs from the market's.
- **10b5-1 plan**: a pre-set plan for price, amount and dates of trades, set up before the insider holds material non-public information. The footnotes usually say if a trade was under one. The author says the plans "are subject to being gamed".
- **Regulation FD**: bars selective disclosure of material information.
- **Quiet period**: a company-set window around earnings in which insiders may not trade.
- **Opportunistic versus automatic**: a discretionary trade versus a mechanical one.

## Mental Models
- Think of insider buying as "an idea discovery tool". It "cannot replace deep research".
- Think of insiders as early, like value investors. Netflix "plateaued and built a base for several months" after the purchase. Enter gradually over weeks or months in a bear market.
- When an industry is in crisis and its insiders buy "in a frenzy", look for the strongest companies in it. "Take your time to do your due diligence and don't rush to buy at the first sign of trouble."
- Analysts can forecast better than managers when macro factors dominate the business (Hutton, Lee and Shu, discussed in Ch 4).

## Anti-patterns
- **Chasing the early return**: a Wharton study of 1975-1996 found about a quarter of the abnormal return accrues in five days and half in the first month. The author says that with algorithmic trading the chance to capture it is "likely gone".
- **Trying to buy every insider purchase**: hundreds or a thousand stocks, many small and illiquid.
- **Reading routine selling as bearish**: tech insiders dominated selling in the 2016 to 2021 bull market. Selling or shorting on that basis "would have caused significant damage".
- **Trusting a press-released purchase**: insiders know they are watched, and some buy "just to signal the market". The author links this to Soros's reflexivity. A press release is normally "a big red flag". Harold Hamm was the stated exception.
- **Reading the website and not the filing**: Musk's Tesla purchases from 2012 to 2017 were all in secondary offerings.

## Worked Example: Carvana (Case study 2)
1. The stock rose from about $30 in March 2020 to over $370 in August 2021.
2. A cluster of insiders sold more than $750m in two months near the top.
3. The stock lost nearly 95% by June 2022. Four insiders then bought as a cluster.
4. The stock more than doubled to over $50 by 16 August 2022.
5. It then fell to low single digits by December 2022. So the insider buying cluster failed.
6. The author's reasons: nine years of negative free cash flow, cars bought at high prices, and the $2.2bn purchase of KAR Global's auction business. The company tried to issue over $3bn of debt that "appeared to have few takers". Apollo took half at 10.25%. Net debt reached $7.2bn by end 2022, before capital leases, and "the market was pricing the stock for bankruptcy".

## Worked Example: Continental Resources (two entries)
- Entry one: follow the founder's June 2020 purchases at about $16.87.
- Entry two: the go-private bid at $70 with the stock at $65.05. That is 7.6%, or more than 22% annualized over four months. Hamm and family already owned 83%. The bid was raised to $74.28.

## Key Takeaways
1. Weight purchases over sales.
2. Rank a purchase by who bought, how rare the purchase is, and whether others bought with them.
3. Read the footnotes before you count a transaction as a signal.
4. Use the sell/buy ratio only at extremes, and never alone.
5. Treat cluster selling into a falling price as a prompt to recheck your thesis.
6. Expect insiders to be early.

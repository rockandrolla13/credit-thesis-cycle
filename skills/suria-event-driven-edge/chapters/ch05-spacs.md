# Chapter 5: SPACs

## Core Idea
A SPAC gives the early holder a redemption right at about $10 plus interest, and a warrant that survives redemption. That pair is the opportunity. After the merger the picture reverses: most de-SPAC companies fall hard, which the author treats as a hunting ground for short ideas.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **SPAC mechanics**:
  1. The IPO sells "units", usually at $10. A unit is usually one share plus a warrant or a fraction of one. Some SPACs include no warrants.
  2. Units split into shares and warrants 52 days after the IPO. Both then trade.
  3. The sponsor has a set period to find an operating company. The text gives "usually 12 to 24 months" in one place and "two years" in others.
  4. When a business combination is announced, a holder can vote for it or redeem for about the $10 IPO price "plus a little extra" from interest.
  5. Warrants stay outstanding after redemption.
  6. If no deal is found, the IPO proceeds go back to investors.
  7. Insider lockup is usually 12 months, against six for a traditional IPO.
  8. Sponsors typically own 20% of the combined company. They cannot take management fees from the IPO capital.
- **SPAC arbitrage** (the author's "closest thing to a free lunch in financial markets"): buy units after the IPO and before the split. If you dislike the target, redeem the shares for about $10 plus interest and keep the warrants "for free". The upside comes from the warrants and from any rise in the shares after an attractive deal is announced.
- **De-SPAC short**: "most of these companies performed poorly after the business combination was completed." The author tracks completed combinations for short ideas.
  - Limits he states: low-volume SPACs can be hard to borrow. Borrow fees can "erode gains". Momentum and meme rallies have hurt short sellers.
- **Viability test for the target**: check that the operating company is "a viable business and not a money-losing entity or a science project with pipe dreams of future success". The two cases turn on free cash flow, revenue trend, leverage, and macro tailwind.
- **EBITDA skepticism**: EBITDA removes interest and tax, and "there is no getting away from paying interest on debt or taxes on profits". "Adjusted" EBITDA became "a cookie jar" in which "one-time charges kept recurring like clockwork". The author prefers free cash flow, which Buffett calls owner's earnings.
- **Free cash flow versus net income check**: some gap is normal because of depreciation. If free cash flow is "consistently and significantly below net income" year after year, dig into why.
- **Full enterprise value**: market cap, plus net debt, plus capitalized leases.

## Key Concepts
- **Unit**: the security sold in a SPAC IPO.
- **Warrant**: a right to buy common shares at a set price. It can last five, 10 or 15 years.
- **LEAPs**: call options expiring more than a year out. Most go no further than 39 months.
- **De-SPAC**: the company after the merger.
- **Redemption**: handing shares back for the trust value at the vote.
- **Reverse merger**: a private company merging into a public shell. The author compares the SPAC boom to the Chinese reverse mergers of the early 2010s.
- **Rollup**: a company that grows by a series of acquisitions in a fragmented market.
- **Community-adjusted EBITDA**: WeWork's metric, which excluded design, development and marketing costs to turn a large loss into a positive number.

## Mental Models
- "An IPO is basically a company looking for money, while a SPAC is money looking for a company" (Don Butler, quoted in the summary).
- Think of the pre-merger SPAC as a floor near $10 with a free option, and the post-merger company as an ordinary stock that skipped IPO scrutiny.
- "Turnaround situations rarely turn around and if they happen to be leveraged companies, the probability goes down even more."
- Rollups that borrow heavily "have left a bad taste in the mouths of investors" (Valeant, Lifestance).

## Anti-patterns
- **Holding through the merger without a view on the business.**
- **Trusting projections in a SPAC presentation**: some companies later dropped "their earlier rosy projections" when they had to report earnings.
- **Valuing on EBITDA or adjusted EBITDA** and ignoring interest, tax, and recurring "one-time" items.
- **Stopping at market cap** and missing debt and lease commitments.
- **Letting a free warrant expire into a redemption**: Bowlero redeemed its warrants for $0.10 each four months after listing. Holders had a short window to exercise at $11.50.

## Worked Example: WeWork (Case study 1)
1. 2019: the first IPO attempt. The S-1 ran 220 pages. 2018 loss of $1.9bn on revenue of $1.8bn. The company had paid the founder nearly $6m of stock for the "We" trademark. The IPO was pulled. The founder left with $245m of stock and $200m of cash.
2. August 2020: BowX Acquisition Corp has its IPO. Buyers do not know the target. Each unit is one share and one-third of a warrant with an $11.50 exercise price.
3. October 2021: the combination closes. The stock trades over $14 intraday in the first week.
4. February 2022, the author's review:
   - Occupancy 59%.
   - Market cap $4.42bn. Net debt $3bn. Enterprise value $7.42bn.
   - Capitalized leases $18.4bn. Enterprise value including leases $25.82bn.
   - Q3 2021: revenue $661m and shrinking. Net loss $844m.
5. His thesis: the new CEO had run General Growth Properties after its Chapter 11. The author expected the same playbook, with equity wiped out and debt and leases renegotiated.
6. The model portfolio shorted at $6.40 in March 2022 and covered at $2.76 on 30 October 2022. In March 2023 the stock closed at $0.97 with a market cap of $713m.

## Worked Example: Bowlero (Case study 2)
- Over 320 bowling centers. More than 3,500 independent centers remain in the US as acquisition targets. The author flags the debt risk of a rollup.
- Merged with Isos Acquisition Corp at the end of 2021. The stock barely moved on announcement, then fell from about $10 to a little over $7 by early February 2022.
- It then more than doubled and stood more than 50% above the combination price.
- Why it differed from WeWork, per the author: revenue was growing for three years before Covid, the company was free cash flow positive at listing, and consumers returned to in-person activity while office demand stayed weak.

## Key Takeaways
1. Before the merger, the redemption right limits the downside and the warrant holds the upside.
2. After the merger, assume poor performance until the business proves otherwise.
3. Test the target on free cash flow, revenue trend and total obligations, including leases.
4. Check the borrow cost before any short.
5. Know your warrant's terms, including early redemption by the company.

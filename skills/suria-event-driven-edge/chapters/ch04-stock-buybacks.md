# Chapter 4: Stock Buybacks

## Core Idea
A buyback is management's judgment that the stock is cheap. That judgment is often wrong in cyclical industries, and sometimes it is only a signal sent to the market. The author tracks the actual fall in share count, not the announcement, and looks for buybacks that coincide with insider buying.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Why companies buy back**: to return value when the stock is cheap, to offset dilution from employee stock awards, and to raise reported earnings per share. Buybacks are tax-efficient against dividends. A 1% buyback tax took effect in 2023 under the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022.
- **Three methods**:
  1. *Open-market purchases.* Most flexible. The company can start, stop, or never follow through.
  2. *Accelerated share repurchase (ASR).* An investment bank buys large blocks on the company's behalf and delivers shares quickly. Used when management thinks the stock is "particularly cheap".
  3. *Tender offer* ("issuer tender offer"). The company offers a price, usually above market, or a price range. It may set a minimum tender condition. Oversubscription leads to proration.
- **Odd lot clause**: holders tendering fewer than 100 shares are accepted in full at the tender price. It was once "a near-guaranteed method of earning a profit". It is now rare, the stock tends to move up fast on announcement, and the cap is 99 shares.
- **The cyclical buyback trap**: "Stocks often look the cheapest at their cyclical peaks and companies are also flush with cash at those moments." Companies "often end up buying back stock near cyclical peaks". The shipping cycle shows the mechanism: high rates, new ship orders, delivery years later into weaker demand or oversupply, falling rates, losses, bankruptcies, then consolidation.
- **Supply discipline versus peak mistake**: a buyback near a cyclical top can still be sound if the industry is holding back supply. US frackers stopped chasing volume after the OPEC price war and returned cash. The author says the energy buybacks came "near a cyclical top, but the driving factors were very different" from Citigroup in 2006.
- **Uber cannibals** (Pabrai and Zhao, Forbes, December 2016): a backtest from 1992 to 2016 holding the five companies that repurchased the most stock in the prior year, rebalanced each 18 March. Filters:
  1. Market cap of at least $100m.
  2. No insurance companies.
  3. Revenue is growing.
  4. Price/sales below 2.5.
  5. Buyback percentage above the dividend yield by more than 2%.
  - Stated result: outperformed the S&P 500 by 6.3% annualized over a 26-year period.
- **Size the buyback against market cap, then against enterprise value**: the author prefers announcements that are large relative to market cap. He then checks net debt. A leveraged company buying back stock instead of paying down debt is, in "the simple answer", wrong. The nuanced answer weighs the cost and maturity of the debt, interest coverage, growth, and how undervalued the stock is.
- **Announcement versus action**: track the share count in the 10-Q and 10-K. An announcement may be followed by no purchases.
- **The Double Dipper screen**: companies buying back shares while their insiders independently buy on the open market. It shows the board's decision and the insiders' own money pointing the same way.
- **Spotting deliberate signaling** (Chan, Ikenberry, Lee and Wang, 7,628 open-market programs, 1980 to 2000): suspect firms got the same short-term lift and no long-term outperformance. Their marks:
  1. Management under pressure, with pay heavily exposed to stock options.
  2. Declining sales.
  3. A sharp fall in the stock price.
  4. Analysts revising earnings estimates down.
  - The suspected share was "well below 10%" of the sample.
- **Spotting misguided valuation** (Hutton, Lee and Shu): analysts forecast better than management when macro factors such as the business cycle, commodity prices and regulation drive the company. Management forecasts better when the issue is internal (high inventory growth) or the company is a multi-division "black box".
  - The author's rule: look for buybacks or insider buying while analysts revise estimates *up*. Avoid black boxes unless you have special insight.
- **Capital allocation audit** (end of Case study 2): before trusting a buyback, ask:
  1. When did management issue or pay down debt?
  2. Did past M&A grow revenue and improve net margin?
  3. Did they issue stock when it was expensive and buy when it was cheap?
  4. How is the company placed against peers, against intrinsic value, and against macro headwinds?
  5. Are managers buying with their own money, or selling while the company buys?

## Key Concepts
- **Proration factor**: the share of tendered stock accepted when a tender is oversubscribed.
- **Round lot / odd lot**: 100 shares / fewer than 100.
- **Value trap**: a cyclical company that looks cheap at its peak.
- **Short interest (as the author defines it)**: shares short divided by average daily volume, read as days to cover.
- **Net debt and enterprise value**: market cap plus net debt gives enterprise value.
- **Cannibal**: Munger's term for a company that keeps buying back its own shares.

## Mental Models
- Think of issuing and buying back as the two halves of one skill. Singleton used expensive stock to buy 130 companies in eight years, then bought back 90% of Teledyne over 12 years when it was cheap.
- Think of a new name in the top-five buyback list as a warning. Few names repeated in Pabrai's list (AutoZone and NVR were the exceptions), which the author reads as support for the cyclical-peak mistake.
- A buyback also says management cannot find a new business or acquisition worth funding.
- EPS growth from a shrinking share count is not business growth. DaVita retired nearly 50% of its shares from Q3 2017 to Q3 2022 and doubled EPS. Revenue grew under 20% and "the stock barely appreciated".

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying back into a spike**: Bed, Bath & Beyond bought most of its stock while it was a meme favorite.
- **Accelerating a buyback while the business weakens**: the same company finished a three-year program in one year.
- **Buying back with a leveraged balance sheet**: Franchise Group announced $500m, over 31% of market cap, with $2.35bn of net debt and $3.92bn of enterprise value.
- **Using capital right before a crisis**: Citigroup repurchased shares before 2007-2009 and then took the largest bailout of any American bank.
- **Treating the announcement as the purchase**.
- **Using buybacks as the primary signal**: the author uses them and insider buying as "idea generation mechanisms".

## Worked Example: two CEOs appointed within two months (Case studies 1 and 2)
| | Avis Budget Group | Bed, Bath & Beyond |
|---|---|---|

| Shock | Stock from over $50 to about $7.78 in under a month. Q2 2020 loss of $6.91 per share | Declining sales and losses of over $1bn |

The author's reading: both served consumers and both changed CEO at about the same time. The difference was operations and "most importantly, their capital allocation policy using buybacks".

## Key Takeaways
1. Count shares, not announcements.
2. Scale the buyback to market cap, then check the debt.
3. In a cyclical industry, ask whether the cash comes from a peak or from supply discipline.
4. Use analyst revisions as a check on management's view of its own value.
5. Prefer buybacks that insiders match with their own money.
6. Judge the buyback by management's past record of issuing and retiring capital.

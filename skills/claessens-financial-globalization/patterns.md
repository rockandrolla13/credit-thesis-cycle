# Patterns

Techniques and causal patterns from "A Cross-Country Perspective on the Causes of the Global
Financial Crisis" (Claessens, Dell'Ariccia, Igan, Laeven). Each pattern names its chapter file.

## Old-versus-new cause decomposition
**When to use**: opening any crisis diagnosis or "is this time different?" question. (Ch 1, Ch 2)
**How**: test the four commonalities (asset price bubble, credit boom, marginal loans and
systemic risk, regulatory failure). Then test the four new dimensions (opaqueness,
interconnectedness, financial leverage, household role). Assign origin to the first set and
reach and speed to the second.
**Trade-offs**: the authors do not rank the factors and say many are closely related. The
decomposition gives a checklist, not weights.

## Big 5 house price overlay
**When to use**: judging whether a housing boom matches a pre-crisis path. (Ch 1)
**How**: rebase the real house price index to 100 five years before the candidate crisis date.
Compare with the Big 5 average. The US reference values are a rise above 30% over five years and
a peak six quarters before the crisis.
**Trade-offs**: the crisis date is an assumption (2007 here). The benchmark averages only five
episodes.

## Credit boom size-and-duration screen
**When to use**: estimating how dangerous a credit boom is. (Ch 1)
**How**: measure boom duration in years and average annual credit-to-GDP growth. Longer and
larger booms ended in crisis more often (Figure 48.4). Then check the two mechanisms: leverage
of borrowers and lenders, and lending standards.
**Trade-offs**: most booms do not end in crisis. The longest and largest bins hold one to three
booms. Aggregate credit can hide a boom in one segment, as in US subprime.

## Bubble-plus-leverage pairing
**When to use**: ranking countries by vulnerability. (Ch 1)
**How**: flag countries with both rapid house price growth and a rapid buildup in household
leverage. Add capital inflows, which tracked credit growth in later crisis countries.
**Trade-offs**: the authors assert the pairing "turned out most dangerous" and show scatter
plots. They report no estimated threshold.

## Marginal-loan correlation test
**When to use**: assessing a loan book or a securitized pool. (Ch 1)
**How**: name the macro variable on which repayment depends. For US subprime it was rising house
prices and refinancing. For Eastern European foreign-currency loans it was a stable exchange
rate. If most loans share one variable, treat default correlation as high.
**Trade-offs**: the feature that creates the risk also made the loans affordable.

## Opacity-to-freeze chain
**When to use**: judging whether a price fall in a complex asset will freeze funding. (Ch 2)
**How**: check securitization share, re-tranching, reliance on ratings, off-balance-sheet
holdings and wholesale funding. If all are high, expect failed price discovery, interbank
adverse selection and forced sales.
**Trade-offs**: none of this is visible before prices fall.

## Liquidity-to-solvency leverage loop
**When to use**: deciding whether a liquidity event will become a solvency event. (Ch 2, Ch 3)
**How**: check leverage of core institutions. Follow the loop: losses, write-downs,
mark-to-market deleveraging, fire sales, hedge fund unwinding, lower prices, larger capital
needs.
**Trade-offs**: partial recapitalization does not break the loop.

## Overextended-sector classification
**When to use**: forecasting real-economy transmission and resolution speed. (Ch 2)
**How**: identify the overextended sector: official (Latin America, 1980s), corporate (Asian
crisis) or household (this crisis). For households, expect consumption cuts through wealth,
collateral and confidence effects, and slow restructuring.
**Trade-offs**: track debt-to-income and debt-to-assets separately. Japan shows they can
diverge.

## Catalyst / trigger / amplifier split
**When to use**: structuring a causal thesis. (Ch 3)
**How**: name the catalyst (the vulnerability), the trigger (the event) and the amplifiers (the
feedback loops). Attach each indicator to one layer.
**Trade-offs**: the layers interact. The authors note policy responses conditioned later events.

## Three-phase spillover map
**When to use**: locating a stress episode and choosing the relevant policy tool. (Ch 3)
**How**: Phase 1 is direct exposures. Phase 2 is asset markets: liquidity shortages, frozen
credit markets, equity falls, currency moves. Phase 3 is solvency of systemically important
institutions.
**Trade-offs**: phase boundaries are clear only afterwards. Liquidity tools lose effect by
phase 3.

## Five-form intervention classification
**When to use**: reading a rescue package. (Ch 4)
**How**: sort measures into liquidity provision, wholesale funding support, guarantees, asset
purchases or exchanges, and capital injections. Map them to Table 48.1 columns.
**Trade-offs**: pledged amounts are potential exposure, not cost. Central bank balance sheet
change (column D) has other causes too.

## Distortion audit
**When to use**: assessing second-round effects of any support measure. (Ch 4)
**How**: ask what it distorts directly, indirectly, over the long run and across borders. Check
who is forced to match it and who cannot.
**Trade-offs**: the authors hold that the interventions were necessary. The audit sizes the
cost. It does not argue against acting.

## Guarantee risk-transfer check
**When to use**: after a state guarantees bank liabilities. (Ch 4)
**How**: compare bank CDS spreads and the sovereign spread before and after. In Ireland bank
spreads fell sharply and the sovereign spread rose.
**Trade-offs**: the source gives direction only. It reports no spread levels.

## Coordinated exit
**When to use**: planning or forecasting the removal of support. (Ch 4)
**How**: sequence exit within fiscal constraints and macro conditions. Coordinate removal of
guarantees across countries to avoid large capital movements.
**Trade-offs**: the authors expect coordination to be difficult in practice.

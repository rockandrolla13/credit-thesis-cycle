# Cheatsheet

Decision aid for "A Cross-Country Perspective on the Causes of the Global Financial Crisis"
(Claessens, Dell'Ariccia, Igan, Laeven). Written as of mid-2009.

## Decision rules
- When house prices and household leverage both rise fast, treat the country as high risk,
  because the authors find this combination "most dangerous" (Ch 1).
- When a credit boom is present, raise crisis probability but keep "no crisis" as the base case,
  because only a minority of booms end in crisis (Ch 1).
- When a boom is long or large, raise the probability again, because both size and duration
  increase the odds (Ch 1).
- When aggregate credit looks calm, check segments, because the US boom sat in subprime and
  household debt while corporate credit was slow (Ch 1).
- When repayment of most loans depends on one macro variable, assume high default correlation
  (Ch 1).
- When someone names low policy rates or global imbalances as the main cause, discount it,
  because countries differed on both yet shared the crisis, and surplus countries had crises too
  (Ch 1).
- When funding is wholesale and short term and assets are opaque, expect a freeze once prices
  fall (Ch 2).
- When core institutions are highly leveraged, expect liquidity fears to become solvency fears
  quickly (Ch 2, Ch 3).
- When households are the overextended sector, expect transmission through consumption and a slow
  policy response (Ch 2).
- When one country issues a blanket guarantee, expect neighbours to match it and outflows from
  countries that cannot (Ch 4).
- When a state guarantees its banks, watch for bank spreads falling and the sovereign spread
  rising (Ch 4).
- When a cross-border bank fails with no burden-sharing rule, expect ring-fencing (Ch 4).

## Decision tree: which phase is the episode in? (Ch 3)
- Are losses confined to institutions holding the bad asset?
  - Yes → Phase 1, direct exposures. Watch wholesale-funded lenders.
  - No → Are funding markets, equities and currencies of unrelated assets disrupted?
    - Yes, and core solvency is not questioned → Phase 2, asset markets. Liquidity tools help
      for a short time.
    - Yes, and core solvency is questioned → Phase 3. Only recapitalization and guarantees
      address it.

## Trade-off matrix: intervention forms (Ch 4)

| Form | What it fixes | Distortion the authors name |
|---|---|---|
| Liquidity provision | Short-term funding stress | Central bank replaces private transactions |
| Wholesale funding support | Specific markets such as commercial paper | Purposely distorts short-term markets |
| Guarantees | Runs on deposits and liabilities | Risk moves to the sovereign. Beggar-thy-neighbor |
| Asset purchases | Illiquid or nonperforming assets | Distorted asset prices and flows |
| Capital injections | Solvency | State ownership, pay caps, weaker intermediation |

## Tells and smells
- Debt service at a record high while interest rates are low → leverage, not rates, is the
  problem (Ch 1).
- Households borrowing in a foreign currency for a lower rate → systemic exposure to the exchange
  rate (Ch 1).
- Lending growing fast outside the regulatory perimeter → systemic risk building unseen (Ch 1).
- Investors pricing credit on ratings alone → exposure is masked, including at insurers (Ch 2).
- Debt-to-income and debt-to-assets both rising → no equity cushion, unlike Japan (Ch 2).
- A mortgage lender funded in interbank markets → run risk, as at Northern Rock (Ch 2, Ch 3).
- Multiple-notch downgrades across one asset class → the pricing model has failed (Ch 3).
- Banks taking vehicles back on balance sheet → interbank rates and ABCP are next (Ch 3).
- Liquidity injections with fading effect → the problem is solvency (Ch 3).
- Support tied to local lending → cross-border retrenchment (Ch 4).
- Falling political support for rescues → more national bias and more distortion (Ch 4).

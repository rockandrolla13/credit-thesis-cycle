## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Old-versus-new decomposition of crisis causes (ch01, ch02)
The authors' bottom line: the crisis "may appear unique but it shares many features common with
other financial crises". Use two checklists.

**Four commonalities with previous crises** (explain the boom):
1. Asset price increases that turned out to be unsustainable.
2. Credit booms that led to excessive debt burdens.
3. Buildup of marginal loans and systemic risk.
4. Failure of regulation and supervision to keep up with and get ahead of the crisis.

**Four new dimensions** (explain severity, transmission and global scale):
1. Widespread use of complex and opaque financial instruments.
2. Increased interconnectedness among financial markets, with the United States at the core.
3. High degree of leverage of financial institutions.
4. Central role of the household sector.

The authors do not rank these factors. They explicitly set aside monetary policy and global
imbalances as top causes: countries differed too much on both and still shared the crisis, and
many surplus countries had crises too.

### 2. House price boom template: the Big 5 overlay (ch01)
Rebase real house prices to 100 five years before the crisis date. Compare with the average of
the Big 5 banking crises (Spain 1977, Norway 1987, Finland 1991, Sweden 1991, Japan 1992). The US
rose more than 30% in five years and peaked six quarters before the crisis. Prefer the pair
"fast house prices plus fast leverage" over either signal alone. The authors call that
combination the most dangerous.

### 3. Credit boom → crisis (ch01)
- Only a minority of credit booms end in crisis. The probability still rises with a boom.
- Larger and longer booms end in crisis more often. Chart readings from Figure 48.4
  (approximate): about 3% for 1-year booms (n = 33), about 50% for 6-year booms (n = 8), about
  20% for credit-to-GDP growth of 1–4% a year (n = 81), about 36% for 9–12% (n = 11).
- Two mechanisms: more leverage of borrowers and lenders, and lower lending standards.
- Macro signature: output, consumption and investment above trend in the buildup and below trend
  in the unwinding. The current account deteriorates, capital inflows surge, and house prices and
  the real exchange rate rise.
- Use segments, not aggregates. The US boom was in subprime and household debt while corporate
  credit was slow.
- Credit growth tracked capital inflows most clearly in the countries that later had a crisis.

### 4. Marginal-loan correlation test (ch01)
Ask which single macro variable repayment depends on. US subprime depended on rising house prices
and refinancing. Eastern European loans in euros, Swiss francs and yen depended on a stable
exchange rate. One shared variable means high default correlation and systemic exposure.

### 5. Opacity → agency problems → freeze (ch02)
Nonconforming US mortgages securitized rose from under 35% (2000) to over 70% (2007). Delinking
of borrower and lender weakened due diligence. Investors relied on inflated ratings. When prices
fell, complexity blocked price discovery, securitization froze, and opaque balance sheets caused
adverse selection in interbank markets. Wholesale and short-term funding turned this into forced
sales.

### 6. Leverage loop: liquidity → solvency → fire sales (ch02, ch03)
High leverage means small losses cannot be absorbed. Liquidity concerns turn into solvency
worries. Write-downs and mark-to-market rules force deleveraging and fire sales. Hedge funds add
selling. Prices fall and capital needs rise. Early recapitalizations reached only a few banks and
fell short of losses.

### 7. Household-centred crisis (ch02)
Classify crises by the overextended sector: official (Latin America, 1980s), corporate (Asian
crisis), household (this crisis). A household crisis transmits through wealth, collateral and
confidence effects on consumption. It resolves slowly because of moral hazard, the number of
cases, and equity and distribution issues. Track debt-to-income and debt-to-assets separately. In
Japan's boom the first rose and the second fell.

### 8. US-at-the-core hub (ch02)
US financial assets were about 31% of global financial assets. The dollar was about 62% of
reserve currency assets. Any US shock has global effects. Distressed intermediaries spread it
through "common lender effects". Emerging markets reliant on external financing, and those with
more liquid markets, were hit through capital account and bank-funding pressures.

### 9. Catalyst, trigger, amplification (ch03)
- Catalyst: overextended US housing and mortgage markets.
- Trigger: the turnaround in US house prices, partly after monetary tightening.
- Amplification: frozen markets and fire sales.

### 10. Three phases of global spillover (ch03)
1. Direct exposures: IKB (July 2007), BNP Paribas (August 2007), Northern Rock (fall 2007).
2. Asset markets: liquidity shortages, frozen credit markets, equity falls, currency moves.
3. Solvency: after Lehman Brothers. Near meltdown in October 2008.

Use liquidity tools in phases 1 and 2. Their effect "proved short lived". Phase 3 needs
recapitalization. The authors call the mid-October 2008 shift "overdue".

### 11. Five forms of intervention and their distortions (ch04)
Forms: (i) liquidity provision, (ii) wholesale funding support, (iii) guarantees, (iv) asset
purchases or exchanges, (v) capital injections. The authors judge them "necessary but distortive
by nature". Distortions are direct, indirect, long-lasting and international.

Scale as of June 2009, advanced economies, percent of 2008 GDP: capital 3.4, treasury asset
purchases and lending 5.3, guarantees 14.0, central bank support 6.9, upfront financing 5.5.
Ireland's guarantees were 198.1%. These are pledges and potential exposure, not final cost.

### 12. Guarantee dynamics (ch04)
- Risk transfer: after Ireland's guarantee, bank CDS spreads fell sharply and the sovereign
  spread rose.
- Beggar-thy-neighbor: one guarantee forces others. Countries that cannot match suffer outflows.
- Chain reaction: the US money market fund guarantee drew deposits from banks, which forced
  higher deposit insurance.
- Ring-fencing: with no burden-sharing rule, countries grab local assets of failing cross-border
  banks (UK and Icelandic banks, Germany and Lehman).

### 13. Exit and coordination (ch04)
Plan exit from guarantees, enlarged deposit insurance, ownership and asset holdings within fiscal
and macro limits. Coordinate the removal of guarantees to avoid large capital movements. The
authors expect coordination to be difficult in practice.

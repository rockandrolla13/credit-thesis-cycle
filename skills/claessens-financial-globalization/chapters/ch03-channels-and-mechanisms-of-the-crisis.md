# Chapter 3: The Evolution of the Crisis — Channels and Mechanisms

Source sections: "The Evolution of the Crisis" and "The Channels and Mechanisms of the Crisis".
Printed pp. 746–748.

## Core Idea
A crisis has underlying causes, then a catalyst, a trigger and amplification mechanisms. Here
the catalyst was the overextended US housing and mortgage markets, the trigger was the turn in
US house prices, and the shock went global in three phases: direct exposures, asset markets,
then solvency after Lehman Brothers. The authors call it the first global financial crisis
since the Great Depression.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Catalyst / trigger / amplification taxonomy**: separate four layers of any crisis.
  - Underlying causes: the eight factors of Chapters 1 and 2.
  - Catalyst: the overextended US housing and mortgage markets.
  - Trigger: the turnaround in US house prices, "in part related to a cycle of monetary policy
    tightening". The subprime sector was the main initiator of the turmoil.
  - Amplification mechanisms: frozen markets and fire sales, where falling prices trigger more
    sales as institutions become undercapitalized and risk premiums rise.
  - When to use: to keep a thesis from confusing what started a move with what made it large.
  - How: name one item per layer. Then ask which layer a given indicator measures.

- **Three phases of global spillover**:
  1. **Phase 1 — direct exposures.** Limited to banks with direct exposure to the US market and
     to a few financial markets. Some cases were liquidity runs linked to excessive wholesale
     funding. Cases: IKB in Germany (July 2007), BNP Paribas in France (August 2007), Northern
     Rock in the UK (bank run, fall 2007).
  2. **Phase 2 — asset markets.** Transmission through liquidity shortages, freezing of credit
     markets, stock price declines and foreign exchange moves (UK sterling, euro, Swiss franc).
  3. **Phase 3 — solvency.** After the collapse of Lehman Brothers. In October 2008 solvency
     concerns hit systemically important global institutions, with massive sell-offs and the
     risk of a financial meltdown.
  - When to use: to place a developing stress episode and to judge which policy tool still fits.
  - How: check (a) whether losses sit only with directly exposed holders, (b) whether funding and
    asset markets in unrelated assets are disrupted, (c) whether the market questions the
    solvency of core institutions.
  - Why it works / failure mode: each phase widens the set of affected parties from holders, to
    markets, to the whole system. Liquidity tools suit phases 1 and 2. They do not answer
    phase 3. The authors call the mid-October 2008 shift from liquidity to solvency action
    "overdue".

- **US-side escalation chain (between phases 1 and 2)**:
  1. Credit deterioration in subprime mortgages is worse than expected.
  2. Major rating agencies make "surprising multiple-notch downgrades".
  3. Spreads on asset-backed securities widen sharply.
  4. Liquidity is disrupted in interbank and commercial paper markets.
  5. Uncertainty and opacity about counterparty risk amplify the disruption.
  6. Commercial banks absorb legally separate vehicles, which strains their balance sheets.
  7. Interbank rates spike and asset-backed commercial paper issuance contracts sharply.

- **Limits of liquidity provision**: central bank liquidity cannot substitute for trust in
  counterparties.
  - Central banks made liquidity injections that grew larger and more flexible in maturity and
    in the collateral accepted. Their calming effect on interbank markets "proved short lived".
  - Currency swaps between major central banks were needed to supply US dollar funding abroad.
  - Central bank liquidity "increasingly replaced private transactions" but could not remove
    unease about the viability of internationally active banks.
  - Doubt extended to credit insurance and to counterparties in the "massive but unregulated"
    credit default swap market, through the weakening of ultimate insurers.

- **Resolution-framework deficiencies (three gaps)**: advanced-economy frameworks failed on
  - Scope: investment banks and insurance corporations were not covered.
  - Coordination: for example between deposit insurance and lender of last resort facilities.
  - Speed: there were no specific frameworks for bank resolution.
  - Recognition of insolvency was also delayed. Together these let problems intensify.

## Key Concepts
- **Catalyst**: the vulnerable condition that makes a crisis possible (overextended US housing
  and mortgage markets).
- **Trigger**: the event that starts it (the turn in US house prices).
- **Amplification mechanism**: a feedback process that enlarges the initial shock.
- **Fire sales**: forced asset sales into a falling market, in a cycle of declining prices and
  further sales.
- **Direct exposure**: holdings of subprime-related assets by an institution.
- **Liquidity run**: withdrawal of wholesale funding from an institution reliant on it.
- **Interbank spread**: in Figure 48.13, 3-month LIBOR minus a 3-month borrowing rate, in
  percent.
- **Asset-backed commercial paper (ABCP)**: short-term paper funding off-balance-sheet vehicles.
  Issuance contracted sharply.
- **Forced deleveraging**: balance sheet contraction compelled by losses, funding loss or
  mark-to-market rules.
- **Ultimate insurers**: the sellers of credit protection whose weakening put the reliability of
  credit insurance in doubt.

## Mental Models
- Use the phase number to choose the question. Phase 1: "who holds it?" Phase 2: "who funds in
  the same markets?" Phase 3: "who is solvent?"
- Think of a rating downgrade of several notches as an information shock, not only a credit
  event. It reveals that the models were wrong for a whole asset class.
- Think of off-balance-sheet vehicles as contingent on-balance-sheet assets. Banks took them
  back under stress.
- Treat piecemeal policy as an amplifier. The authors write that "ad hoc and piecemeal
  interventions created, at times, further disruptions and loss of confidence".

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a solvency problem with liquidity**: liquidity support calmed markets only briefly.
- **Ad hoc, piecemeal intervention**: it added disruption and reduced creditor and investor
  confidence.
- **Delaying recognition of insolvency**: delay let problems intensify.
- **Resolution regimes that exclude nonbanks**: investment banks and insurers fell outside
  existing frameworks.
- **Relying on unregulated bilateral credit insurance**: AIG nearly collapsed through its credit
  default swap exposures before receiving public funds.
- **Partial recapitalization**: early capital raisings were limited to a few banks and
  "increasingly fell short of losses".

## Worked Example
**Walking the crisis through the taxonomy, as the authors do.**

- Catalyst: US housing and mortgage markets are overextended.
- Trigger: US house prices turn, partly after monetary tightening. Subprime defaults start the
  turmoil.
- Domestic broadening: the problem moves from subprime to the wider US housing market and then
  to other US markets, such as other asset-backed securities.
- Phase 1: European banks holding subprime assets report problems (IKB, BNP Paribas). Interbank
  markets tighten. US stress exposes housing weakness in Western Europe. Northern Rock suffers a
  run.
- Escalation: downgrades, wider ABS spreads, ABCP contraction, banks absorbing vehicles.
- Phase 2: liquidity shortages, frozen credit markets, equity and currency moves. Central banks
  inject liquidity and open swap lines. Relief is temporary.
- Phase 3: Lehman Brothers collapses. Its interconnections shock confidence globally. Money
  market funds face runs. AIG nearly fails. Leveraged US investment banks and European
  commercial banks are forced to deleverage. Hedge funds add to the selling.
- End state: near meltdown in October 2008, then system-wide intervention (Chapter 4).

The authors' judgement on the sequence: events and policy responses conditioned each other in
ways that, "at least in retrospect, made the crisis more severe".

## Key Takeaways
1. Name the catalyst, the trigger and the amplifier separately. They call for different
   indicators.
2. Spillover moves from direct holders, to shared funding and asset markets, to solvency of the
   core.
3. Interbank spreads mark the phases: a first step up in August 2007 and a spike in fall 2008.
4. Liquidity support buys time and does not restore trust in counterparties.
5. A failure of one highly interconnected institution can move the system from phase 2 to
   phase 3.
6. Leverage at US investment banks and European commercial banks was what made phase 3 a forced
   deleveraging cycle.
7. Resolution gaps in scope, coordination and speed lengthen a crisis.

# Chapter 4: Government Interventions to Resolve the Crisis, and Conclusions

Source sections: "Government Interventions to Resolve the Crisis" and "Conclusions: Crisis
Resolution Going Forward and the Path to Economic Recovery". Printed pp. 748–752. The "See
also" line and the reference list (p. 752) are folded in under Connects To.

## Core Idea
Interventions were necessary and worked to stabilize financial systems, but they are distortive
by nature. They distort directly, indirectly, for a long time, and across borders. Because they
were mostly national, they produced beggar-thy-neighbor effects and ring-fencing. Exit needs
international coordination that the authors expect to be hard to achieve.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Five principal forms of intervention**:
  1. Liquidity provision through collateralized lending and other schemes.
  2. Support for short-term wholesale funding markets.
  3. More extensive guarantees of retail deposits and other liabilities.
  4. Purchases or exchanges of nonperforming or illiquid assets.
  5. Capital injections to banks.
  - Alongside these: more accommodative monetary and fiscal policy, which "did not stop the
    decline", and large external financial support for several emerging markets.
  - When to use: to classify any announced rescue measure and map it to a Table 48.1 column.

- **Distortion taxonomy ("necessary but distortive by nature")**:
  - Direct distortions: support of financial institutions in nonmarket ways. Examples: central
    bank intervention in short-term markets, the US money market fund guarantee.
  - Indirect distortions: skewed resource allocation in the real sector. Examples: lending
    programs for small and medium enterprises, recapitalization of large nonfinancial firms in
    Japan (law passed April 2009, through the Development Bank of Japan), support for car
    companies in the United States, France and Italy, informal local-lending requirements.
  - Long-lasting distortions: more state ownership and a larger state role in finance. The
    authors call the long-term consequences of state-owned banks "well documented". US pay caps
    changed incentives at supported and unsupported institutions.
  - International distortions: effects on cross-border capital flows and competition.
  - How: for each measure ask (1) which price it changes, (2) which flow it redirects, (3) who
    must match it, (4) how it will be removed.
  - Why it works / failure mode: a guarantee does not remove risk. It moves it to the guarantor
    and to anyone who cannot match the guarantee.

- **Guarantee chain reaction (US case)**:
  1. One money market fund "broke the buck" (net asset value below one dollar). Large outflows
     followed.
  2. The authorities guaranteed money market funds.
  3. Deposits then flowed out of commercial banks.
  4. The authorities raised deposit insurance coverage.

- **Bank-to-sovereign risk transfer (Ireland case)**:
  - Before the guarantee: CDS spreads on the large Irish commercial banks were very high.
  - After the guarantee: bank CDS spreads fell sharply and the sovereign spread rose.
  - Table 48.1 shows the scale: Irish guarantees of 198.1% of 2008 GDP.

- **Beggar-thy-neighbor guarantees**:
  1. One country issues guarantees on deposits and other liabilities. Ireland was first.
  2. Other countries are forced to follow.
  3. Closely integrated countries (the European Union/EMU) coordinate, for example on uniform
     deposit guarantee coverage.
  4. Emerging markets that cannot match the guarantees suffer capital outflows as depositors and
     creditors seek safe havens.

- **Ring-fencing ("asset grabs")**: when a cross-border entity looks likely to fail and no
  burden-sharing mechanism exists, countries seize assets in their own jurisdiction.
  - UK supervisors used the Anti-Terrorism, Crime, and Security Act to ring-fence Icelandic bank
    assets in the United Kingdom.
  - Germany froze Lehman's assets to keep cash available for depositors ahead of the US
    bankruptcy proceedings.
  - The authors add that such actions were partly anticompetitive, since they favoured local
    interests.

- **Dollar-funding asymmetry**: US actions first focused on domestic support, though interbank
  prices showed dollar-funding pressure for European banks and emerging markets.
  - Mature markets: "it took several weeks to act". Bilateral central bank swap lines followed
    and widened, and market prices still showed problems.
  - Emerging markets: the response was slower and the amounts more limited. External support
    arrived after "the real consequences had already been incurred".

- **Exit and coordination principle**: governments need to plan the exit from guarantees, large
  deposit insurance, ownership and asset acquisitions, within fiscal constraints and with regard
  to macroeconomic conditions.
  - Uncoordinated unwinding can weaken confidence, create new distortions and be anticompetitive.
  - Removal of guarantees most needs coordination, "to avoid large capital movements".
  - The authors' own caveat: more coordination, while desirable, "will in practice be difficult".

## Key Concepts
- **Upfront government financing**: gross support that requires upfront government outlays. It
  excludes recovery from later sale of acquired assets (Table 48.1, column E).
- **Announced or pledged amounts**: the basis of columns A, B, C and E. They are not actual
  uptake.
- **Contingent costs**: costs that are hard to quantify, such as asset insurance schemes and
  higher deposit insurance limits.
- **"Broke the buck"**: a money market fund's net asset value falling below one dollar.
- **Beggar-thy-neighbor effects**: one country's guarantee forcing others to follow or lose
  funds.
- **Ring-fencing**: a jurisdiction isolating a failing cross-border entity's local assets.
- **Burden-sharing mechanism**: an agreed rule for splitting the cost of a cross-border bank
  failure. None was clear.
- **Zombie firms**: inefficient firms kept alive by support, which may drive out efficient firms.
- **Exit**: withdrawal of the intervention packages.
- **Rules of the game in flux**: uncertainty over how shareholders and creditors are treated when
  large institutions are restructured.

## Mental Models
- Think of a blanket guarantee as a swap of bank credit risk for sovereign credit risk. Ireland
  is the reference case.
- Use "who cannot match this?" to find the loser from any national guarantee. In 2008 the answer
  was many emerging markets.
- Treat announced support as potential exposure, not as cost. Footnote 7 says the mid-2009
  numbers "represent more the potential exposures than necessarily the actual costs".
- Read falling political support as a signal of more nationally oriented support and more
  distortion. The authors state this as a conditional.
- Think of export-led recovery as unavailable in a synchronized recession. Countries with large
  foreign exchange exposures lose that recovery channel.

## Anti-patterns
- **Comparing column D with columns A–C as like for like**: column D is the actual change in
  central bank balance sheets from June 2007 to April 2009. The other columns are pledges.
- **Reading central bank balance sheet growth as crisis support everywhere**: Hungary's reflects
  IMF and EU disbursements, Saudi Arabia's reflects foreign asset accumulation, and Norway's
  includes sovereign wealth fund assets (table notes h, r, s).
- **Unilateral guarantees**: they spread turmoil to other markets and force matching.
- **Support with a local-lending bias**: it disadvantages international operations.
- **Uncoordinated exit**: it risks large capital movements.
- **Assuming the fiscal figures are a ceiling**: "past experiences suggest that amounts can
  increase".

## Worked Example
**Tracing one national guarantee through the system (the Irish guarantee, as the authors
describe it).**

1. Starting point: CDS spreads on the large Irish commercial banks are very high.
2. Action: Ireland extends guarantees to its largest banks. Table 48.1 records guarantees of
   198.1% of 2008 GDP, against capital injection of 5.9%.
3. Direct price effect: bank CDS spreads fall sharply. The sovereign spread rises. Risk moved
   from bank to state.
4. Cross-border effect: other countries are forced to follow with similar measures.
5. Integrated blocs coordinate. The European Union/EMU adopts uniform deposit guarantee
   coverage.
6. Those who cannot match lose. Many emerging markets suffer capital outflows as depositors and
   creditors move to safe havens. The spread of guarantees causes further turmoil elsewhere.
7. Exit problem: removing the guarantee later risks the reverse capital movement, so removal
   needs coordination.

Scale comparison from Table 48.1: guarantees were 49.7% of GDP in the United Kingdom, 47.5% in
Sweden, 33.6% in the Netherlands, 30.1% in Austria and 10.9% in the United States. Emerging
economies averaged 0.1%.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify every rescue measure into one of five forms, then ask what it distorts.
2. Guarantees were the largest pledged item in advanced economies: 14.0% of GDP on average, or
   USD 4,646 billion.
3. Emerging economies needed little capital or guarantee support. Their column D average of
   13.6% of GDP is central bank balance sheet change, part of which is not crisis support.
4. A bank guarantee shows up in sovereign spreads. Watch the bank CDS and sovereign spread pair.
5. National measures export stress to countries that cannot match them.
6. Without burden-sharing rules, expect ring-fencing of failing cross-border banks.
7. Exit is itself a risk event. Coordinated removal of guarantees matters most.

# Chapter 2: New Dimensions of the Crisis

Source section: "New Dimensions of the Crisis" (Increased opaqueness; Financial integration and
interconnectedness; The role of leverage; Central role for households). Printed pp. 742–746.

## Core Idea
Four features were new and explain the severity and global reach of the crisis: opaque complex
instruments, interconnected markets with the United States at the core, highly leveraged
financial institutions, and an overextended household sector. These features acted mainly on
transmission and amplification, not on the origin of the boom.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The four new dimensions**:
  1. Widespread use of complex and opaque financial instruments.
  2. Increased interconnectedness among financial markets, nationally and internationally, with
     the United States at the core.
  3. High degree of leverage of financial institutions.
  4. Central role of the household sector.
  - When to use: to judge how far and how fast a local credit bust can spread.
  - How: score each dimension for the system in question, then map it to the amplification
    channel it feeds (table under Reference Tables).

- **Opaqueness → agency problems → market freeze (causal chain)**:
  1. Securitization expands beyond conforming loans. Nonconforming US mortgages securitized went
     from less than 35% in 2000 to more than 70% by 2007.
  2. Cash flows are re-tranched into further securities (CDOs and others).
  3. Borrower and lender are delinked. Risk assignment becomes unclear. Due diligence incentives
     weaken. Originators monitor less and chase fee volume.
  4. Investors cannot or will not assess underlying value and rely on ratings. Rating
     deficiencies produce inflated, less informative ratings.
  5. Ratings mask exposure at institutions seen as prudent, such as insurance companies.
  6. House prices fall and defaults rise. Complexity undermines price discovery. Markets turn
     illiquid and securitization freezes.
  7. Opaque balance sheets, including off-balance-sheet instruments, stop the market separating
     healthy from unhealthy institutions. Adverse selection freezes interbank markets.
  8. Institutions sell securities to raise funds, which deepens the fall.
  - Why it works / failure mode: the chain needs a price fall to start. Before step 6 opaqueness
    is invisible in spreads. Reliance on wholesale and short-term funding is what converts
    opacity into a run.

- **Interconnectedness and the "common lender effect"**:
  - Intermediaries hit by losses and short of liquidity sell other assets and cut lending. The
    shock reaches markets and institutions that had no direct exposure.
  - Early cross-border channels named by the authors: liquidity pressures, a global sell-off in
    equities (financial stocks in particular), and depletion of bank capital.
  - Direct-exposure cases: IKB in Germany (July 2007) and BNP Paribas' money market fund in
    France (August 2007).
  - Emerging markets were hit through capital account and bank-funding pressures. The most
    exposed were those that relied heavily on external financing and, "paradoxically", those
    with more liquid markets.

- **US-at-the-core hub argument**: a shock to the US has global effects because of size and
  centrality.
  - US financial assets were about 31% of global financial assets.
  - The US dollar share in reserve currency assets was about 62%.
  - US assets were held for their combination of safety and liquidity by private and public
    investors.

- **Leverage as shock multiplier (liquidity → solvency → fire sale loop)**:
  1. High leverage means small losses cannot be absorbed.
  2. Initial liquidity concerns turn quickly into solvency worries. Counterparty risk rises.
  3. Institutions take losses and write down illiquid securities.
  4. Mark-to-market rules force more deleveraging and fire sales.
  5. Hedge funds, facing financing constraints and redemptions, add to the unwinding.
  6. Asset prices fall further, recapitalization needs rise, confidence falls again.
  7. The loop came "to a near melt down in October 2008".
  - Why it works / failure mode: early recapitalizations were large and fast, including from
    sovereign wealth funds, but reached only a few banks and "increasingly fell short of losses".
    Partial recapitalization does not stop the loop.

- **Household-centred crisis framework**: classify a crisis by the sector that is overextended.
  - Official sector: for example Latin America's debt crisis of the 1980s.
  - Corporate sector: for example the Asian crisis.
  - Household sector: this crisis, through nontraditional mortgage loans.
  - Consequence 1: transmission to the real economy runs through wealth, collateral and
    confidence effects on consumption.
  - Consequence 2: resolution is slower. Household debt restructuring faces moral hazard, a very
    large number of cases, limited information, and equity and distribution issues. Corporate
    restructuring, by contrast, has established international best practice.

- **Household leverage versus household indebtedness (the Japan contrast)**: track debt-to-income
  and debt-to-assets separately.
  - Japan's real estate run-up: debt-to-income rose sharply, but debt-to-assets fell. Homeowners
    built equity as prices rose.
  - This crisis: loan-to-income values were larger than in the past and high loan-to-value
    mortgages meant "even moderate declines in house prices" pushed many households into negative
    equity.

## Key Concepts
- **Opaqueness**: complexity of instruments and balance sheets that prevents outsiders from
  valuing assets or telling sound institutions from unsound ones.
- **Nonconforming mortgages**: loans outside the underwriting standards of the
  Government-Sponsored Enterprises (GSEs).
- **Agency problems**: weakened incentives for due diligence and monitoring when the originator
  does not hold the loan.
- **Adverse selection (interbank)**: lenders withdraw from all counterparties because they cannot
  identify the weak ones.
- **Common lender effects**: spillover through a shared, distressed intermediary that sells
  assets and cuts lending elsewhere.
- **Gross cross-border positions**: external assets plus liabilities. These rose massively,
  mostly among OECD countries.
- **Sudden stop**: an abrupt loss or reversal of foreign funding, which hit emerging economies
  during global deleveraging.
- **Negative equity**: a mortgage balance above the value of the home, which creates an incentive
  to "walk away".
- **Wealth, collateral and confidence effects**: the three routes by which household balance
  sheet stress cuts consumption.

## Mental Models
- Think of financial integration as two-sided. The authors credit it with more risk sharing,
  competition and efficiency, and with a higher risk of transmitting shocks across borders.
- Use "who is overextended?" to predict the speed of resolution. Households are the slowest
  sector to restructure.
- Think of wholesale and short-term funding as the fuse. Leverage and opacity are the charge.
- Treat a benign global backdrop (low interest rates, narrow risk spreads) as a reason to expect
  simultaneous booms in many countries. The US crisis was "the spark that triggered the unwinding
  of imbalances in other countries".

## Anti-patterns
- **Pricing credit risk on ratings alone**: ratings were inflated and less informative, and
  investors skipped their own assessment.
- **Assuming insurers and other "prudent" institutions are unexposed**: ratings hid their
  exposure.
- **Funding long assets in interbank markets**: Northern Rock relied on interbank markets and
  not deposits, and suffered a bank run in fall 2007.
- **Reading rising debt-to-income as harmless because assets are rising**: that held in Japan,
  where debt-to-assets fell. It did not hold here.
- **Counting on a few early recapitalizations**: they fell short of losses.
- **Assuming liquid emerging markets are safer in a global deleveraging**: the authors list
  those with more liquid markets among the most affected and call this a paradox. They give no
  mechanism.

## Worked Example
**The US household vicious cycle, as the authors trace it.**

1. Vulnerable borrower cohorts face rising interest rates and falling home values.
2. They can no longer refinance. Monthly payments rise. Delinquencies and defaults rise.
3. Finance companies can no longer securitize subprime mortgages and fail in a wave. Mortgage
   origination virtually breaks down.
4. Foreclosures add downward pressure on house prices. This is the adverse feedback loop.
5. US house prices fall on a national basis for the first time since the Depression era. Heavily
   indebted borrowers in negative equity have an incentive to "walk away".
6. Lenders tighten standards on new mortgages and consumer credit.
7. Household savings and net assets are already at historic lows, so tighter credit passes
   straight into lower consumer spending.
8. Lower spending cuts corporate profits, causes layoffs and raises unemployment. That slows the
   economy and produces more foreclosures. The cycle starts local and spreads.

The same balance sheet weakness existed elsewhere. Debt-to-income rose sharply in the UK, Spain
and Ireland. Household credit grew fast in several emerging markets. Falling real estate prices
then damaged loan portfolios, most where values had grown fastest.

## Key Takeaways
1. Old features explain the boom. New features explain the reach and speed of the bust.
2. Watch the share of credit funded through securitization and wholesale markets. It measures
   both opacity and run risk.
3. Leverage decides how fast a liquidity problem becomes a solvency problem.
4. Track household debt-to-income and debt-to-assets together. A falling debt-to-assets ratio
   offsets a rising debt-to-income ratio. A rising one compounds it.
5. A household-centred crisis transmits to the real economy through consumption and resolves
   slowly.
6. The US is the hub. About 31% of global financial assets and about 62% of reserve currency
   assets make any US shock global.
7. External funding reliance and market liquidity both raised emerging-market exposure to
   sudden stops.

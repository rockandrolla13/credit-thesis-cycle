# Chapter 1: Introduction and Causes of the Crisis — Commonalities with Previous Crises

Source sections: "Introduction", "Causes of the Crisis", "Commonalities with Previous Crises"
(Asset price bubbles; Credit booms; Marginal loans and systemic risk; Regulation and
supervision). Printed pp. 737–742 of Chapter 48, "A Cross-Country Perspective on the Causes of
the Global Financial Crisis" (Claessens, Dell'Ariccia, Igan, Laeven), in *The Evidence and
Impact of Financial Globalization* (Elsevier, © 2013). The authors wrote it as of mid-2009.

## Core Idea
The 2007–09 crisis looks unique but shares four features with earlier financial crises: an
unsustainable asset price boom, a credit boom, a buildup of marginal loans with correlated
default risk, and regulation that failed to keep up. The authors explain the scale of the
crisis by the confluence of these four old features with four new ones (Chapter 2). They
decline to rank the causes.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Old-versus-new decomposition of crisis causes ("four commonalities, four new dimensions")**:
  split the causes of a crisis into features shared with past crises and features that are new.
  - When to use: at the start of any crisis post-mortem or any "is this time different?" check.
  - How: (1) test the four commonalities listed below; (2) test the four new dimensions in
    Chapter 2; (3) attribute origin mostly to the commonalities and transmission, amplification
    and global scale mostly to the new dimensions.
  - Why it works / failure mode: the old features are the ones with a historical base rate, so
    they can be screened in advance. The authors do not rank the factors, "in part as many of
    them are very closely related". Do not read the list as a weighting.

- **The four commonalities with previous crises**:
  1. Asset price increases that turned out to be unsustainable.
  2. Credit booms that led to excessive debt burdens.
  3. Buildup of marginal loans and systemic risk.
  4. Failure of regulation and supervision to keep up with, and get ahead of, the crisis.

- **House-price boom template (the "Big 5" comparison, after Reinhart and Rogoff 2008)**:
  index real house prices to 100 at five years before the crisis date and compare the path with
  the average of the five major advanced-economy banking crises.
  - Big 5 = Spain 1977, Norway 1987, Finland 1991, Sweden 1991, Japan 1992.
  - US pattern in this episode: house prices rose by more than 30% in the 5 years before the
    crisis and peaked six quarters before its start. The crisis start date is assumed to be 2007.
  - When to use: to judge whether a current housing boom matches the pre-crisis signature.
  - How: (1) pick the candidate crisis date; (2) rebase the real house price index to 100 at
    quarter −20; (3) compare the cumulative rise and the lead of the peak with the Big 5 average.

- **Credit boom → crisis mechanism (Dell'Ariccia et al., 2008, 2009)**:
  - Only a minority of credit booms end in a financial crisis, but the probability of a crisis
    rises with a boom.
  - The larger the size and the longer the duration of the boom, the greater the likelihood of a
    crisis (Figure 48.4, reproduced below).
  - Two transmission mechanisms: (a) higher leverage of borrowers and lenders; (b) a decline in
    lending standards. In the US both operated. Metropolitan areas with higher loan origination
    growth had higher later delinquency (Figure 48.5).
  - Failure mode: aggregate credit can understate the boom. US aggregate credit growth was less
    pronounced than in earlier episodes because corporate credit grew slowly. The boom was
    concentrated in one segment, subprime mortgages, and in household debt.

- **Macro signature of a credit boom (Claessens et al., 2012; Mendoza and Terrones, 2008)**:
  - Buildup phase: real output, consumption and investment rise above trend.
  - Unwinding phase: they fall below trend.
  - Upswing companions: the current account tends to deteriorate, often with a surge in private
    capital inflows. House prices and the real exchange rate often rise.

- **Marginal-loan test (correlated default risk)**: ask whether the viability of the new loans
  depends on one macro variable continuing to behave favourably.
  - US (and partly UK) subprime: repayment depended on house prices continuing to rise so that
    mortgages could be refinanced.
  - Eastern Europe: large shares of domestic credit, including to households, were denominated
    in euros, Swiss francs and yen. Repayment depended on continued exchange rate stability.
  - Both cases produce high default correlation across loans and systemic exposure to a single
    macroeconomic shock.

## Key Concepts
- **Big 5**: the average house-price path of five major advanced-economy banking crises
  (Spain 1977, Norway 1987, Finland 1991, Sweden 1991, Japan 1992).
- **Credit boom**: a sustained episode of rapid credit growth that coincides with large cyclical
  swings in output, consumption and investment.
- **Marginal assets / marginal loans**: assets "whose viability relied on continued favorable
  macroeconomic conditions".
- **Subprime borrowers**: borrowers "with limited credit and employment histories".
- **Shadow banking system**: finance companies, merchant banks, investment banks and
  off-balance-sheet vehicles of commercial banks that operated, to varying degrees, outside
  banking regulation.
- **Originate-to-distribute model**: lending for onward sale. Regulators underestimated its
  conflicts of interest and information problems. The text also writes "originate-and-distribute".
- **Systemic versus individual-institution focus**: authorities watched the liquidity and
  solvency of single institutions and not the resilience of the system as a whole.
- **Debt service ratio**: debt service relative to disposable income. In the US it reached a
  historical high despite low interest rates.

## Mental Models
- Treat "house prices up fast AND leverage up fast" as the dangerous combination. Either one
  alone is a weaker signal. The authors write that the combination "turned out most dangerous".
- Think of a marginal loan book as one large bet on a single macro variable. Affordability
  gained from a lower foreign-currency rate is the price of that bet.
- Use the segment, not the aggregate, when sizing a boom. A flat aggregate can hide a boom in
  one segment.
- Read derivative-market growth as a by-product of benign conditions. Mortgage-backed securities
  and collateralized debt obligations were priced on continued house price rises. The corporate
  credit default swap market grew "on the back of favorable spreads and low volatility".

## Anti-patterns
- **Blaming monetary policy or global imbalances as top causes**: the authors set both aside
  (footnote 3). Countries differed too much in monetary policy and in external position, yet
  shared too much in crisis outcome, for these to rank among the top causes. Many surplus
  countries also had financial crises (footnote 6). The authors still list them as contributing
  factors analysed by others.
- **Liberalizing without regulatory reform**: past crises often followed expansions triggered by
  financial liberalization that came without regulatory and supervisory upgrades (Laeven and
  Valencia, 2008a). Badly sequenced reforms produced currency and maturity mismatches and large,
  concentrated credit risks.
- **Letting intermediation grow outside the regulatory perimeter**: the shadow banking system
  grew without adequate oversight and created systemic risk.
- **Supervising institution by institution**: this underestimates the probability and cost of
  systemic risk.
- **Having no procedure to resolve global institutions**: weak coordination among regulators and
  no clear resolution procedure hindered containment of the crisis.

## Worked Example
**Applying the commonality checklist to the United States, as the authors do.**

1. Asset price boom. Real house prices rose more than 30% in the five years before 2007 and
   peaked six quarters early. The path is "remarkably similar" to the Big 5 average.
2. Credit boom. Aggregate credit growth was modest because corporate credit grew slowly. The
   boom was in household debt after 2000, driven by mortgages, low interest rates and financial
   innovation. Debt service to disposable income hit a historical high.
3. Marginal loans. Much of the mortgage expansion went to subprime borrowers. Repayment relied
   on refinancing at higher house prices, which "maximized default correlations across loans".
   Large nonperforming loans after prices fell confirmed this ex post.
4. Regulation. The shadow banking system intermediated a growing share outside banking rules.
   Regulators underestimated the agency problems of originate-to-distribute.

Result: all four boxes ticked. The authors then run the same check across countries. The UK and
Iceland had fast house price growth fuelled by credit. The UK, Spain, Iceland and several
Eastern European countries had very fast credit growth, often funding real estate. Credit
growth tracked capital inflows most clearly in the countries that later had a crisis. Eastern
Europe carried the foreign-currency version of the marginal loan.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen for the pair: rapid house price growth together with rapid growth in leverage.
2. A credit boom raises crisis probability without making a crisis the central case. Size and
   duration both raise the odds.
3. Look for booms in segments (subprime, household, foreign-currency lending), not only in
   aggregate credit.
4. Ask what single macro variable a loan book depends on. If the answer is one variable for most
   loans, default correlation is high.
5. Capital inflows and domestic credit growth moved together in the countries that later had a
   crisis. Financial integration "helped these patterns along".
6. Check the regulatory perimeter. Fast-growing intermediation outside it is a systemic risk
   flag.
7. The authors do not treat low US rates or global imbalances as top causes. Use them as
   conditioning factors, not as the main driver.

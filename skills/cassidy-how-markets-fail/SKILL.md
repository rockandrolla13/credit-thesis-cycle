## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**Rational irrationality (Cassidy's central term; Intro, Ch 11).** Definition: "a situation in which the application of rational self-interest in the marketplace leads to an inferior and socially irrational outcome." Also "purposeful but self-defeating behavior". It is not stupidity, greed or crime. Use it when each actor can justify its own action and the total is destructive.
- Core mechanism: the **prisoner's dilemma**. Each player has a dominant strategy that leaves both worse off. With many players, cooperation is "virtually impossible".
- Test: ask what happens to one firm that holds back alone. If rivals take its business and its stock suffers, expect everyone to "get up and dance" (Chuck Prince, July 2007).
- Other forms in the book: Keynes's beauty contest (Ch 13), rational herding and information cascades (Ch 14), the tragedy of the commons (Ch 11), insurers refusing the sick (Ch 10), Minsky's credit cycle (Ch 16), simultaneous deleveraging and bank runs (Ch 22).
- Policy rule: where individual incentives produce a collectively bad result, only compulsory rules for all players change the outcome (Conclusion).

**The three illusions of utopian economics (Intro).** Use them as a checklist against "the market will handle it".
1. **Illusion of harmony**: free markets always generate good outcomes. Answered by spillovers, hidden information and strategic interaction (Ch 9-12, 19).
2. **Illusion of stability**: the economy is sturdy and self-correcting. Answered by the Sonnenschein-Mantel-Debreu result, Keynes and Minsky (Ch 5, 13, 16).
3. **Illusion of predictability**: market moves follow a distribution that can be foreseen. Answered by fat tails, uncertainty and the failure of value-at-risk (Ch 7, 13, 20).

**Utopian vs reality-based economics.** Part I traces the free-market doctrine: Smith's invisible hand (a negative-feedback claim, which Smith did not apply to banking), Hayek's price system as a "system of telecommunications", Walras-Pareto-Arrow-Debreu general equilibrium, Friedman's monetarism and natural rate, Fama's efficient market hypothesis, Lucas's rational expectations. Part II sets out what answers them. Treat the competitive model as the special case and market failure as the norm.

**Market failure toolkit (Part II).**
- **Spillovers** (Pigou, Ch 9): prices carry private cost, not social cost. Fix with a Pigovian tax, auctioned permits or regulation. The **Coase theorem** covers only cases with negligible transaction costs.
- **Bator's taxonomy** (Ch 10): uncertainty, imperfect information, monopoly and scale economies, public goods, externalities. Add network effects and lock-in (Arthur).
- **Hidden information** (Akerlof, Arrow, Stiglitz; Ch 12): adverse selection before a deal, moral hazard after it. Banks exist to manage both. Deposit insurance stops runs and creates moral hazard. With imperfect information, markets are in general never Pareto-efficient (Greenwald-Stiglitz).
- **Risk vs uncertainty** (Keynes, Ch 13): for many decisions "we simply do not know". Prices rest on convention. Following the crowd is rational under leverage and career risk.
- **Rational herding** (Ch 14): reputational herding (Scharfstein-Stein), noise trader risk and limits to arbitrage (Shleifer), information cascades (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, Welch). Sophisticated money rides bubbles.
- **Heuristics and disaster myopia** (Kahneman-Tversky; Guttentag-Herring; Ch 15): representativeness, availability, anchoring, overconfidence. The perceived chance of a systemic shock falls toward zero as calm lasts.

**Minsky's financial instability hypothesis (Ch 16).** "Stability is destabilizing." Over prolonged prosperity the economy moves from **hedge finance** (cash flow covers interest and principal) to **speculative finance** (interest only) to **Ponzi finance** (neither). The move is endogenous and driven by competition among lenders. It ends in a **Minsky moment**, forced asset sales and possible debt deflation. Cassidy calls it "a theory of rational irrationality". Securitization (originate-to-distribute) removes the lender's reason to check the borrower.

**Bubble template (Ch 17-18).** Kindleberger's stages: displacement, boom, euphoria, peak, bust. Cassidy's three features of every bubble: policymakers under the illusion of stability, financial innovation that makes speculating easier, and New Era thinking. His causal claim for 2002-2006: "the combination of cheap money and lax oversight". Neither alone did it. Check the policy rate against a rule (the funds rate ran at least 2 points below the Taylor rule), debt to GDP (350% total by 2006), and price to income (US median 4.6 against a long-run 3).

**Securitization failure chain (Ch 19-20).** No link in the mortgage chain screened the borrower. Each link was paid on volume. Diversification failed because the loans shared one risk factor, house prices. Subordination was under 10 percent. Ratings were issuer-paid and shopped. Value-at-risk treated recent calm as low risk and so encouraged leverage. Credit default swaps added unfunded leverage and network risk. Risk ended up concentrated in the big banks, not spread.

**Incentive map (Ch 21).** Build Cassidy's table: decision-maker, distorted incentive, result. For bank pay, use the **trader's option** and **CEO's option**: upside shared, downside capped, in a firm levered thirty to one. A rational chief executive "will be willing to literally bet the bank" (Bebchuk and Spamann).

**Amplification (Ch 22).** Shin's **loss spiral**: leverage target plus mark-to-market plus many firms acting at once. A 1 percent asset fall at 20 times leverage forces sale of $38 billion of $198 billion. The Millennium Bridge is the analogy: price is the signal that synchronizes everyone. Markets can also fail by freezing, when hidden information leaves no price at all.

**Crisis resolution and reform (Ch 23, Conclusion).** Separate liquidity from solvency. For solvency: acknowledge the scale, excise bad debts, recapitalize viable banks (Scandinavia, not 1990s Japan). Judge firms by interconnection. Then offset the moral hazard: leverage caps, more capital and liquidity, no hidden vehicles, retention of securitized loans (Cassidy suggests up to a fifth), central clearing of derivatives, compulsory pay deferral, and a financial stability mandate for the Fed.

# Chapter 10: A Taxonomy of Failure

## Core Idea
Francis Bator's 1958 classification gives a working list of where the invisible hand "will fumble". Cassidy adds modern cases to each class (network monopolies, knowledge as a public good) and argues that textbooks should teach market failure first, not as an addendum.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Bator's taxonomy of market failure** (Francis Bator, "The Anatomy of Market Failure", *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, August 1958). Bator is, as far as Cassidy can tell, the first economist to use the phrase "market failure", and he also coined "externalities".
  - **Background violations of Arrow-Debreu**: imperfect information, inertia, the infeasibility of costless lump-sum taxes, businessmen's desire for a "quiet life", uncertainty and inconsistent expectations, the vagaries of aggregate demand. Cassidy singles out **uncertainty** and **imperfect information** as fundamental, because the future is unknown.
  - Then three sources that would exist "even in a world of perfect foresight":
    1. **Monopoly or oligopoly power.** Atomized competition needs diminishing returns to scale. Production-line industries have falling unit costs, so big firms undercut small ones and a handful dominate. They then price above cost.
    2. **Public goods.** Firms cannot charge enough to make supply worthwhile.
    3. **Spillovers / externalities**, negative or positive.
  - How to use: test any market against all five headings (uncertainty, imperfect information, market power, public goods, spillovers) before assuming its prices are right.
- **Rival and excludable classification of goods**:
  - **Private good**: rival (one person's use prevents another's) and excludable (non-payers can be kept out). Example: an airline seat.
  - **Public good**: nonrival and nonexcludable. Prototype: national defense. Also irrigation systems, streetlights, clean air. Cassidy adds that education and health care "are essentially public goods".
  - Consequence: **free riding**. Offered a private air-defense policy, a person declines, knowing the neighbours' cover protects them too. Only taxation funds the good.
  - Rule: relying on the market for both kinds "will always lead to underprovision" of public goods.
- **Network externalities, tipping and lock-in** (W. Brian Arthur, mid-1980s). For some goods the value to a user depends on how many others use them. Each new user incidentally benefits all others. As users grow, the product becomes more attractive, the market "tips" to one product, and the maker "locks in". Chance events can let an inferior technology win.
  - When to use: for platforms, standards, consoles, social networks and search.
  - Policy implication noted: breaking up such a firm may not restore competition. Split in two, Microsoft's halves "would almost certainly have retained their strangleholds".
- **Knowledge as a public good** (Paul Romer, early 1980s). Technical knowledge is nonrival and largely nonexcludable. Originators often do not capture the benefit. Result, per Miles and Scott: "market economies with competitive firms will not produce enough R&D."
  - Remedies listed: stronger patents (a temporary monopoly) or government-funded research. The US combined both through the Bayh-Dole Act.
- **Micro and macro failures**: failures range from single markets to the whole economy, and the line is unclear. "The subprime crisis started out as a microfailure: it developed into a global recession." Slumps are macro failures with roots in "uncertainty and coordination problems at the micro level, especially in the financial sector".

## Key Concepts
- **Antitrust laws**: Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 (restraints of trade, attempts to monopolize). Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914 (price discrimination, exclusive dealing, predatory tactics).
- **Trusts**: turn-of-the-century combines such as Standard Oil and United States Steel.
- **Contestability argument** (William Baumol): the mere threat of competition curbs a monopolist.
- **Harberger's estimate**: economy-wide efficiency losses from monopoly of one-tenth of 1 percent of GDP.
- **"Private affluence, public squalor"**: the usual summary of Galbraith's *The Affluent Society* (1958).
- **Positive spillover**: James Meade's beekeeper and apple orchard. Bees pollinate the trees and the trees feed the bees. Neither owner counts the benefit to the other, so output is too low.
- **Solow's finding** (Robert Solow, 1950s): technical progress accounted for about 51 percent of annual US GDP growth between 1909 and 1949.

## Mental Models
- Reverse the textbook order. Treat the competitive model as the special case.
- Think of market prices under failure as "loaded with misinformation" (Bator, 1960).
- Use Galbraith's test for state functions: "as a purely technical matter, there is no alternative to public management."
- Look for the middle ground. Cassidy's examples are the jet engine and the Internet, both developed by government and then commercialized.

## Anti-patterns
- **Overstating corporate power**: Galbraith's *The New Industrial State* (1967) called the economy "in substantial part a planned economy". Globalization and corporate raiders then exposed big firms to competition.
- **Overstating its demise**: eight of the top ten firms on the 1967 Fortune list were still on the 2007 list, alone or inside larger groups.
- **Accommodating winners by default**: Judge Learned Hand's dictum that the successful competitor "must not be turned upon when he wins". The 2008 Justice Department guidelines made suits for predatory conduct much harder. The Obama administration abandoned them in May 2009.

## Worked Example
**Microsoft.** Windows and Office held about 90 percent of the PC market. Many experts considered the Apple Macintosh superior. This is Arthur's lock-in.
1. 1994: under Justice Department pressure Microsoft agrees to loosen licences and stop tying products to Windows.
2. 1997: the Department sues, saying Microsoft forced manufacturers to bundle Internet Explorer.
3. November 1999: Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson finds a monopoly. He later finds a Sherman Act violation and recommends a split into a Windows unit and an Office unit.
4. 2001: the appeals court overturns the rulings and the case settles.
Evidence of predation: a senior executive was quoted on plans to "cut off Netscape's air supply" by giving the browser away. Daniel Rubinfeld's summary: "there are very few economists I have talked to who would argue that leaving it to the market is always the best solution."

## Key Takeaways
1. Five headings cover most failures: uncertainty, imperfect information, market power, public goods, spillovers.
2. Network markets tip. Market share alone does not show product superiority.
3. Competitive markets underinvest in research. Cited result of the Bayh-Dole Act: more than 4,500 companies and more than 40,000 licensing deals from US universities.
4. Financial crises are micro failures that become macro failures.

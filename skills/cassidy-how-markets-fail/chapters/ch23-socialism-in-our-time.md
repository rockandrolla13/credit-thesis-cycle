# Chapter 23: Socialism in Our Time

## Core Idea
Between March 2008 and mid-2009 the US government moved from "let the market work" to explicit responsibility for the financial system. Cassidy's lesson: when private actors left to market incentives "pursue collectively self-defeating actions", "only the government could overcome the threat of rational irrationality". This is a case study of the rescue, with a general template for resolving a banking crisis.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Too interconnected to fail**: a Fed official on Bear Stearns: "The problem wasn't the size of Bear Stearns … people use the phrase 'too interconnected to fail.'" Bear reportedly had some hundred and fifty million trades with more than five thousand counterparties.
  - Transmission chain the Fed feared: Bear fails → money market mutual funds that lent to it in repo receive illiquid collateral, not cash → a run on that whole market → other investment banks cannot fund themselves.
  - How to use: assess a firm by its position in funding and derivatives networks, not by its size. Count counterparties, repo funding, and guarantees written.
- **Banking crisis vs liquidity crisis**: after credit markets had been locked for more than half a year, "it was silly to go on pretending that this was simply a liquidity crisis". Losses "had permanently wiped out hundreds of billions of dollars of bank capital". Lending fixes illiquidity. It does not fix "the mountain of bad debts".
- **The resolution template** (Cassidy, from history): "the only way for policymakers to get ahead of the problem is to acknowledge its scale, excise some of the bad debts, and recapitalize the banks deemed able to survive."
  - Positive cases, early 1990s Scandinavia: Finland merged more than forty savings banks into one state-owned bank. Norway nationalized its three biggest banks and wiped out shareholders. Sweden seized its two largest banks and moved toxic assets into a state-owned company.
  - Negative case: Japan in the 1990s did not do this and "endured a decade of economic stagnation".
  - Stefan Ingves's lesson: "you cannot rely on the private sector or markets alone to solve systemic banking problems."
- **"Break-the-glass" options** (Treasury memo, per Phillip Swagel): (1) buy toxic assets from banks, (2) leave the assets in the banks and insure them, (3) buy equity stakes in the banks, (4) launch a large publicly financed mortgage refinancing plan. All needed Congress.
- **Three elements of financial stabilization** (adopted in the US and elsewhere):
  1. "A pledge not to let systemically important institutions collapse."
  2. "A commitment to use taxpayers' money to socialize some of the losses."
  3. "An endorsement of unorthodox central bank policies aimed at kick-starting the credit markets."
- **Disguised subsidy** (Cassidy's political-economy point): open rescues such as TARP "create uproar". Loan guarantees, asset insurance and central bank financing proliferate because "while Wall Street investors understand the fiscal commitments they involve, most congressmen and editorial writers do not". Central bank credit to troubled firms is "another way of quietly providing subsidies to the financial sector".
- **Rescue expectation corrupts risk testing** (Andrew Haldane, Bank of England, February 2009): a banker told regulators that risk departments had no incentive to simulate real disasters. In one, they would lose bonuses and jobs anyway, and "the authorities would step in and rescue the bank". Haldane: "the unnamed banker was spot-on."

## Key Concepts
- **Section 13.3 of the Federal Reserve Act**: lets the Fed lend to any "individual, partnership, or corporation" in "unusual and exigent circumstances". Unused since the 1930s until March 14, 2008.
- **Conservatorship**: the status imposed on Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac on September 7, 2008. Cassidy calls it "backdoor nationalization".
- **Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP)**: up to $700 billion, about 5 percent of GDP, authorized October 3, 2008. Planned for asset purchases, used for bank preference shares.
- **Breaking the buck**: a money market fund's net asset value falling below a dollar a share.
- **Temporary Liquidity Guarantee Program**: FDIC guarantee of debt issued by big financial institutions, from November 2008.
- **"Finger in the dike" strategy**: the Fed's internal name for gradually expanding its lending while hoping the system would heal.
- **"Foaming the runways"**: Geithner's private phrase for opening the discount window to investment banks.
- **Moral hazard trade-off**: Bernanke on Bear: "Yes, it's moral hazard-inducing, but the right way to address this question is not to let institutions fail and have a financial meltdown."

## Mental Models
- Judge crisis policy by consistency. Cassidy convicts Paulson and Bernanke of "inconsistency and self-deception": they rescued Bear, then let Lehman, "a bigger and more interconnected firm", fail.
- A credible blanket pledge can itself restore order. After the bailout law, the pledge "alone was enough to restore a semblance of order". Cassidy thinks a categorical statement right after Bear "would have had a salutary effect".
- Use the counterfactual test. Ask what would have happened under strict laissez-faire. Cassidy calls the answer "patently absurd", and then asks "why anybody believed in utopian economics to begin with".
- "Like VAR-based risk modeling, the miraculous self-stabilizing power of the market fails just when it is most needed."
- Wall Street and Main Street "are locked in a symbiotic embrace".

## Anti-patterns
- **Punishing one firm to restore discipline mid-crisis**: the Lehman decision. Swagel says September 15 "felt like a good day at Treasury in that the market was allowed to work". The commitment "lasted less than forty-eight hours".
- **Buying assets at low prices**: it would force banks to recognize more losses. Paulson "sensibly shelved the original plan".
- **Insuring assets when ownership would be cheaper**: the Citigroup deal below.
- **Stress tests without teeth**: the 2009 tests "many independent experts regarded as a charade".

## Worked Example
**The Citigroup asset guarantee.** On a $306 billion pool, Citi bears the first $29 billion of losses. The Treasury and FDIC take the next $15 billion. The Fed takes 90 percent of the rest. The government received $7 billion of preferred stock, which Cassidy calls "artificially low". Glenn Hubbard, Hal Scott and Luigi Zingales put the potential taxpayer cost at about $60 billion. Citigroup's whole market capitalization at the end of 2008 was less than $40 billion. So "it may have been cheaper to nationalize the entire firm". The insurance form was chosen because it avoided both Congress and explicit public ownership.

**Lehman.** Barclays stayed interested until Sunday. Talks reportedly broke down when the Treasury required a public pledge to meet all of Lehman's commitments before Asian markets opened, which Barclays could not give without shareholder approval. Cassidy's judgement: the Fed "could certainly have kept it afloat for a few days". Bernanke's account is that Lehman lacked collateral and "there wasn't a decision to be made". Cassidy says that "has the sound and feel of a post hoc rationalization".

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify early whether the problem is liquidity or solvency. The cures differ.
2. Recognize losses, remove bad assets, recapitalize. Delay is the Japanese path.
3. A hub firm's failure spreads through funding markets within a day. Money market funds were the channel in September 2008.
4. Intervention prevented collapse but came too late to prevent the sharpest downturn since the 1930s.
5. Rescues create moral hazard and hidden subsidies. That is the case for the regulation discussed in the Conclusion.

# Chapter 9: The Prof and the Polar Bears

## Core Idea
Arthur Cecil Pigou showed that when private costs and social costs diverge, market prices carry only the private part and the outcome "is neither efficient nor socially desirable". The market "fails in a very specific and predictable sense". Cassidy uses global warming to teach the idea, then carries it into finance in later chapters.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Spillovers / externalities** (Pigou, *The Economics of Welfare*, 1920): costs or benefits that fall on "people not directly concerned". Pigou's key step is the distinction between the **private net product** and the **social** value of an activity. "Industrialists are interested, not in the social, but only in the private net product of their operations."
  - When to use: whenever an actor does not "face directly, neither via markets nor in other ways, the full consequences of the costs of their actions" (Stern Report wording).
  - How to diagnose: (1) list who bears costs and gains benefits outside the transaction, (2) ask whether the price includes them, (3) if not, expect too much of a harmful activity and too little of a beneficial one.
- **Pigou's two remedies**:
  - **"Extraordinary restraints"**: a tax where social value is below private value. Example: a factory pumping toxic waste into a river.
  - **"Extraordinary encouragements"**: a subsidy where social value exceeds private value. Examples: lighthouses, parks, streetlamps. These later became known as **public goods**. The market will not supply enough of them.
- **The Coase theorem** (Ronald Coase, "The Problem of Social Cost", 1960; named by George Stigler): if property rights are well specified and laws enforced, private bargaining between affected parties yields a Pareto-efficient outcome, whichever side the law favours.
  - Boundary condition, in Coase's own Nobel lecture: it applies only when **transaction costs** are negligible. Coase called it "a stepping stone" to analysing an economy with positive transaction costs. It does not imply that government action could not do better.
  - Decision rule from the chapter: few parties, measurable damage and clear law point to bargaining. Many parties, long-lived damage or unborn parties point to tax, regulation or public provision.
- **Pigovian tax**: a tax on products such as coal and gasoline, proportional to the greenhouse gases they emit, to bring the private cost of carbon closer to its social cost. Cited scale: $100 per ton of carbon emissions is about $0.20 a gallon of gasoline.
- **Cap and trade**: a cap on total emissions with tradable "emission rights". Cassidy's equivalence rule: if rights are auctioned to the highest bidders, cap and trade and a Pigovian tax "aren't very different". Both correct the cost divergence and both raise revenue.

## Key Concepts
- **Atomistic competition**: the Robinson Crusoe ideal in which one person's actions do not affect another's welfare. Cassidy: among social animals "spillovers are endemic".
- **Social cost**: the full cost to society of an activity, including costs on third parties.
- **Public good**: something whose benefits everyone enjoys without being charged. "A stable climate is a public good."
- **Transaction costs**: the costs of negotiation.
- **Pigou Club**: Greg Mankiw's informal group of supporters of carbon taxes, launched 2006. Members named: Gary Becker, Richard Posner, Alan Greenspan, George Shultz.
- **Stern Report** (Sir Nicholas Stern, October 2006): climate change is "the greatest and widest-ranging market failure ever seen". William Nordhaus accused Stern of exaggerating the costs.

## Mental Models
- Think of polluters as responding to price signals, not as villains. Coal power is cheaper than wind or solar, so "market incentives are usually what drive a firm to become a polluter".
- Use Pigou's method as an internal critique. He used the same marginal analysis as Walras and Pareto, so defenders of the invisible hand could not dismiss it: "his method was theirs!"
- State the problem as a wrong price. "Carbon is too cheap."

## Anti-patterns
- **Relying on the Coase theorem for large-scale spillovers**: the GE case below. For global warming "most of the interested parties haven't been born yet".
- **"We should expunge the concept of externality"**: a follower's position that Coase himself did not hold.
- **Giving away emission rights**: the House bill of June 2009 stripped the auction provision and gave away 85 percent of rights. Cassidy says it "didn't meet the necessary economic standards".
- **The syllogism "taxes are bad, Pigovian taxes are taxes, so Pigovian taxes are bad"**: Mankiw's summary of the reasoning to avoid.

## Worked Example
**Coase's two farmers.** A cattle farmer's livestock stray onto a neighbouring arable farm and destroy crops. Both can put a dollar figure on the damage and trespass law is clear.
- If the law makes the cattle farmer liable: the cattle farmer fences the land, cuts the herd, or pays an agreed sum.
- If the law does not: the arable farmer, if the crops are worth enough, pays for the neighbour's fence.
- Either way the pattern of production is Pareto-efficient. Only who pays differs.

**Why it does not scale: General Electric and the Hudson.** For three decades after World War II two GE factories dumped more than a million pounds of PCBs into the Hudson, polluting a two-hundred-mile stretch. New York State stopped fishing in the worst area in 1976. The federal government banned PCBs in 1977. The EPA ordered dredging in 2002. Work started in May 2009. Property rights "were hardly the issue". Millions of people live in the valley and PCBs linger for decades, so calculating individual costs and one-to-one negotiation were impractical.

**Pigou's railway.** Fares reflect users' private benefits and the company's private costs. Sparks from engines do uncompensated damage to surrounding woods. That cost is outside the company's calculation, but "all such effects must be included" in the social value.

## Key Takeaways
1. A spillover is a pricing error with a predictable sign.
2. Check transaction costs before trusting private bargaining.
3. Taxes, auctions of rights, public provision and regulation are all Pigovian tools.
4. Carbon figures in the text: about 280 parts per million 250 years ago, about 430 today, about 550 by 2050 on current trends.
5. Spillovers are one of many market failures. The chapter ends by listing monopoly power, health insurers refusing the sick, and speculative bubbles in financial markets.

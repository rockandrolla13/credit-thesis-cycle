# Chapter 12: Hidden Information and the Market for Lemons

## Core Idea
When one side of a trade knows more than the other, prices stop carrying the information Hayek said they carry. Two problems follow: **adverse selection** before the deal and **moral hazard** after it. Both are endemic in insurance and finance. Banks exist to manage them, and in doing so create credit crunches, runs, and the moral hazard of deposit insurance.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The market for lemons / adverse selection** (George Akerlof, "The Market for Lemons: Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism", *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 1970, after three rejections). Cassidy prefers the name **hidden information**.
  - Mechanism: (1) sellers know quality and buyers do not, (2) buyers therefore will not pay the price good sellers ask, (3) good sellers cannot prove quality and withdraw, (4) average quality falls again. "The bad cars tend to drive out the good." In the limit "good cars may not be traded at all".
  - Either side can hold the information. In used cars the seller knows more. In insurance and banking the buyer does.
  - When to use: any market "where the quality of goods would be difficult to see by anything other than casual inspection". Akerlof thought that "include[s] most markets".
- **Mitigating devices**: warranties, money-back guarantees and brand names. Cassidy's boundary: where brands and reputations matter (consumer goods, personal services), leave the market alone. In pharmaceuticals, finance and health care the problem is acute.
- **Market signaling** (Michael Spence, *Market Signaling*, 1974): a signal works only if it is hard to obtain. Higher degrees signal productivity in a way less able people find hard to match.
- **Moral hazard** (term following Kenneth Arrow, *American Economic Review*, 1963): insurance changes the behaviour of the insured, because the insurer "can't fully monitor" it.
  - Test: can the policyholder affect the probability of a payout? No for hurricanes and earthquakes, so it is minor there. Yes for driving, fire precautions and medical use.
  - Mitigation: deductibles, co-payments, precertification, limits on choice, claim investigation. Cassidy notes that competition makes holding the line hard, since each insurer is tempted to offer more to win customers.
- **Why banks exist (financial intermediation)**. A direct lender faces both problems: it cannot tell good projects from bad (lemons), and it cannot stop the borrower switching to a riskier venture after the loan (moral hazard). Banks mitigate them through long-term relationships, credit history checks, collateral, higher rates and covenants on unsecured loans, and monitoring of accounts.
- **Credit crunch from adverse selection**. In a downturn many seek credit. Banks cannot tell good from bad at once. "Those who have been hardest hit by the downturn will be those most eager to take out more credit." Banks cut lending. Firms are starved of credit, the downturn deepens, and adverse selection worsens. "In extreme circumstances, the entire lending market might freeze up."
- **Bank runs and deposit insurance**. Banks do not publish their loans, so a depositor cannot know whether the bank is sound and has "every incentive to withdraw his savings at the first sign of trouble". Deposit insurance stops runs but creates moral hazard: banks offer high rates and fund them with high-risk loans, with principal protected.
- **Greenwald-Stiglitz result** (Joseph Stiglitz with Bruce Greenwald): economies with imperfect information are, in general, never Pareto-efficient. Stiglitz: Arrow-Debreu "had identified the single set of assumptions under which markets were (Pareto) efficient. There had to be perfect information."
  - Cassidy's gloss: hidden information "acts like one of Pigou's spillovers" and makes the price system "issue the wrong price signals".

## Key Concepts
- **Lemon**: a low-quality good whose defects the seller knows and the buyer cannot see.
- **Stigma of unemployment**: being out of work signals a possible lemon. The odds of a new job fall steadily as the spell lengthens.
- **Risk pool unravelling**: as premiums rise, healthier members leave, costs per member rise, premiums rise again.
- **Single-payer argument** (Arrow, 2005): government "is better than the private sector at keeping costs down for insurance purposes". He says this is true in no other industry.
- **Covenants**: loan terms restricting a borrower's ability to enter new businesses or take other risks.
- **Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982**: deregulated thrifts.
- **Resolution Trust Corporation**: set up by Congress in 1989 to take over failed thrifts.

## Mental Models
- Think of information as "more like air": its adequate provision is "a precondition for other things to happen" (Cassidy on Stiglitz). It is not fully revealed by prices and is not just another input.
- A party's eagerness to trade is itself information. A team that releases a player and a borrower rushing for credit both signal lower quality.
- Use Stiglitz's contrast. Older market failures were "easily identified and limited in scope". Information failures "are pervasive".
- L. William Seidman's rule: "As Adam Smith recognized, banking is different."

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming long unemployment is voluntary "search"**: Cassidy says most unemployment is strictly involuntary.
- **Fully private health insurance**: universal coverage removes the lemons problem by stopping the healthy leaving the pool.
- **Deregulating insured institutions**: the S&L case below.
- **Assuming imperfect markets mean government does better everywhere**: Cassidy gives Friedman's objection its due and asks that interventions be "examined critically and monitored carefully".

## Worked Example
**Unravelling of age-rated insurance (assume no Medicare).** Insurers offer plans with higher rates for people over fifty-five.
1. Premiums rise with age.
2. Healthier members drop out.
3. The insurer is left with the frail and sick, so revenue falls relative to cost.
4. The insurer raises prices again, and more healthy people leave.
5. Only lemons remain and the insurer loses heavily. Akerlof: every individual might be willing to pay his expected cost, "yet no insurance company can afford to sell him a policy".

**The savings-and-loan rehearsal.** In the 1970s thrifts lost depositors to money market accounts. The 1982 act let them pay higher rates and lend in commercial real estate and junk bonds. The insured deposit limit rose from $40,000 to $100,000. The result was reckless lending and fraud tied to a Sunbelt real estate boom and bust. Many thrifts were insolvent by the mid-1980s and lobbied to stay open. More than seven hundred closed. No depositor lost money. The cost to taxpayers was about $125 billion. Cassidy's three shared causes with subprime: "a misguided faith in the free market, deregulation that was heavily influenced by industry lobbyists, and an unsustainable real estate boom."

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask who knows more, before the deal (selection) and after it (hazard).
2. Evidence cited (Kenneth Lehn, baseball): players who re-signed averaged fewer than ten days a season on the disabled list. Free agents averaged more than seventeen.
3. Banking history figures: more than five thousand US banks failed between 1929 and 1932. The deposit insurance limit was recently raised to $250,000.
4. Health care figures: spending topped $2 trillion in 2006, about a sixth of GDP. The US spends roughly twice as much per person as Canada, the United Kingdom and France, with lower life expectancy.
5. Stiglitz: there is "no general theorem" that markets allocate resources most efficiently.

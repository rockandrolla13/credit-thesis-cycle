# Chapter 4: The Perfect Markets of Lausanne

## Core Idea
General equilibrium theory shows that, under stated conditions, a market-clearing set of prices exists and the result is Pareto-efficient. Cassidy's point is how little this proves: Pareto efficiency is "a minimum requirement", and the theorem only says markets "don't leave win-win deals on the table".

## Frameworks Introduced
- **General equilibrium** (Leon Walras, *Elements of Pure Economics*, 1874; Walras coined the term). For each industry write one demand and one supply equation. Ask whether one set of prices solves all of them together.
  - Conditions Cassidy states for existence: each industry contains many competing suppliers, and firms cannot lower unit costs merely by raising output.
  - Results claimed at equilibrium prices: supply equals demand in every industry, no resources are idle, inputs go to their most productive uses, and nobody can be made better off without making somebody worse off.
  - Caveat in the text: Walras counted equations and unknowns and claimed a unique solution. His "proof" "wasn't as convincing as he thought it was".
  - Lasting insight: "everything depends on everything else". All prices are determined at once, so a big disruption in one industry spills into others.
- **Theorem of Maximum Utility / the marginal condition** (Walras; derived independently by Stanley Jevons and Karl Menger). A consumer buys each good up to the point where the last franc spent yields equal satisfaction:
  - U(Bread)/P(Bread) = U(Wine)/P(Wine), rearranged as U(Wine)/U(Bread) = P(Wine)/P(Bread).
  - U is the satisfaction from the last unit consumed. P is the price. Cassidy calls it "probably the most important equation in the entire subject".
- **Pareto improvement and Pareto efficiency** (Vilfredo Pareto, *Manual of Political Economy*, 1906).
  - Rule: outcome B is preferable to A if nobody is worse off in B and at least one person is better off. An outcome with all such moves exhausted is Pareto-efficient.
  - When to use: as a diagnostic. "If an economic outcome isn't Pareto-efficient, something is preventing mutually beneficial transactions from taking place."
  - Limits: Pareto-efficient outcomes are rarely unique. The criterion cannot weigh gains against losses, so it cannot rule out extreme inequality. Amartya Sen: an economy "can be Pareto optimal and still be perfectly disgusting."
- **First fundamental theorem of welfare economics**: every free market outcome is Pareto-efficient. Cassidy: it holds "under certain circumstances" and to treat markets as "inviolate" on this basis "is stretching things".
- **Marginal cost pricing rule** (Abba Lerner, *Review of Economic Studies*, 1934): for Pareto efficiency, firms must set prices to cover marginal costs. Prices above marginal cost with excess profits indicate monopoly power, which is not consistent with Pareto efficiency.
- **Lange's correspondence** (Oskar Lange, "On the Economic Theory of Socialism", 1936; *Econometrica*, 1942): the mathematical conditions for Pareto efficiency closely match those for competitive equilibrium. Wages proportional to worker productivity, prices covering marginal cost, and consumers satisfying the marginal condition.
  - Meaning: an idealized free market reaches what a benevolent planner with full information would impose.

## Key Concepts
- **Utility**: the pleasure a good gives, assumed to diminish as more is consumed.
- **Hedonistic calculus** (Jeremy Bentham): maximize the sum of individual happiness. It fails on measurement and on comparison across people. Cassidy calls the measurement problem another version of Hayek's division-of-knowledge problem.
- **Lausanne School**: Walras and Pareto.
- **Market socialism**: Lerner and Lange's proposal. Planning boards set input prices by trial and error, raising them in shortages and cutting them in gluts.
- **Barone's result** (Enrico Barone, 1908): a collectivist ministry should direct state firms to act as if capitalist, minimizing costs and relating prices to costs.

## Mental Models
- Use Pareto efficiency as a floor, never as a target. It checks for wasted mutual gains and says nothing on fairness.
- Think of the economy as "an organic whole". Shocks propagate through linked markets.
- Treat textbook claims that general equilibrium "illustrates the invisible hand" (Cassidy quotes Pindyck and Rubinfeld) as resting on the conditions, which the next chapters test.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading the first welfare theorem as proof that markets are superior to any other system**: Cassidy says the theory shows only a minimum level of efficiency under certain circumstances.
- **Ignoring distribution**: the Bill Gates example. If one person owned 99 percent of world wealth, the allocation could still be Pareto-efficient.

## Worked Example
**Apples and oranges at a farmers' market.** I have six apples. You have six oranges. You like apples twice as much as oranges. I like them equally. Two barter deals are Pareto improvements but each leaves one side no better off, so either might be refused. Now a market opens with apples at $1.50 and oranges at $1.00.
1. You sell six oranges for $6.00 and buy four apples. You value each apple at $2.00, so you hold $8.00 of value.
2. I sell four apples for $6.00 and buy six oranges. I hold two apples and six oranges, which I value at $8.00.
3. Each of us started with fruit valued at $6.00. Each gains the equivalent of $2.00.
The lesson: trading at market prices that differ from private values lets both sides gain, where bilateral bargaining might stall.

**Wage example.** A: you earn $500 a week, I earn $1,000. B: you earn $750, I earn $1,000. B is a Pareto improvement. C: you earn $800 and I earn $975. Moving from B to C is not.

## Key Takeaways
1. Existence of equilibrium prices needs competition and no falling unit costs. Check both before invoking the result.
2. Efficiency here means only "no unexploited win-win trades".
3. Left-leaning economists (Barone, Lerner, Lange) were the first to formalize why competitive prices are efficient.
4. Walras showed interdependence. That same interdependence is how crises spread.

# Chapter 8: The Triumph of Utopian Economics

## Core Idea
Robert Lucas extended the efficient market approach to the whole economy. Rational expectations, placed inside a unique and stable Arrow-Debreu equilibrium, ruled out slumps, bubbles and credit crunches by assumption. The theory failed its empirical tests but still shaped the models central banks used, which "have virtually nothing to say about what policymakers should do to maintain financial stability".

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rational expectations hypothesis** (introduced by John Muth, Carnegie Mellon, 1961; applied by Robert Lucas in the early 1970s): everyone holds the same correct mathematical model of the economy and uses it to form expectations of wages, prices and other variables.
  - Replaced **adaptive expectations**: backward-looking extrapolation (if inflation is 5 percent this year, expect 5 percent next year). Those models did badly when inflation spiked in the early 1970s.
  - Cassidy's key qualification: "the assumption of rational expectations, by itself, has few definitive policy implications: everything depends on the economic framework in which it is embedded." With other auxiliary assumptions, rational-expectations models let government stabilize the economy.
- **Lucas's embedding framework**: assume all Arrow-Debreu conditions hold and that equilibrium is unique and stable. Supply equals demand everywhere, so unemployment is always at or near Friedman's natural rate. After a shock the economy "jumps right back" next period.
  - Only imperfection allowed: for short periods workers cannot tell a rise in their own wage from a rise in the general price level.
- **Policy ineffectiveness proposition** (phrase coined by Thomas Sargent and Neil Wallace): anticipated changes in monetary policy have no effect on output or employment. Money is "neutral". Only unanticipated changes matter.
  - Tax version (Robert Barro): a tax cut leads consumers to expect higher taxes later, so they save the extra and negate the stimulus.
  - Combined verdict of the school: predictable government is powerless, and surprising government is destabilizing.
- **Real business cycle models** (Edward C. Prescott and others; part of **new classical economics**): keep optimization and rational expectations. Booms and slumps come from random fluctuations in productivity growth.
  - Failure noted: when calibrated, the models could mimic actual data only "by invoking implausibly large productivity shocks".
- **New Keynesian models**: "self-correcting equilibrium models, built on the foundations of efficient financial markets and rational expectations", with one or two added **rigidities** that let unemployment deviate from its natural rate for a time. Michael Woodford, 1999: rational expectations "is now entirely orthodox".
- **Friedman's methodological rule** (1953 essay): judge a theory by whether its predictions are accurate enough, not by whether its assumptions are "descriptively realistic". Cassidy applies the rule against the theory.
- **Paradox of thrift** (Keynes, *The General Theory*): saving makes sense for one person. If everyone saves more at once, spending falls, firms cut output and jobs, incomes fall, and total saving may fall too.
  - When to use: as the model case of individual logic failing in aggregate. It is an early form of rational irrationality.

## Key Concepts
- **Microfoundations project**: Paul Samuelson's *Foundations of Economic Analysis* (1947) treated decision-makers as "rational automatons" maximizing functions under constraints. Lucas wanted macroeconomics built the same way.
- **Neutral money**: money affects only financial variables such as inflation, not real ones.
- **Unemployment as choice**: in a Lucasian economy the jobless of the Great Depression "must have been unwilling to work at the prevailing wages".
- **Capitalism without capitalists**: Cassidy's description. Firms are shells. No innovation, no monopolists, no oligopolists, and financial markets exist only abstractly.
- **Dynamic optimization**: the methods first-year doctoral students still learn first.
- **Lender-of-last-resort tradition**: Walter Bagehot, *Lombard Street* (1873), gives justifications for cutting rates and adding money in a crisis.

## Mental Models
- Ask what a macro model rules out by assumption. Lucas's had "no place for stupidity, ignorance, or herd behavior", and none for "stock market bubbles, banking crises, or lending crunches".
- Check whether a model contains recognizable financial markets. If not, do not use it for financial stability questions.
- Read long calm as a selling condition for utopian economics. After the 1981-82 recession the US went twenty-five years without a prolonged downturn, and problems such as "the potential for financial instability" take a long time to emerge.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Economic theory is mathematical analysis. Everything else is just pictures and talk"** (Lucas): Cassidy says the problem is not mathematics "but how it is used (and abused)".
- **Declaring victory**: Lucas, January 2003, said the "central problem of depression-prevention has been solved, for all practical purposes".
- **Policy by instinct**: three leading New Keynesians admitted that support for the Fed's rate cut after the October 1987 crash "was based largely on instinct", since "there is virtually no formal theoretical work that rationalizes this kind of intervention".
- **Defending a failed model as too subtle for statistics**: Cassidy calls this "an argument that smacked of desperation".

## Worked Example
**The Volcker test, 1979 to 1982.** Fed chairman Paul Volcker announced that he would wring out inflation by restricting money growth. The policy was anticipated.
- Rational expectations prediction: workers and firms foresee lower inflation, cut wage demands, and the economy jumps to a low-inflation equilibrium with little change in output or unemployment.
- Keynesian prediction: a drastic cut in money supply causes a deep recession.
- Outcome: a deep recession. Short-term interest rates rose from 10 percent to 19 percent. Unemployment rose to 9.5 percent, then its highest since the Great Depression.
Lucas later conceded to Cassidy: "Monetary shocks just aren't that important: that's the view I've been driven to."

## Key Takeaways
1. Evidence shows anticipated monetary policy does affect unemployment and output. Unanticipated changes matter less.
2. The stark conclusions came from the equilibrium framework, not from rationality itself.
3. Central bank models before the crisis maintained all three illusions: harmony, stability and predictability.
4. Chicago economics triumphed because its arrival coincided with 1970s turmoil, then a long expansion protected it.

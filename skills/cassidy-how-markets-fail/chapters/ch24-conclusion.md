# Conclusion

## Core Idea
The crisis came from "more than twenty years of free market idolatry". The modern invisible hand "explains too much with too little". Cassidy offers reality-based economics as the replacement: it accepts that markets are useful and limited, and it uses **rational irrationality** as the guide for where government must change private incentives. This chapter is general method: a policy programme.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The utopian equation and what it leaves out** (Cassidy): "self-interest plus competition equals nirvana."
  - Omitted institutions: multinational corporations, derivatives markets, universal banks, mutual funds.
  - Omitted features of reality: "information asymmetries, uncertainty, copycat behavior, network effects, and disaster myopia".
  - How to use: check any market-will-fix-it argument against these two lists.
- **Reality-based economics as a philosophy**: it "acknowledges the usefulness of markets but also their limitations", and "recognizes the existence of Hayek's telecommunications system but also its tendency to break down". It gives market failure "a central position, recognizing the roles that human interdependence and rational irrationality play in creating it".
- **The autonomy-coordination trade-off**: "Too much supervision, and healthy innovation will be stifled. Too little oversight, and the economy can veer off in the direction of boom and bust", and it generates negative externalities.
  - Decision rule from rational irrationality: "In many cases of government action, the problem is one of remedying private incentive structures that give rise to socially damaging activities." Where each actor has a dominant strategy that harms the group, "the only way to coordinate on the socially desirable outcome is to enforce compliance, and the only body that can do that is the government".
- **Cassidy's reform programme** (see Reference Table). Its ordering principle: "The more systemic risk an institution poses, the more tightly it should be controlled."
- **Function over label**: "the labels attached to financial institutions are often misleading." AIG, Fannie Mae and GE Capital were effectively "big financial intermediaries: that is, banks". Any business that "deals in money and is large and interconnected enough that its failure would seriously endanger many other financial firms" should meet the same capital, liquidity and reporting rules as big banks.
- **Regulatory migration**: "If there is any wiggle room, excessive risk-taking and other damaging behavior will simply migrate to the unregulated sector."
- **Robust over advanced** (Viral Acharya and Matthew Richardson, NYU): "The goal is not to have the most advanced financial system, but a financial system that is reasonably advanced but robust." Their comparison is with aircraft whose designs have proved reliable. Cassidy accepts that pre-approval of products would slow innovation: "that is exactly the point."
- **Pay reform as a prisoner's dilemma**: restraint suits Wall Street as a whole, but "each individual firm has an incentive to pay star traders what they want when they want it". When business picks up, some banks will hire traders away from firms with pay limits. So voluntary reform "won't survive the next market upturn".
- **Four views of history** applied to the crisis: Macaulay (individuals), Marx (impersonal forces), Keynes (ideas), and Rudge of *The History Boys* (one thing after another). Cassidy grants each something and concludes that "the application of misguided ideas was largely responsible".

## Key Concepts
- **Greenspan doctrine** (as used here): the Fed's refusal to act against bubbles. Cassidy wants "a formal renunciation".
- **"Taking away the punch bowl just when the party gets going"**: William McChesney Martin's definition of the Fed's traditional role.
- **Dual mandate**: "maximum sustainable employment and price stability". Stephen Roach proposed adding financial stability as a third mandate.
- **Utility vs casino**: deposits, check clearing and payments on one side, investment banking and proprietary trading on the other. The 1930s reforms separated them. The 2009 proposals did not.
- **Central clearinghouse**: a body that imposes leverage limits and collateral on derivatives trades and so removes much counterparty risk.
- **Claw-back**: a scheme letting a firm rescind bonuses if investments later sour.
- **Consumer Financial Protection Agency**: proposed regulator of consumer financial products, from an idea of Elizabeth Warren.
- **Crony capitalism writ large**: a few too-big-to-fail firms free to take risk with a taxpayer backstop.

## Mental Models
- "The idealized free market is a fiction, an invention: it has never existed, and it never will exist." What exists is "a hybrid of private and public enterprise", which "has passed … the market test".
- Reject the binary of "liberty and socialism".
- Securitization is sound in principle. Conforming prime loans worked "pretty well for more than twenty-five years". It failed when extended to subprime, where it ended up "disguising and concentrating" risk.
- Expect forgetting. "As memories of September 2008 fade, revisionism and disaster myopia will become increasingly common."
- Hoarding cash in a network with unknown losses is "a privately rational action", and credit dries up.

## Anti-patterns
- **Loopholes for "customized" derivatives**: engineers will exploit them quickly.
- **A 5 percent retention rule**: Cassidy suggests "perhaps as much as a fifth".
- **Leaving consumer protection out of inter-firm securities.**
- **Teaching "the efficiency properties of market outcomes" unchanged**: Cassidy's criticism of Greg Mankiw's May 2009 column. "In the world of utopian economics, the latest crisis of capitalism is always a blip."
- **Economists who admit single market failures privately** but never add them into an overall critique.
- **Blaming Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac or the "Ownership Society" for everything**: "hard to take seriously".

## Reference Table
Cassidy's proposals, and his verdict on the 2009 Obama administration plan where he gives one:

| Area | Cassidy's proposal | Verdict on the 2009 plan |
|---|---|---|
| Securitization | Issuers keep some of the securities, "perhaps as much as a fifth" | Only 5 percent |
| Mortgage origination | Federal regulation of brokers and lenders. Tougher predatory lending laws with "draconian penalties". Outlaw stated-income loans. Enforce fraud laws on applicants | Not assessed |
| Consumer products | A Consumer Financial Protection Agency | "Seems sensible". Remit excludes complex securities traded between firms |
| Bank balance sheets | Maximum leverage ratios. "More than adequate" liquidity and capital. No hiding liabilities in SIVs and shell companies | Capital rules promised, key terms undefined |
| Nonbanks | Same capital, liquidity and reporting rules for any large, interconnected money business | Unclear how far rules reach hedge funds, private equity and finance arms |
| Structure | Consider splitting utility functions from casino functions | "No return to the Glass-Steagall Act" |
| Derivatives | Regulate anything that threatens system stability. Central clearing with leverage limits and collateral | Covers "standardized" derivatives only |

| Monetary policy | Renounce the Greenspan doctrine. Add financial stability to the Fed's mandate | Not publicly repudiated |
| Economics teaching | Put market failure at the centre | Profession largely unchanged |

Overall verdict: the plan "doesn't fully deal with the problem of rational irrationality: it amounts to tinkering".

## Worked Example
**Why counterparty discipline fails in a network.** Greenspan held that self-interest would make counterparties police one another. Cassidy's reply uses hidden information. A firm is exposed to its counterparties, "their counterparties' counterparties, their counterparties' counterparties' counterparties, and so on". When a problem appears, "nobody knows where the losses are located, or where they might ultimately end up". Each firm then hoards cash, which is privately rational, and credit dries up for all. The text gives the credit default swap industry here as $60 trillion. AIG's financial-products division, in Bernanke's words "a hedge fund, basically, that was attached to a large and stable insurance company", ended up taking $180 billion in cash and credit lines.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use rational irrationality to decide where rules are needed: wherever individual incentives produce a collectively bad result.
2. Regulate by systemic function. Close the gaps risk will move into.
3. Any firm under the government safety net gives up the right "to behave like hedge funds".
4. Pay and leverage rules must be compulsory and universal, or competition will undo them.
5. A central bank should lean against bubbles and hold a financial stability mandate.

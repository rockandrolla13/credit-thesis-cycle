# Chapter 17: Greenspan Shrugs

## Core Idea
The housing and credit bubble began with a policy "displacement": the Fed's cut of the funds rate to 1 percent. Cassidy's thesis for Part III is that neither cheap money nor deregulation alone did the damage. "It was the twinning of the two policies that was to prove so disastrous." This chapter is a historical case study of US policy from 1987 to 2006.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Kindleberger's five stages of a bubble** (Charles P. Kindleberger, citing Minsky's influence): **displacement, boom, euphoria, peak, bust**.
  - **Displacement** is what starts the process: the start or end of a war, a transformative technology, or a change in economic policy. It must be important enough "to alter how investors and other financial players conceive of the future".
  - How to use: name the displacement first, then place the market in one of the five stages.
- **Cheap money plus lax oversight** (Cassidy): "In a modern economy with a large financial sector, the combination of cheap money and lax oversight, if maintained for years on end, is sure to lead to trouble."
  - Two-variable test: (1) is the policy rate below a rule-based benchmark, and for how long? (2) Is supervision of leverage and new products being loosened?
- **The Taylor rule benchmark** (John B. Taylor, Stanford): "a simple equation that shows what interest rate the Fed should set depending on the level of inflation and unemployment". From 1983 to 2002 it tracks the funds rate closely. Through much of 2003, 2004 and 2005 the rate was "at least 2 percent lower" than the rule implied. The text does not give the equation.
- **Leverage works both ways** (worked below): the same gearing that raises returns removes the capital cushion.
- **Debt-to-GDP as a crisis signal** (Bank for International Settlements discussion paper, 2002): a big rise in debt outstanding relative to GDP "often presaged a financial crisis". The BIS repeated the warning regularly through the boom.
- **Crony capitalism, not free markets** (Cassidy): a central bank that can print money, deposit insurance, and a Congress that can authorize bailouts form "an extensive safety net". Easy money plus deregulation on top of that net means "the gains of financial innovation and speculation are privatized" and "much of the losses are socialized".
  - Mechanism: once the Fed stops preventing excessive risk-taking, "rational irrationality will eventually ensure that the system moves toward what Minsky referred to as Ponzi finance".
- **"Mitigate the fallout" doctrine** (Greenspan, Senate testimony, July 1999): bubbles cannot be told apart from justified price rises, and raising rates to burst one could cause the recession it aims to prevent. The Fed should instead "mitigate the fallout when it occurs".
  - Cassidy's objection: bubbles "feed on themselves" and their consequences are severe. The clean-up rate cuts then started the next bubble.
- **Private (counterparty) regulation** (Greenspan, 1994): "Risks in financial markets, including derivatives markets, are being regulated by private parties."
  - Refutation in the text: Long-Term Capital Management. "In a world of hidden information, there is often no way for financial firms to know what risks their counterparties have taken on."

## Key Concepts
- **Federal funds rate**: the rate at which banks lend to one another. Other rates tend to follow it.
- **Negative real rates**: with inflation near 2 percent and the funds rate at 1 percent, the real cost of short-term borrowing was "well below zero" for about two years.
- **Great Moderation**: the long period of relative calm from the 1980s. Bernanke credited "improvements in monetary policy" in February 2004.
- **Glass-Steagall Act**: Depression-era law keeping depository institutions out of investment banking. Mostly repealed by the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, November 1999.
- **Derivatives**: securities whose value is derived from the price of something else.
- **Credit default swaps**: contracts that protect investors against a bond defaulting.
- **Lender of last resort**: the Fed's founding purpose (1913), after the Bankers' Panic of 1907 and the failure of the Knickerbocker Trust Company.

## Mental Models
- Think of the Fed as "an institution that was designed to save financial capitalism from itself". Cassidy calls it "an absurdity" for its chairman to claim markets are innately stable.
- Look at the whole credit stock, not only mortgages. Mortgage growth "was just part of a much larger credit boom", and the financial sector borrowed most.
- Diagnose officials with the Part II tools. Cassidy says Greenspan "had fallen victim to disaster myopia and the illusion of stability".
- Read Greenspan's eighteen and a half years as "a classic confirmation of Minsky's financial instability hypothesis".

## Anti-patterns
- **Declaring success after one clean-up**: Greenspan, January 3, 2004, said the strategy of addressing "the bubble's consequences rather than the bubble itself has been successful".
- **Blocking derivatives oversight**: in 1998-1999 Brooksley Born of the CFTC tried to bring credit default swaps under regulation, with minimal capital requirements and disclosure. Greenspan and the Treasury asked Congress in November 1999 to bar it, and Congress did so the next year.
- **Calling complex instruments a source of resilience**: Greenspan, January 2004, said they had built "a far more flexible, efficient, and hence resilient financial system".

## Worked Example
**Leverage in both directions.**
1. A bank has $50 million of capital, borrows $50 million and lends $100 million. A recession makes 10 percent of clients default. The loss is $10 million, a fifth of its capital.
2. The same bank borrows $450 million and lends $500 million. A 10 percent default rate is a $50 million loss. Its entire capital is gone.
3. The incentive to gear up: in much of 2003 and 2004 a bank could borrow $100 million for twelve months for less than $2 million, then lend it at "considerably higher rates".

**Long-Term Capital Management, 1998.** When the fund opened its books, lenders found leverage close to thirty to one and derivatives exposures of about $1.4 trillion. Greenspan called it "a major failure of counterparty surveillance", and still opposed derivatives regulation.

**Bubble to bubble.** The NASDAQ bust from March 2000 destroyed more than $2 trillion of paper wealth, and about $7 trillion by the end of 2001. The Fed cut rates. The 2001 recession lasted eight months. Rates stayed low, and GDP grew at a 7.5 percent annualized rate in the third quarter of 2003. Cassidy: the Fed "was in the process of substituting one bubble for another".

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify the displacement. Here it was the rate cut from 6.5 percent to 1 percent.
2. Compare the policy rate with a rule. A gap of two points for three years is the tell Cassidy cites.
3. Track financial sector debt to GDP. It is where the largest borrowing was.
4. An implicit safety net plus deregulation is not a free market.
5. Critics from inside the establishment: Taylor said the Fed "caused" the crisis by deviating from precedent. Richard Fisher of the Dallas Fed said in November 2006 that the rate "was held lower longer than it should have been".

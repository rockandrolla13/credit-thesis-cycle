# Chapter 1: Warnings Ignored and the Conventional Wisdom

## Core Idea
The subprime crisis came with warnings. The authorities did not lack information. They lacked imagination, and the "conventional wisdom" of deregulation made the warnings impossible to hear. This is a short chapter: a case study of one episode (Jackson Hole, August 2005) plus one diagnostic framework.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Dearth of imagination, not of warnings**: Cassidy's reading of Pearl Harbor, 9/11 and Hurricane Katrina. The catastrophic outcomes "weren't regarded as low-probability outcomes"; they "weren't within the range of possibilities that were considered at all".
  - When to use: when testing whether a scenario set is complete. An omitted scenario is a different failure from a low-probability one.
- **Rajan's vulnerability cocktail** (Raghuram G. Rajan, then IMF chief economist, Jackson Hole symposium, August 2005). Four ingredients:
  1. **More risk and tighter linkages**: banks moved from deposit-and-lend into trading and into creating MBSs and CDOs, and kept some on their books. The system diversifies across small shocks but is exposed "to large systemic shocks: large shifts in asset prices or changes in aggregate liquidity".
  2. **Incentive-based compensation**: bonuses tied to returns give "perverse incentives" to take more risk, especially **tail risks** (very low probability, disastrous consequences).
  3. **Herding**: investors and traders ape each other, so they buy assets they consider overvalued.
  4. **Low interest rates after a period of high rates**, from financial liberalization or very accommodative monetary policy. Cheap money leads to more borrowing and bigger bets, euphoria, then a "sudden stop".
  - How: Rajan's combination rule is that compensation plus herding is "a volatile combination", because herding moves prices away from fundamentals and so raises the chance of the large realignments that trigger tail losses. Low rates make the cocktail "particularly volatile".
  - Rajan's ranking of shocks: "a shock to the equity markets, though large, may have less effect than a shock to the credit markets."
- **The Greenspan doctrine** (named by Fed governor Don Kohn in reply): new financial products such as securitized loans and credit default swaps let institutions diversify risk and choose risk profiles, and so make them "more robust". **Private regulation**, Greenspan's term for the actions of private parties to protect themselves, is "generally quite effective", and government risks undermining it.
- **The conventional wisdom** (John Kenneth Galbraith, *The Affluent Society*, 1958): the unquestioned assumptions that frame policy debate. It belongs to no party. Because it is identified with sound scholarship, the sceptic is disqualified by the act of doubting.

## Key Concepts
- **Tail risk**: an event with very low probability and disastrous consequences.
- **Herding**: the tendency of investors and traders to copy each other's strategies.
- **Sudden stop**: the abrupt end of freely flowing credit after euphoria.
- **"Froth"**: Greenspan's June 2005 word for local housing excess. He ruled out a nationwide bubble because "housing markets were local".
- **Economic fundamentals argument**: the list used to justify prices. Frank Nothaft (Freddie Mac): low mortgage rates, large-scale immigration, modest inventory of new homes. Bernanke (August and October 2005): jobs, incomes, very low mortgage rates, growing population, shortages of land and housing.

## Mental Models
- Use "has never fallen" claims as a warning sign. Karl Case noted the average US home price had risen every year since 1945. Nothaft said a fall "ain't going to happen".
- Read hostile reception by insiders as a measure of how entrenched a doctrine is, not as evidence against the critic.
- Denial has a simple source: nobody whose asset rises 20 or 30 percent a year likes to hear the wealth is illusory.

## Anti-patterns
- **The plane-crash analogy** (Lawrence Summers at Jackson Hole): the occasional crash should not hide that flying is safer. Summers called Rajan's premise "slightly lead-eyed" and "largely misguided". Cassidy presents this as the conventional wisdom defending itself.
- **Assuming executives will not reach for short-run gains** (Kohn): he found the market-failure case for intervening in compensation unconvincing. Parts II and III argue the opposite.

## Worked Example
**Levittown, Long Island, fall 2002.** Houses that Levitt and Sons sold after World War II for $7,990 were selling for roughly $300,000, up about 50 percent in two years. Buyers were the same people as always: police officers, firefighters, janitors, construction workers. The realtor Richard Dallow's arithmetic: at $330,000 with 5 percent down, the mortgage is $313,500, a jumbo mortgage "for Levittown". Cassidy's signal is prices losing touch with incomes at the bottom of the market, not only at the top. Supporting signals cited: Ian Morris of HSBC ("normal mom-and-pop homes are now very expensive relative to income"); The Economist, June 2005 (price-to-rent ratio at a historic high, prices rising more than 20 percent a year in some areas, "the biggest bubble in history").

## Key Takeaways
1. Warnings existed from 2002. Track price-to-income and price-to-rent, as the cited sceptics did.
2. Rajan's four ingredients are a ready checklist for systemic fragility.
3. Official reassurance used a fundamentals list. The same list appeared from Freddie Mac and from Bernanke.
4. A doctrine that both parties share is the hardest to question.

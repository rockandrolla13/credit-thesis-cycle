# Chapter 18: The Lure of Real Estate

## Core Idea
The US housing boom ran from the mid-1990s to 2006 and became a nationwide bubble from about 2003. Cassidy uses it to show his three-part template: every bubble needs complacent policymakers, financial innovation, and New Era psychology. He argues most buyers acted reasonably on limited information. This is a historical case study that carries a general bubble template.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three common features of bubbles** (Cassidy): "policymakers beholden to the illusion of stability; financial innovations that make speculating easier; and New Era thinking typified by overconfidence and disaster myopia."
  - How to use: score a market on all three. The chapter treats each in turn (monetary policy and housing policy, exotic mortgages and securitization, crowd psychology).
- **Stable vs unstable markets** (the demand-response test): in markets for cars or airline tickets a price rise cuts demand and prices fall back. In markets "that contain a speculative element, such as real estate, higher prices can generate higher demand".
  - Housing loop: prices rise, buyers expect more rises and pay more. Banks lend more because collateral is worth more. More credit means more demand and higher prices.
  - Supply responds too slowly. "During a speculative bubble, the laws of supply and demand don't get repealed, but they do get suspended."
- **Mortgage rate sets the price ceiling**: "Low interest rates provide the helium." Once prices are rising, the mortgage rate "effectively sets their upper limit, and the relationship is pretty much one to one" (worked below).
- **Why housing is prone to speculation**: Greenspan argued homes are illiquid and markets local. Cassidy answers that access to financing matters as much as liquidity. A margin account might double a stock investor's money. Banks routinely lend four times the down payment on a house, and from 2002 to 2006 often much more.
- **Price-to-income ratio as the valuation test**: median home price divided by median household income. Per Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies it held at about three from 1980 to the late 1990s (table below).
- **Exotic mortgages mapped to Minsky's regimes**:
  - **Interest-only loans** are speculative finance. No principal is paid for an initial period, often five years.
  - **Option ARMs** ("pick-a-payment loans") are "Ponzi finance, pure and simple". The minimum payment did not cover the interest, so the amount owed rose every month.
  - **Stated-income loans** ("liar loans"): income not verified, and in "stated-income stated-assets" loans neither income nor assets.
  - Common attribute: each let a borrower buy a more expensive property than a conventional loan would allow.
- **Market-induced moral hazard** (Cassidy): because lenders could pass new loans to Wall Street "often within days of their origination", they "didn't have to worry about the true costs of their actions". "Rather than encouraging lenders to allocate capital wisely, the market was sending signals that greatly distorted their behavior." Remedy implied: make lenders keep some of the loans they originate.
- **Lenders' prisoner's dilemma**: established lenders would have preferred traditional loans. In a bubble, firms that keep old standards "lose market share, their stock prices suffer, and their top managers get criticized". "Going with the crowd is a dominant strategy: it is the rationally irrational thing to do."
- **Buyers in an information cascade**: nearly everyone's **private signal** said homes were overvalued. The **public signal** was that homes sold at these prices every day and kept rising. People followed the public signal, so "the information that homes are overvalued didn't get reflected in the market".

## Key Concepts
- **Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM)**: a loan with a lower fixed rate for a set period, usually one, five or eight years. Fixed-rate loans fell from three fifths of mortgages issued in 2001 to a third in 2006.
- **Alt-A**: mortgages, and bonds backed by them, that fall between prime and subprime. Originations rose from $60 billion in 2001 to $400 billion in 2006.
- **FICO score**: credit score from 300 to 850. A typical person scores about 725, and 620 was a common cutoff for stated-income loans.
- **Matching strategy**: Countrywide's commitment to offer any deal a rival offered. Cassidy reads it as tit for tat.
- **Ownership society**: the Bush administration's home-ownership agenda, continuing the Clinton administration's National Homeownership Strategy of 1993.
- **Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac**: by 2008 they together owned about $1.5 trillion of home loans and mortgage securities and had guaranteed roughly another $3.5 trillion.
- **Wildcat banks**: 1830s banks that financed a land rush with notes and little real capital.

## Mental Models
- At the peak "it appears that the really crazy people are those not in the market". Cassidy says economists who blame stupidity miss this point.
- Bubbles "erase prior history from the minds of the participants". San Francisco prices fell 11 percent from April 1990 to April 1996, about a third after inflation, and buyers in 2005 had forgotten.
- Define rationality as "making the best judgment you can on the basis of limited information". On that definition most home buyers were rational.
- Discount expert reassurance issued late in a boom. In September 2005 a New York Fed study by Himmelberg, Mayer and Sinai found "little evidence of a housing bubble".

## Anti-patterns
- **"Housing markets are local, so no national bubble"** (Greenspan, April 2002). What was new in this boom was "its geographic spread".
- **Recommending adjustable-rate loans at the bottom of the rate cycle** (Greenspan, 2004).
- **Relying on credit scores in place of income verification.**
- **"It can't go to zero" and "you just hold on 'til it comes right back up"**: speculators' reasoning quoted from Fortune, May 2005.

## Worked Example
**Mortgage rate to affordable price.** A buyer can pay $2,500 a month.
1. At a 10 percent thirty-year fixed rate the buyer can borrow $285,000. With a 20 percent down payment the price ceiling is $342,000.
2. At 5 percent the same buyer can afford a $564,000 home, if the larger down payment is available.
3. Loan type moves the ceiling again. In May 2005 a $500,000 house cost roughly $2,100 a month on an interest-only loan, against about $3,000 on a fully amortizing thirty-year loan.

**Countrywide.** Angelo Mozilo wrote on September 25, 2006: "We have no way, with any reasonable certainty, to assess the real risk of holding these loans on our balance sheet." The next day he wrote that "pay options are currently mispriced in the secondary market". He did not stop issuing them. He made about $140 million from options and stock sales between 2005 and 2007. When the mortgage market froze, loans awaiting securitization brought the firm to the brink of collapse.

**Liar loan audit.** In 2006 the Mortgage Asset Research Institute compared a hundred stated-income loans with IRS records. In almost 60 percent the borrower had overstated income by more than 50 percent. Brokers were told to resubmit with higher incomes, and systems let them try "more than fifteen times".

## Key Takeaways
1. A national price-to-income ratio of 4.6 against a long-run 3 was the plain warning.
2. Watch the loan mix. A rising share of interest-only and negative-amortization loans is Minsky's drift in data.
3. Survey tell: San Francisco buyers in 2005 expected price rises averaging 14 percent a year for ten years, and about a third expected far more.
4. Policy pushed the same way from both parties: the Community Reinvestment Act, pressure on Fannie and Freddie, and the American Dream Downpayment Act. Home ownership passed 69 percent in early 2004.
5. Securitization demand was "the primary driver" of falling credit standards.

# Chapter 14: The Rational Herd

## Core Idea
Herding has three rational sources: career incentives, the risk of betting against naive traders, and inference from other people's actions. Each lets "rational behavior on the part of individual investors" produce "a collectively irrational outcome: a bubble". Sophisticated money rides bubbles. It does not fight them.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Reputational herding** (David Scharfstein and Jeremy Stein, "Herd Behavior and Investment", *American Economic Review*, June 1990). In some circumstances it is optimal for a manager to mimic others instead of trusting personal judgement.
  - Logic: a manager who goes with the crowd and fails shares the blame. A contrarian who fails "bears sole responsibility".
  - System effect, per Stein: opinion loses diversity, so the market is "more fragile and unstable". "If the group shifts, then everybody shifts."
  - Evidence: Judith Chevalier and Glenn Ellison found young fund managers who deviated from peers more likely to lose their jobs "regardless of how their funds performed". Harrison Hong, Jeffrey Kubik and Amit Solomon (1983 to 1996) found inexperienced analysts who strayed from consensus lost jobs at higher rates. "Being bold and good does not significantly improve an analyst's future career prospects."
- **Noise trader approach and limits to arbitrage** (Andrei Shleifer and colleagues; *Inefficient Markets*, 2000). Investing is a game between rational sophisticates and naive **noise traders** who react to irrelevant information.
  - **Noise trader risk**: "the possibility that the mispricing gets worse before it disappears". So "even an arbitrage that looks nearly perfect from the outside is in reality quite risky and therefore likely to be limited".
  - Three obstacles to shorting a bubble: (1) borrowing the shares is hard when few are outstanding, (2) quarterly judgement and investor redemptions, (3) leverage and margin calls.
  - Conclusion: "Rational arbitrage can destabilize security prices." "Smart investors might rationally choose to jump on the bandwagon."
- **Four-period bubble-riding model** (Shleifer and colleagues):
  1. Period one: not much happens.
  2. Period two: rational speculators bid up prices to entice noise traders.
  3. Period three: noise traders see rising prices and buy, pushing prices higher. Speculators sell out above their cost.
  4. Period four: prices collapse and noise traders take the losses.
  - Cassidy's caveat: "The model is neat, too neat, probably." Timing exit is hard.
- **Social learning and information cascades** (Sushil Bikhchandani, David Hirshleifer and Ivo Welch, *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 1998). Social learning is inferring information from others' actions. If private information is not wholly reliable, rational people weigh others' actions, and often "everybody will end up doing the same thing".
  - Failure mode: once a cascade begins, "public information stops accumulating". Later individuals ignore their private signals, "which thus never join the pool of public knowledge". Outcomes become "essentially arbitrary".
  - When to expect one: where private information is weak. Not for new cars, which a buyer can test drive. Yes for stocks and commodities, where "the average investor is pretty much at sea" and reads a rising price as others knowing more.
- **Short-term momentum and long-term mean reversion**: over days, weeks and months winners keep winning. Over several years high fliers fall back.
  - Evidence: Narasimhan Jegadeesh and Sheridan Titman (1965 to 1989). Buying stocks that rose over the prior three to twelve months and selling those that fell beat the average, in every three-year period per Titman.

## Key Concepts
- **Conformity under social pressure** (Solomon Asch, early 1950s): with stooges giving a unanimous wrong answer, roughly one in four subjects stayed independent every time. The normal error rate on such tests is about one in a hundred.
- **Informational conformity** (Morton Deutsch and Harold Gerard, 1953): with answers given privately the error rate was still 23 percent, against 37 percent in Asch's trials. Subjects could not believe everyone else was wrong.
- **Neural evidence** (Gregory Berns, 2005): the error rate rose to 41 percent when subjects saw others' wrong answers. Brain imaging suggested peer pressure changes perception.
- **Anomalies**: January and Monday effects, small caps beating large caps, value beating growth.
- **Fama-French findings**: 1963 to 1990, low price-to-book stocks systematically outperformed. Using 1941 to 1986 data, initial dividend yield explained more than a quarter of the variability in total market returns. Low yield preceded poor returns.
- **Momentum funds**: funds that buy what is rising and hold briefly. They "don't even pretend to estimate" fundamental value.

## Mental Models
- "There's no difference between being early and being wrong" (Barton Biggs, on Jeffrey Vinik).
- Treat professional consensus as partly a career-safety equilibrium, not independent evidence.
- When a market's participants privately call it a bubble and stay in, expect fragility. Positions will reverse together.
- Split a bubble's participants in two. Insiders play the beauty contest (rational irrationality). The public is moved by psychology (Ch 15).

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming speculators stabilize**: Markus Brunnermeier and Stefan Nagel (*Journal of Finance*, October 2004): "Hedge funds were riding the bubble, not fighting it."
- **Believing bubbles are the exception**: Burton Malkiel conceded in 2003 that the market "may have temporarily failed" in the dot-com period, and called such episodes "the exception rather than the rule". Cassidy notes that this was just as the housing bubble was starting.
- **Treating herd members as lemmings**: Cassidy says the copying is often reasonable inference.

## Worked Example
**Shorting Amazon in 1999.** The stock quintupled in 1998 and looked grossly overvalued.
1. You must borrow shares to short. New Internet companies had few shares outstanding.
2. You short ten thousand shares. More buyers enter daily. By April 1999 the stock is up another 80 percent, so the trade is down 80 percent.
3. You are judged quarterly, face redemptions, and are leveraged, so margin calls arrive.
4. The price did tumble in 2000. Only a patient, unleveraged investor would still hold the position.

**Being early: three managers.**
- Jeffrey Vinik (Fidelity Magellan) moved toward bonds at the end of 1995, citing "euphoria". The fund lagged and he resigned in May 1996.
- Robert Stansky, his successor, made America Online the $90 billion fund's fourth-largest holding late in 1998 and said he evaluated "investor psychology". He kept his job after the crash and ran the fund until late 2005. Cassidy reads this as confirming Scharfstein-Stein.
- Stanley Druckenmiller (Soros) sold technology early in 1999, lagged, bought back in the summer, and lost close to $2.5 billion when the bubble burst in March and April 2000.

**Hedge fund positioning.** Fifty-three hedge funds raised their weighting in high-priced technology stocks from 16 to 29 percent in the third quarter of 1999, and to almost a third by March 2000, when the NASDAQ peaked at 5,132.52. The market weighting was about a fifth. They cut individual holdings before each stock's peak.

## Key Takeaways
1. Survey tell: in the May 1999 Barron's Big Money Poll, 72 percent of professional managers said the market was in a speculative bubble. They stayed invested.
2. Career risk, funding risk and weak private information are the three conditions to check.
3. Rationality "actually aggravated the market failure".
4. In a cascade, prices stop aggregating information. That breaks Hayek's mechanism from within.

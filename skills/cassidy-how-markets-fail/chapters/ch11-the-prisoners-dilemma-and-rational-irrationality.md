# Chapter 11: The Prisoner's Dilemma and Rational Irrationality

## Core Idea
This chapter gives the formal core of the book. In the prisoner's dilemma each player's dominant strategy leads to an outcome that is worse for both. Cassidy calls the general result **rational irrationality** and applies it directly to mortgage lending.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rational irrationality** (Cassidy's own term). Definition in this chapter: "a situation in which the application of rational self-interest in the marketplace leads to an inferior and socially irrational outcome." He says the prisoner's dilemma is "in many cases" its basis.
  - Earlier statements: "purposeful but self-defeating behavior" (Introduction). First labelled example (end of Ch 10): a profit-making health insurer rationally turns away sick people, which for society is "inhumane and inefficient".
  - Three conditions drawn from the chapter: (1) outcomes depend on what others do, (2) each must choose independently, since collusion is ruled out or illegal, (3) deviating from cooperation pays whatever the other does.
  - What it is not: stupidity, or a failure to see the better collective outcome. Ken Binmore: a player "isn't some abstract entity called 'everybody'".
- **The prisoner's dilemma** (experiment by Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at RAND, January 1950; named and framed by Albert Tucker a few months later). Two players cannot communicate. Both gain if both cooperate, but each gains more by defecting whatever the other does.
  - **Dominant strategy**: the option that is best whatever the opponent does. In the one-shot game it is to confess, "even though it leads to a bad outcome for both players".
  - Method: fix the opponent's choice, pick your best reply, repeat for the opponent's other choice. If the same reply wins both times, it is dominant.
- **Repeated games and tit for tat** (Robert Axelrod, University of Michigan, 1980 tournament, fifteen strategies, two hundred rounds per match). If the game repeats many times and players do not know when it ends, cooperation is a rational solution, and so is non-cooperation. The winner was tit for tat: "Cooperate on the first round and then in each consecutive round copy what your opponent did in the previous round."
  - Cassidy's limit: "Applying Axelrod's results to economics is dangerous." Chief executives and traders are judged quarterly, so their setting is closer to the one-shot game.
- **N-person prisoner's dilemma**: with two players cooperation is difficult. With ten, twenty or a hundred it is "virtually impossible". Lenders face this version.
- **The Tragedy of the Commons** (Garrett Hardin, 1968). A herder keeps all the proceeds from one more animal and shares the overgrazing cost with everyone. So each "rational herdsman" adds another, and another. "Ruin is the destination toward which all men rush."
  - Cassidy on privatization: it sometimes helps, but it "doesn't remove the conflict between private benefits and social benefits". Property rights may be necessary. Blind reliance on self-interest is "a recipe for further environmental catastrophes".

## Key Concepts
- **Zero-sum game**: one player's winnings are another's losses. Most methods in von Neumann and Morgenstern's *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* (1944) applied to these.
- **Positive-sum (non-zero-sum) game**: cooperation and mutual gain are possible. John Nash introduced a general solution method in the late 1940s.
- **Tacit collusion**: firms restricting supply without explicit agreement. Hard to sustain, most of all for a homogeneous good such as oil where cheating is hard to detect.
- **Individual vs collective rationality**: the conflict the dilemma typifies. Flood and Dresher hoped someone would resolve it. "The solution never came."
- **Global commons**: atmosphere, oceans, oil and gas reserves, biodiversity.

## Mental Models
- Do not fixate on the best joint outcome and rationalize why players reach it. Cassidy says he made this mistake in his college entrance interview.
- Use the dilemma in both directions. It is good when it breaks cartels (OPEC quota cheating keeps oil cheap). It is bad when more output is not socially desirable.
- Think of the residential mortgage as a product that can be oversupplied. Only a limited number of households can service a mortgage to maturity.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Rational players would never choose an inferior outcome"**: true only for joint decisions. The game requires independent choice.
- **"Both will reason alike, so both will deny"**: ignores the conflict. If you believe the other will deny, confessing still pays.
- **Counting on reputation in short-horizon settings**: one bad quarter can end a career.

## Worked Example
**Two coal power plants.** Each earns $20 million operating profit. A residents' lawsuit costs each $10 million a year, so net profit is $10 million. Filters cost $5 million each. If both install, the suit is dropped and each nets $15 million. Explicit coordination on price is illegal under the Sherman Act, so each decides alone. If only one installs, the laggard sells cheaper power and nets $20 million while the installer nets $5 million.
- If B installs, A earns 15 by installing and 20 by not. A does not install.
- If B does not install, A earns 5 by installing and 10 by not. A does not install.
- The same holds for B. Result: (10, 10), half of the available (15, 15), and the town keeps the smoke.

**Mapping to mortgage lending (Table 11.3).** "Low" is tight credit standards and "High" is loose ones. When house prices rise and rates are low, some lenders loosen. Responsible lenders at first play "Low". They watch rivals gain share, and "the pressure to switch strategies becomes irresistible". In the end almost all play "High", "with deleterious consequences for one and all".

## Key Takeaways
1. Find the dominant strategy. If it is defection and the horizon is short, expect the bad equilibrium.
2. More players make cooperation harder. Industry-wide restraint will not arise by itself.
3. Experiment cited: in one-shot play with ordinary people, about 90 percent choose the self-preserving strategy by the tenth trial.
4. Experts are not immune. In the 1950 RAND run of a hundred plays, Armen Alchian cooperated sixty-eight times and John D. Williams seventy-eight.
5. The first step is recognizing "the pervasive nature of rational irrationality and how difficult it is to overcome".

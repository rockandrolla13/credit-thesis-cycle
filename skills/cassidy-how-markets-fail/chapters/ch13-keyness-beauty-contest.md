# Chapter 13: Keynes's Beauty Contest

## Core Idea
Keynes argued that the future is not merely risky but unknowable, so asset prices rest on convention and on guesses about other people's guesses. In that setting, following the crowd is individually rational even when prices have left fundamentals. Cassidy states that Keynes's theories "pivot on rational irrationality", not on mass irrationality.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Risk vs uncertainty** (John Maynard Keynes, *The General Theory*, 1936, and his 1937 reply to reviewers).
  - **Probabilistic risk**: the distribution is known, as with actuarial tables for life insurance.
  - **Intrinsic uncertainty**: one-off decisions where "our existing knowledge does not provide a sufficient basis for a calculated mathematical expectation". Keynes's examples: the prospect of a European war, the price of copper and the rate of interest twenty years hence, the obsolescence of a new invention. "About these matters, there is no scientific basis on which to form any calculable probability whatsoever. We simply do not know."
  - When to use: before applying any probability model. Ask whether a stable distribution exists at all. Keynes held that near ignorance is "the normal course of affairs", not a feature of crises only.
- **Conventional valuation**: a price set by "the mass psychology of a large number of ignorant individuals". It "is liable to change violently" on factors that do not much affect prospective yield, "since there will be no strong roots of conviction to hold it steady".
- **The beauty contest**: Keynes's newspaper competition in which entrants pick the six prettiest faces from a hundred photographs and the prize goes to whoever matches the average choice.
  - First degree: pick what you find prettiest.
  - Second degree: pick what average opinion finds prettiest.
  - Third degree: "anticipating what average opinion expects the average opinion to be". Some practise "the fourth, fifth, and higher degrees".
  - Rule for playing: "withhold your own opinions and try to select the outcome on which others will converge."
- **Why fundamentals do not reliably win in the long run** (Keynes's three reasons):
  1. Fundamental investing is "so difficult to-day as to be scarcely practicable", given the near impossibility of prediction.
  2. Leverage. Many professionals use borrowed money. In a fall they face margin calls and their positions are liquidated.
  3. Peer pressure. The long-term investor looks "eccentric, unconventional and rash". "Worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally."
- **Destabilizing speculation**: against the Chicago view (Ch 7) that professionals correct mispricing, Keynes held they add to it. They are concerned "with what the market will value it at, under the influence of mass psychology, three months or a year hence".
- **Fallacy of composition**: confusing the sum with the parts. Keynes's charge against believers in the invisible hand.
- **The multiplier**: knock-on effects mean an initial rise in saving of $100 "can generate a fall in spending of $200 or $300". It explains how small shocks lead to recessions.
- **Liquidity trap**: in a slump the wealthy flee risky paper for cash. This rise in the "propensity to hoard" means extra money is held, not spent, and further increases in the money supply have "little or no impact on interest rates or spending". Keynes called them rare and cited the United States in 1932.
- **Rationality as a continuum** (Cassidy): at one end the "ultrarationality" of Lucas's agents, at the other unthinking behaviour. Most people occupy "a realm of purposeful but constricted decision-making, of limited information", using forethought and rules of thumb.

## Key Concepts
- **Greater Fool theory**: today's name for Keynes's aim "to pass the bad, or depreciating, half-crown to the other fellow".
- **Animal spirits**: the "spontaneous urge to action rather than inaction".
- **Stimulus**: the central Keynesian policy. In a deep recession somebody must spend more, and the burden falls on government.
- **"Bastard Keynesianism"**: Joan Robinson's phrase for the Hicks-Modigliani textbook model. Its only financial assets are money and risk-free government bonds, so it "largely ignores the financial sector".
- **Psychological feedback loop**: Robert Shiller's term for the chain from price declines to selling to further price declines.

## Mental Models
- Treat a large price move with no matching news as evidence of an internally generated move.
- Think of downturns as positive feedback: they "feed on themselves and get amplified".
- Ask what horizon a manager is paid and judged on. A correct long-run view can still be the wrong trade.
- Watch what governments do in hard times. Cassidy lists stimulus packages under Reagan (1981), George W. Bush (2001 and 2008) and Obama.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming known distributions**: the rational-expectations and efficient-market premise. Cassidy says Keynes "refuted it in advance".
- **Reading Keynes as a theory of wage rigidity**: he used rigidity "mainly as an analytical device". His concern was irreducible uncertainty.
- **Reading Keynes as a theory of mass irrationality**: Keynes wrote, "We should not conclude from this that everything depends on waves of irrational psychology."

## Worked Example
**Keynes's 25 / 30 / 20 rule.** You believe an investment's prospective yield justifies a value of 30. It trades at 25. You also believe the market will value it at 20 three months from now. Keynes: "it is not sensible to pay 25" for it. The fundamental buyer lags the market, may face larger losses first, and if leveraged may be liquidated before being proved right. Following the herd "cannot be described as wrongheaded".

**Black Monday, October 19, 1987.** The Dow fell 508 points, from 2,246.73 to 1,738.74, a fall of 22.6 percent and its biggest percentage fall. No news explained it. The prior week stocks had dropped almost 10 percent and the Dow fell 108 points on the Friday. Programs that sold as indices fell through set levels accentuated the panic. In Shiller's survey, investors ranked the 200-point fall at the opening as the news most on their minds, then the prior week's declines. More than 60 percent cited investor psychology over fundamentals.

**Indian jute, 1907.** Some merchants secretly sold adulterated jute. Keynes judged that competition would force honest merchants to copy them: adulteration "is plainly opposed to the interests of the trade as a whole", yet "it is nevertheless to the interest of every individual to practice it". His remedy was legislation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Separate risk from uncertainty before pricing anything.
2. Model what other investors expect others to expect, not only fundamentals.
3. Leverage and career risk make herd-following rational for professionals.
4. Keynes was a successful investor on these views. The King's College fund grew from £30,000 to £380,000 between 1924 and 1946, which Cassidy gives as more than 12 percent a year.
5. Models with no stock, commodity or mortgage markets cannot show where instability arises.

# Chapter 21: A Matter of Incentives

## Core Idea
Every participant in the housing boom responded sensibly to the incentives in front of them. The incentives were distorted, they reinforced each other, and the result was "an outbreak of rational irrationality". The chapter gives Cassidy's incentive map of the boom (Table 21.1) and a precise account of why bank pay rewards bets that can sink the firm.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Table 21.1: Incentives and the housing boom** (reproduced under Reference Tables). One row per decision-maker: the distorted incentive and the result.
  - How to use: build the same three-column table for any boom. Then look for interactions, because "the table doesn't show … how the various incentive problems aggravated and reinforced one another".
- **Combination, not single cause**: "lax monetary policy, deregulation, and financial innovation shocked the economy out of its stable configuration, placing it on the bubble path. No single one of these factors can be held solely responsible; it was the combination that did the damage."
  - Normal stabilizers that were missing: "the memory of past busts, together with financial regulations and restrictive social conventions".
- **The triptych**: securitization, credit insurance, and risk-based capital requirements. Together they let a bank "issue questionable loans or buy questionable assets, shunt them off into an SIV, and then guarantee itself against the risk of default by purchasing some credit default swaps".
- **Plender's law of insurance** (John Plender, Financial Times, October 2006): "while hedging can reduce the risk to an individual party taking out the insurance, it increases the risk for the system as a whole because of moral hazard." The existence of insurance makes people less risk averse.
- **The trader's option**: a trader is like an entrepreneur renting part of the firm's balance sheet under a profit share. Some desks pay up to half of profits above a target. The downside is capped at losing the job. This equals a **nonrecourse loan** from the firm. The firm cannot claim the trader's assets or past bonuses.
  - Partial fix: charge desks a risk-scaled rental fee for capital, so that pay follows risk-adjusted return (**alpha**).
  - How it is gamed: take risks the benchmark does not capture. Writing credit default swaps earns a steady return and carries hidden tail risk. Raghuram Rajan: such strategies "have the appearance of producing very high alphas", and "every once in a while … they will blow up". Annual pay arrives before the blow-up.
- **The CEO's option**: the same asymmetry at the top. If things go well, options gain. If badly, there is a generous retirement package. Cassidy calls it a bigger problem than the trader's option, because desks face limits and chief executives can "put entire firms on the line".
- **Principal-agent problem** (Michael Jensen and William Meckling, 1976): a principal employs an agent whose effort is hard to monitor. Two partial solutions are payment on completion and a success fee. The **Jensen doctrine** applied this to chief executives by paying them in shares and options.
  - Outcome: fewer than one in three chief executives had options in 1980 and seven in ten by 1994. Then the accounting scandals of 2001-2002. Jensen in 2002: overvalued stock "is to managers what heroin is to a drug addict".
- **Levered, asymmetric equity pay** (Lucian Bebchuk and Holger Spamann, 2009): in a thinly capitalized bank, stock or options give the chief executive "a heavily levered and asymmetric bet on the value of the firm's assets". Shareholders take nearly all the gains. In a catastrophic loss, equity is wiped out and creditors bear most of the burden. A rational chief executive "will be willing to literally bet the bank".
  - Why "shareholder value" misleads at a bank: at a firm with little debt, acting for shareholders maximizes the firm's value. At a bank, "the shareholders' equity is a mere sliver".
- **Liquidity is not available to all at once** (Keynes, *The General Theory*, chapter 12): "there is no such thing as liquidity of investment for the community as a whole." If everybody sells at once, prices collapse and the market seizes. Keynes compared it to Snap, Old Maid and Musical Chairs.

## Key Concepts
- **Externality of bank pay**: when a chief executive levers equity "thirty or forty to one", the result "can bring down the entire economy". Pay schemes "take no account of this externality".
- **Too big to fail**: the assumption that some institutions will be rescued. With deposit insurance and securitization it induces moral hazard.
- **Ex ante vs ex post**: Bebchuk and Spamann: "The mere fact that a risky strategy turned out to produce losses ex post does not mean it was not rational to follow it ex ante."
- **Liquidity**: Cassidy's gloss is "the ability to sell in bulk".
- **Search for yield**: the response of funds to very low rates, satisfied by investment-grade subprime paper.

## Mental Models
- Start from incentives. "Economics, when you strip away the guff and the mathematical sophistry, is largely about incentives." Markets, too, "can create damaging incentives".
- Prefer system explanations. Jensen: "It is much more likely that there were some bad systems in place" than that bad people did it.
- Do not treat a chief executive's large shareholding as proof of caution. That reasoning "starts at the end of the story". The stake was itself the product of risky strategies that had paid off so far.
- In a credit bubble, "sitting on the sidelines simply isn't a realistic option" for the head of a large public financial firm. Growth in market share drives the stock price and the chief executive's standing.

## Anti-patterns
- **Paying annually on unadjusted profit**: it rewards selling tail risk.
- **Equity-only incentives in a highly levered firm**: they align the chief executive with a sliver of the capital structure.
- **Counting on "pools of liquidity"**: Chuck Prince, July 2007, said a disruptive event "now needs to be much more disruptive than it used to be". He added, "I don't think we're at that point."

## Worked Example
**The CEO's option in numbers.**
1. Deal one has a 2/3 chance of a $60 million profit and a 1/3 chance of a $60 million loss. Expected value: (2/3 × $60 million) − (1/3 × $60 million) = $20 million. Accept. (Fractions recovered from EPUB images 00005.jpg and 00006.jpg.)
2. Deal two has a 99/100 chance of a $100 million profit and a 1/100 chance of a $10 billion loss. Expected value: (99/100 × $100,000,000) − (1/100 × $10,000,000,000) = −$1 million. Reject. (Fractions recovered from EPUB images 00007.jpg and 00008.jpg.)
3. The chief executive is paid $2 million a year plus 2 percent of profits. If deal two pays off, which is 99 percent likely, pay is $4 million. If it fails, the bank writes off $10 billion and pay is still $2 million.
4. So the chief executive accepts a deal with negative expected value for the firm. "In the long run, unlikely things happen."

**Citigroup, 2005.** Citi's stock had gone nowhere in five years. Rivals were deep in mortgage bonds and CDOs. The board, with Robert Rubin advising more "intelligent risk taking", asked for a growth strategy. Prince, his authority already questioned, expanded securitization of subprime mortgages and buyout loans. "Going with the crowd was a 'dominant' strategy."

## Key Takeaways
1. Map each actor's incentive before judging anyone's intelligence or honesty.
2. Look for interactions between incentives. They did the damage.
3. Pay that is annual, asymmetric and equity-linked in a levered firm rewards tail risk.
4. Insurance for one party raises risk for the system.
5. Market liquidity vanishes when all need it.

# Introduction

## Core Idea
The 2007-2009 credit crunch was not an aberration caused by greed, stupidity or crime. It was "a failure of business as usual": the rational pursuit of self-interest, under free-market ideology and cheap money, created and prolonged it. The book names the pattern **rational irrationality** and sets it against three illusions of **utopian economics**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The three illusions of utopian economics** (Cassidy's own labels):
  1. **Illusion of harmony**: the claim that free markets always generate good outcomes.
  2. **Illusion of stability**: the idea that a free market economy is sturdy, well grounded and self-correcting. Cassidy says general equilibrium theory gives no "scientific" support for this.
  3. **Illusion of predictability**: the premise that movements in financial markets follow regular patterns, so that their overall distribution, if not their daily moves, can be foreseen. Prices of MBSs, CDOs and CDSs rested on it.
  - When to use: as a three-item checklist against any argument that a market will police itself.
- **Rational irrationality**: "purposeful but self-defeating behavior". Individually rational actions by competing agents produce a bad collective outcome. Cassidy ties it to the prisoner's dilemma of game theory.
  - When to use: when each participant can explain why its own action is sensible, yet the aggregate is destructive.
  - How: ask what would happen to one firm that pulled back alone. If rivals take its business, its short-term earnings fall and its stock price suffers, then the competitive environment gives "no incentive to pull back".
- **Utopian economics vs reality-based economics**: Part I traces utopian economics from Adam Smith to Alan Greenspan, including general equilibrium theory (Walras, Pareto, Arrow) and the Chicago School (Hayek, Friedman). Part II covers "reality-based economics", Cassidy's preferred name for the economics of market failure. He says it is broader and older than behavioral economics, and that much of it keeps the axioms of rationality.

## Key Concepts
- **Market failure**: cases where markets give individuals and firms incentives to act in ways that are individually rational but damaging to themselves and others. Cassidy calls it endemic in health care, high technology and finance.
- **Greenspan Bubble Era**: about 1997 to 2007, with three separate speculative bubbles: technology stocks, real estate, and physical commodities such as oil.
- **"Primitive" of the capitalist model**: Cassidy's term for greed. It is ever present, so it explains nothing specific.
- **Wrong price signals**: the common theme of Part II. The market, through the price system, often sends people the wrong signals. People are not irrational within their mental and environmental limits.
- **NINJA loans**: mortgage loans to borrowers with no income, no jobs and no assets.

## Mental Models
- Treat the last decade as a natural experiment: what happens to a financially driven economy when you deregulate it and supply large amounts of cheap money?
- Once a bubble begins, do not rely on free markets to allocate resources sensibly. The prospect of quick profits distorts incentives.
- Downplay character explanations. Cassidy argues the Wall Street chief executives were "neither sociopaths nor idiots nor felons".

## Anti-patterns
- **Blaming only the Fed, the Treasury, or Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac**: Cassidy cites Richard Posner's 2008 statement that the crisis was primarily the consequence of decisions by private firms under minimal regulation.
- **Trusting risk-spreading by complex securities**: the argument that MBSs, CDOs and CDSs made the system safer. Cassidy says the risk-spreading "proved to be illusory".

## Worked Example
**Chuck Prince's dilemma (Citigroup, chief executive 2003-2007).** Suppose Prince announced in 2005 that Citi was leaving the subprime market as too risky. Rivals would not copy him. They would take the business Citi left. Citi's short-term earnings would lag its peers, its stock price would come under pressure, and Prince would be written off. Prince told the Financial Times in July 2007: "as long as the music is playing, you've got to get up and dance. We're still dancing." Four months later Citi revealed billions of dollars of losses and Prince resigned. Cassidy calls this the prisoner's dilemma in action.

**Greenspan's admission (October 23, 2008, House Oversight Committee, chairman Henry Waxman).** Greenspan said he "made a mistake in presuming that the self-interests of organizations, specifically banks and others" would protect shareholders, and that he "found a flaw in the model".

## Key Takeaways
1. Cassidy's stated cause set for the subprime boom: bounded cognition, uncertainty, hidden information, trend-following, and plentiful credit.
2. Policy mattered too. Cassidy says Greenspan and Bernanke kept interest rates too low for too long, which distorted price signals and created the conditions for the housing bubble.
3. Credit-driven boom-and-bust cycles recur. The text states 124 systemic banking crises around the world "during the past forty years".
4. Markets do work in many settings. Cassidy says nothing in the book argues for returning to central planning.

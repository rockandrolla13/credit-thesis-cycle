# Chapter 6: The Evangelist

## Core Idea
Milton Friedman did more than anyone to bring laissez-faire back. He recast the Great Depression as government failure, replaced Keynesian demand management with monetarism and the natural rate, and tied economic freedom to political freedom. Cassidy's key judgement: Friedman "didn't defeat Keynesianism so much as define it out of existence."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Friedman's four contributions** (Cassidy's list):
  1. Championing individual measures: cutting government programs, reducing taxes, deregulating industries.
  2. A revisionist account of the Great Depression as government failure, not market failure.
  3. A critique of Keynesian demand management, with monetarism as the alternative.
  4. The link, taken from John Stuart Mill, between economic freedom and political freedom.
- **The monetary explanation of the Great Depression** (Friedman and Anna J. Schwartz, *A Monetary History of the United States*, 1963): the culprit was a sharp fall in the money stock. Between summer 1929 and spring 1933, currency in circulation plus demand deposits fell by almost a third. The Fed's ineptness in 1930 and 1931 converted "a moderate contraction into a major catastrophe".
  - Precedent noted: Irving Fisher criticized the Fed on similar grounds as early as 1933.
  - Endorsement: Ben Bernanke, 2002: "You're right, we did it. We're very sorry. But thanks to you, we won't do it again."
- **Monetarism**: Congress should instruct the Fed to hit a set growth rate of the money stock. Friedman's preferred range was "somewhere between 3 and 5 percent a year". No added regulation and no extra central bank discretion.
- **The natural rate of unemployment** (Friedman, American Economic Association presidential address, December 1967; also pioneered by Edmund Phelps). Some unemployment is unavoidable. Pushing unemployment below the natural rate makes workers bid up wages and firms raise prices, so inflation accelerates.
  - Rule: "There is always a temporary trade-off between inflation and unemployment. There is no permanent trade-off."
  - Test, per Robert Lucas: the conditional forecast that a high-inflation decade should not have less unemployment than a low-inflation decade. The 1970s confirmed it.
- **Friedman's description of contagion** (*Capitalism and Freedom*, 1962): a bank facing withdrawals calls loans, sells investments or withdraws its deposits. That pressures other banks, which pressure others. The scramble for cash forces down securities prices, "renders banks insolvent that would otherwise have been entirely sound", shakes depositors, and restarts the cycle.
  - When to use: as a compact causal chain for fire-sale insolvency. Cassidy notes it comes from the leading free-market economist.
- **Self-regulation thesis** (*Free to Choose*, 1980): the only workable regulation is self-regulation, because "alternative sources of supply protect the consumer". Firms will not risk their brand names.

## Key Concepts
- **Phillips Curve**: the inverse relation between unemployment and inflation, from data collated by A.W.H. Phillips of LSE.
- **Stagflation**: the 1970s outcome in which inflation reached double digits and unemployment rose.
- **Fine-tuning**: active Keynesian management of demand through taxes and spending.
- **Quantity theory of money**: the old doctrine Friedman invoked. The text does not state its formula.
- **Shock program**: Friedman's March 1975 advice to Pinochet. Control the money supply, cut government spending by a quarter in six months, remove obstacles to the private market.
- **Chicago Boys**: Chilean economists trained at the University of Chicago. Arnold Harberger was their primary mentor.
- **Mont Pelerin Society**: association of conservative and libertarian academics, first meeting 1947.

## Mental Models
- Notice the definitional move. Natural rate theory says the economy is self-equilibrating, so long recessions cannot occur and stimulus is unnecessary. Cassidy stresses that nothing in Friedman's work shows that stimulus would fail in a slump.
- Use Friedman against his followers. He "was well aware of the dangers that can arise in an unregulated financial system" and covered the banking crises of 1873, 1884, 1890, 1893 and 1907.
- Read the framing "only two ways of coordinating" (central direction or voluntary cooperation) as, in Cassidy's words, "stark and simplistic".

## Anti-patterns
- **Brand reputation as sufficient protection**: Friedman's claim that drug and industrial companies will not sell dangerous products. Part II (hidden information) and Part III (rating agencies, lenders) contradict it.
- **Privatizing everything**: Cassidy notes that without government power to acquire property and rezone land, setting up a national park is practically impossible.
- **Declaring monopoly exaggerated**: asserted in *Capitalism and Freedom*. Ch 10 disputes it.

## Worked Example
**Chile.** When Friedman and Harberger arrived in March 1975, inflation ran above 500 percent a year. Pinochet followed the shock program: he abolished price and wage controls, sold hundreds of state firms, privatized pensions, liberalized trade and deregulated the financial sector. Inflation fell below 10 percent, foreign investment poured in and GDP rose sharply. In 1982 the economy went into a deep slump and financial scandals erupted. A second wave of reformers took office in 1985 and growth resumed. Friedman's own figures for 1973 to 1995: real income per head multiplied more than two and a half times, inflation fell from 500 percent to 8 percent, infant mortality fell from 66 to 13 per 1,000 live births, life expectancy rose from 64 to 73. Cassidy says some experts "might quibble" with the account. Note the sequence for the engine: financial deregulation, boom, then a 1982 slump with financial scandals.

## Key Takeaways
1. The Depression reinterpretation removed the main historical argument against laissez-faire. It blames a central bank, not markets.
2. Stagflation, not theory, discredited Keynesian fine-tuning. James Callaghan in 1976: "You cannot now, if you ever could, spend your way out of a recession."
3. By the early 1990s the monetarist and Keynesian camps had blurred. Summers: "the invisible hand is more powerful than the hidden hand."
4. Chile became the template for "shock therapy" in Russia, Poland and other transition economies.

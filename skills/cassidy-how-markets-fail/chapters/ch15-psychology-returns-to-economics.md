# Chapter 15: Psychology Returns to Economics

## Core Idea
Kahneman and Tversky showed that people facing uncertainty use rules of thumb, not expected-utility arithmetic. The best behavioral work shows how "a seemingly minor psychological quirk" can, "in a competitive setting", scale up "into a significant market failure". For credit, the key combined bias is **disaster myopia**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Heuristics and biases** (Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky; article in *Science*, 1974). People "fall back on rules of thumb ('heuristics') and unsubstantiated beliefs ('biases')" in place of the **expected utility hypothesis** of von Neumann and Morgenstern, under which outcomes are weighed by their likelihood.
  - **Representativeness heuristic** ("law of small numbers"): generalizing from insufficient evidence. A small sample is assumed representative. Finance version: people "predict that short-term trends in the market will continue and … underrate the prospects of a major reversal".
  - **Overconfidence**: once convinced that a small sample is representative, people place unwarranted faith in forecasts and ignore **regression to the mean**.
  - **Availability heuristic**: dramatic, salient or personally experienced events are overweighted. Everyday risks are downplayed.
  - **Anchoring**: judging relative to arbitrary reference points. With the status quo as anchor, people assume little will change (the bias of conservatism).
  - **Confirmation bias**: reading any evidence as support for the anchored view.
- **Disaster myopia** (Jack Guttentag and Richard Herring, "Disaster Myopia in International Banking", which the text dates to 1996). In good times businesses and individuals find it increasingly hard to imagine anything very bad.
  - Mechanism in steps: (1) low-probability shocks are rare, so few have experience of them (availability). (2) A good recent run is taken as representative. (3) The assigned probability drifts down as memories fade. (4) It reaches zero, the **threshold heuristic**. (5) Banks "take on too much lending exposure relative to their capital".
  - Important distinction: bankers handle routine, frequent defaults well and reserve for them. They underestimate "a systemic shock that could render many of their lenders simultaneously unable to repay".
  - When to use: to date fragility. The longer since the last crisis, the lower the perceived probability of one.
- **System One and System Two** (Kahneman, 2003 interview): System One is intuitive and automatic. System Two is "conscious, deliberate, slower, serial, effortful" and can follow rules. Effort is the best indicator of which is at work.
- **Hubris hypothesis of corporate takeovers** (Richard Roll): overconfident executives convince themselves that their valuation is right and the market's is wrong. Takeovers "rarely deliver the financial benefits that bidders hope to reap".
- **Status quo bias and defaults** (David Laibson and colleagues). Where employees must opt in to retirement plans, only about half do within a year. With automatic enrolment and an opt-out, some nine in ten stay in. About three quarters accept a default asset allocation.
- **Ambiguity aversion** (identified by Keynes, *A Treatise on Probability*, 1921; popularized by Daniel Ellsberg, 1961): people prefer a gamble with known odds to one with unknown odds, even when their choices are mutually inconsistent.

## Key Concepts
- **Homo economicus**: the rational agent that postwar economics elevated "to a near-sacred principle".
- **The passions and the impartial spectator**: Adam Smith's two realms of decision in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*. Smith also noted the "overweening conceit which the greater part of men have of their abilities".
- **"Our telescopic faculty is defective"**: Pigou on preferring present to future satisfaction.
- **Hot hand**: the belief that a streak will continue. Tversky's 1985 study of the Philadelphia 76ers' 1980-81 season contested it.
- **Mental accounts**: Richard Thaler's observation that people divide spending into separate budgets.
- **Behavioral economics / behavioral finance**: the field that Thaler's "Anomalies" column introduced to the profession from 1987.
- **Neuroeconomics**: brain-imaging studies of decisions. Complex reasoning shows in the prefrontal cortex. Anxiety and excitement show in the limbic region.
- **Pre-commitment device**: a self-imposed constraint, such as a Christmas savings account, that guards against the urge for immediate reward.

## Mental Models
- Follow Cassidy's own position, which he says is close to Laibson's: "People aren't stupid, but they don't necessarily know what they really want or where their best interests lie." This is not a wholesale rejection of rational choice.
- Treat a long benign record as a hazard. It lowers perceived risk, not actual risk.
- Check what the default option is. In practice it is the decision.
- Ask which system a decision engages. A fast, obvious answer may be wrong.

## Anti-patterns
- **Extrapolating a streak**: the representativeness error.
- **Reserving only for observed loss rates**: this covers idiosyncratic defaults and misses the common shock.
- **Assuming a publicized disaster teaches the public**: after Enron, WorldCom and Global Crossing, the pattern of employees holding employer stock by default "was unaffected".

## Worked Example
**Anchoring on a roulette wheel.** Subjects estimated the percentage of United Nations countries that were African. A wheel was spun first. When it landed on 10 the median answer was 25 percent. When it landed on 65 the median answer was 45 percent. "Payoffs for accuracy did not reduce the anchoring effect."

**Base rates: Steve.** Subjects heard that Steve is shy, tidy and fond of detail, then chose his likely job from farmer, salesman, airline pilot, librarian, physician. Farmers and salesmen far outnumber librarians, yet many chose librarian. Telling subjects the true proportions "had virtually no impact".

**Bat and ball.** A bat and a ball cost $1.10 together and the bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. Most people answer $1.00 for the bat. The answer is $1.05.

**Ellsberg decks.** Deck A has ten black and ten red cards. Deck B has twenty cards in unknown proportions. Asked to draw black for $20, most pick A. Asked to draw red, most pick A again, which is inconsistent with any belief about B.

**Disaster myopia in equities.** After the 1981-82 recession it was almost twenty years before another lengthy stock market downturn. Investors "assigned lower and lower probabilities" to one, and eventually zero.

## Key Takeaways
1. Savings evidence: about 50 percent of eligible employees contributed below the employer match threshold even with penalty-free withdrawal. They gave up match payments averaging 1.3 percent of annual pay.
2. Immediate rewards engage the limbic system (2004 *Science* study: a $15 voucher now against $20 later).
3. Salience distorts risk ranking. The text says that death in a road accident is roughly four hundred times more likely than death in a terrorist attack in advanced countries.
4. The remedies the chapter cites are institutional: automatic enrolment defaults and pre-commitment devices.

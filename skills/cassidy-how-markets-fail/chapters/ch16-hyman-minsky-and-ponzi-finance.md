# Chapter 16: Hyman Minsky and Ponzi Finance

## Core Idea
Minsky's **financial instability hypothesis** says that a capitalist economy with a competitive financial sector moves by its own workings from safe finance to reckless finance. Cassidy's three-word summary: "stability is destabilizing". He adds that it "is a theory of rational irrationality, with the individually rational actions of banks and other financial firms serving to destabilize the entire system".

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Financial instability hypothesis** (Hyman Minsky; *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy*, 1986; theorems as he stated them in 1992).
  - **First theorem**: "the economy has financing regimes under which it is stable, and financing regimes in which it is unstable."
  - **Second theorem**: "over periods of prolonged prosperity, the economy transits from financial relations that make for a stable system to financial relations that make for an unstable system."
  - It is endogenous. The process "doesn't depend on any external precipitating event", such as new technology or easier policy, though these reinforce it. "The primary initiative came from the competitive forces at work within the financial sector."
- **Minsky's three financing regimes** (classify each borrower by what its cash flow covers):

| Regime | Cash flow covers | Dependence |
|---|---|---|
| **Hedge finance** | Interest and amortized principal | None. "Hedge" here means taking precautions |
| **Speculative finance** | Interest only | Loans "have to be rolled over at regular intervals" |
| **Ponzi finance** | Not even the interest. Unpaid interest is added to principal (**negative amortization**) | Repayment needs "a new source of income", in practice rising asset prices or a sale |

  - How to use: estimate the share of credit in each regime and watch the drift. A rising speculative and Ponzi share marks the move to an unstable regime.
- **The Minsky cycle** (steps as Cassidy presents them):
  1. Calm follows a past crisis. Debt financing expands, "weak at first because of the memories of preceding financial difficulties".
  2. In prosperity, banks' risk appetite rises just as firms want more credit. Lenders compete, expand existing credit and invent "'new' forms of money".
  3. Easy credit raises investment, stock prices and profits. That raises both the demand for credit and the willingness to supply it.
  4. Lending moves from hedge to speculative to Ponzi finance.
  5. **Minsky moment**: lenders get nervous, call in loans and restrict new ones. Credit becomes scarce "even for financially sound" borrowers.
  6. Shaky borrowers sell assets. This leads to "a collapse of asset values" and "a spiral of declining investment, declining profits, and declining asset prices".
  7. Unless the authorities lend freely, the result may be "a traumatic debt deflation and deep depression".
- **Banks create money**: a bank loan creates money. When banks lend more together, total spending power grows. When they call loans in, it contracts. A banker's contract does not require regard for the effect on the economy, which Cassidy calls "another type of Pigovian spillover".
- **Leverage and marginal lending are "two sides of a coin"** (Minsky): to employ borrowed money, banks "search out marginal customers" and enter riskier areas.
- **Securitization and originate-to-distribute**: pooling loan payments to back a bond. Once loans can be sold, the originating bank no longer bears the default. Paul Davidson (2002): loan officers "become 'loan pushers' and loan traders rather than investigators of the soundness of the borrower's use of loan money".
- **Financialization** (Paul Sweezy and Harry Magdoff, *The Monthly Review*): with slow wage growth and too few investment outlets, debt issuance and new objects of speculation were needed to keep spending growing.

## Key Concepts
- **Tranches**: layers of a collateralized mortgage obligation. Senior tranches have first claim on the cash flows, mezzanine second, and junior what is left.
- **Shadow banking system**: special-purpose vehicles, structured investment vehicles and conduits that bought securitized products "largely beyond the purview of regulators".
- **Commercial paper**: short-term bonds that corporations issue directly, bypassing banks.
- **Spread**: the gap between the rate a bank pays depositors and the rate it charges borrowers.
- **Post-Keynesian**: Minsky's self-description. Mainstream Keynesians treated finance "in a cursory manner".
- **Minsky on uncertainty** (1986): "'I don't know' is often the most appropriate answer to questions relevant to decision-making."

## Mental Models
- Treat a long calm as the cause of the next crisis, not as proof of safety.
- Think of bankers as "merchants of debt who strive to innovate in the assets they acquire and the liabilities they market".
- Use Minsky's boundary rule: admire free markets for "the many details of economic life", and accept that "the domain of effective and desirable free markets is restricted", because financial institutions "are inherently disruptive".
- In real estate, watch for loans with interest deferred until a project is sold. They "impart a Ponzi flavor to the financial structure".

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating banks as passive intermediaries**: the traditional model that Minsky rejected.
- **Deregulating finance like airlines or trucking**: Minsky opposed it, when most economists supported it.
- **Waiting for failures**: Minsky wanted the Fed to "take initiatives to prevent the development of practices conducive to financial instability", favouring stability-enhancing institutions and discouraging others.

## Worked Example
**The leverage arithmetic.** A bank has $100 million of equity and $400 million of deposits paying 3 percent. It keeps a 10 percent capital reserve and lends the rest at 8 percent. It earns $24 million a year. Now it borrows another $500 million at 4 percent, taking total borrowing to $900 million, and lends that at 8 percent too. Profit rises to $40 million. Return on capital employed rises by two-thirds with no new equity. The catch: if some borrowers default, "much of its capital can quickly get wiped out".

**Observed leverage.** Minsky reported that at the end of 1983 some of the largest banks, such as Bank of America and Bankers Trust, borrowed about ninety-seven cents of every dollar they lent and invested. Mellon Bank had debts above 90 percent of assets.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify credit as hedge, speculative or Ponzi. The mix is the fragility measure.
2. Instability is internal to finance. No outside shock is needed.
3. Selling loans removes the originator's reason to check them.
4. Financial sector profits rose from $32.4 billion in 1980 to $195.8 billion in 2000, and from 19 percent to 29 percent of domestically produced profits (Commerce Department figures cited).
5. The central bank must lend to stricken institutions in a panic. Minsky's model case was the Fed's October 1974 handling of Franklin National Bank. He also wanted it to act earlier.

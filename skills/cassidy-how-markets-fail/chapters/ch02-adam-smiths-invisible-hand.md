# Chapter 2: Adam Smith's Invisible Hand

## Core Idea
Adam Smith described the competitive market as a self-correcting system driven by self-interest. He also exempted banking from it. Cassidy's claim: the modern view of financial markets as rational and self-correcting rests "at least partially, on a misreading of the theory of the invisible hand, which Smith had never intended to be applied to finance."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The invisible hand** (Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations*, 1776): each businessman "intends only his own gain" and is "led by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention".
  - Mechanism as Cassidy states it: if demand exceeds the amount offered, the price rises, suppliers make excess profits, and others enter. If supply exceeds demand, the price falls, profits fall, and some suppliers exit. Prices gravitate toward a **"natural price"** at which suppliers cover labor, raw materials and rent plus an unexceptional rate of profit.
- **Negative vs positive feedback** (Cassidy's systems gloss on Smith):
  - **Negative feedback**: an initial disturbance sets off offsetting movements that restore equilibrium. Found in stable dynamic systems such as thermostats. This is Smith's market.
  - **Positive feedback**: amplifies the initial disturbance. Cassidy lists nuclear explosions, rapid population growth, and stock market bubbles.
  - When to use: classify any price mechanism first. Ask whether a price rise reduces demand (negative) or attracts more buying (positive).
  - The adjustments are **emergent** properties: they result from many individual interactions and are not imposed from above.
- **The division of labor** (Smith): specialization raises productivity three ways: greater dexterity, time saved moving between tasks, and the invention of machines.
- **Smith's three duties of government**: (1) defending the nation, (2) administering justice, (3) "erecting and maintaining certain public works and certain public institutions" that no individual would find profitable but that repay "a great society".
- **The party-wall principle for banking** (Smith, book two): regulations stopping banks issuing notes to speculative borrowers violate natural liberty, but "the natural liberty of a few individuals, which might endanger the security of the whole society" ought to be restrained. Smith compares it to the obligation to build party walls to stop fire spreading.
  - When to use: as the classical precedent for financial regulation as a firebreak against contagion.

## Key Concepts
- **Three markets**: goods, labor, financial. Cassidy says economists obscure the differences between them.
- **Laissez-faire**: "leave alone". In the eighteenth century it meant stronger property rights, lower tariffs, and abolishing guilds, local monopolies and feudal privilege.
- **Perfect competition**: later economists' name for Smith's world in which competition cannot be avoided.
- **Consumer sovereignty**: Ludwig von Mises's phrase for the shopper as final beneficiary.
- **"Less eligibility"**: the principle of the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834, which Nassau Senior helped devise. Its aim was to stigmatize idleness.
- **Projectors**: Smith's word for the entrepreneurs who borrowed from the Scottish bank.
- **Classical economists**: David Ricardo, Nassau Senior, John Stuart Mill. Mill's *Principles of Political Economy* (1848): laissez-faire "should be the general practice".

## Mental Models
- Think of utopian economics as "a political philosophy, a secular faith", in Cassidy's words, and not only a scientific doctrine.
- Use the classical economists as a check on modern free-market claims. They were "less dogmatic than many of their twentieth-century followers". J. R. McCulloch said appealing to laissez-faire on all occasions "savours more of the policy of a parrot".
- Treat credit as the classical source of downturns. Mill traced most downturns to the financial system. Alfred Marshall called "reckless inflations of credit" "the chief cause of all economic malaise".

## Anti-patterns
- **Applying the invisible hand to finance**: Smith did not. Cassidy says "the notion of financial markets as rational and self-correcting mechanisms is an invention of the last forty years."
- **Reading Smith as a spokesman for business**: he wrote that people of the same trade seldom meet "but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public".

## Worked Example
**Smith's Scottish bank.** A bank was set up to lend to local projectors on easier terms than existing lenders. It soon had many heavily indebted customers. Smith's verdict: the bank gave "temporary relief" and let the projectors carry on "about two years longer". But it "only enabled them to get so much deeper into debt, so that, when ruin came, it fell so much the heavier both upon them and upon their creditors." The lesson Cassidy draws: easy credit to speculative borrowers delays and enlarges the loss, and Smith wanted it prohibited.

**The pin factory.** One untrained workman could scarcely make one pin a day. Ten workers splitting about eighteen operations made upwards of 48,000 a day. Cassidy's modern equivalent is the iPod: 451 parts, conceived in Silicon Valley, software written in Hyderabad, manufactured in China.

## Key Takeaways
1. The invisible hand is a negative-feedback claim. It fails where feedback is positive.
2. Smith, Mill and Marshall all treated credit and banking as a special case needing restraint.
3. Before the last forty years, most economists shared Charles Mackay's view (1841) of speculative manias as collective insanity.
4. Markets do raise living standards. Cited evidence: China's poverty rate fell from 84 percent to 16 percent between 1981 and 2005, per a World Bank study.

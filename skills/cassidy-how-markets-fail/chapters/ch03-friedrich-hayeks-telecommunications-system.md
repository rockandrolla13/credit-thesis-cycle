# Chapter 3: Friedrich Hayek's Telecommunications System

## Core Idea
Hayek's lasting contribution is the claim that market prices are primarily "a means of collating and conveying information". Cassidy accepts it as the strongest case for markets, and uses it later as the standard against which price failures are judged: if prices are signals, a wrong price sends a wrong signal to everyone.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The price system as a "system of telecommunications"** (Friedrich Hayek; "Economics and Knowledge", 1937; "The Use of Knowledge in Society", 1945).
  - Problem stated: the **"division of knowledge"** problem, which Hayek called "the really central problem of economics as a social science". Knowledge of wants and of cheapest production methods sits in individual minds. It is "not given to anyone in its totality".
  - Mechanism: prices transmit that knowledge. If consumers want more soap than is supplied, its price rises and signals firms to produce more. If demand for tin rises, its price rises, existing users economize, and miners supply more.
  - Key property: **economy of knowledge**. Participants need to know very little to take the right action. "Only the most essential information is passed on and passed on only to those concerned."
  - When to use: to explain why central price-setting fails, and as the baseline for asking what happens when a price is distorted.
- **Hayek's business cycle theory** (*Prices and Production*, 1931): slumps are the inevitable result of prior booms in which growth became "unbalanced", with investment in industrial capacity outstripping the supply of savings. Recessions restore the balance between savings and investment.
  - Status in the text: Keynes called the book "one of the most frightful muddles I have ever read". Most British economists dismissed the theory because it gave little guidance on ending the Depression. Cassidy returns to Hayek's cycle view later in the book.
- **"Planning leads to dictatorship"** (*The Road to Serfdom*): allocation involves disputes between communities, so the planner must decide whose interests matter more, and that needs coercion.
  - Cassidy's verdict: penetrating about fully centralized economies, which have all involved one-party rule. Wrong as a description of the welfare state and the New Deal, which did not touch "the industrial and financial core of the free enterprise system".
- **Capitalism as evolved rules** (*The Fatal Conceit*, 1988): by learning to follow a few rules (exchange goods for money, respect private property, act honestly) man "stumbled upon" an effective coordination method.

## Key Concepts
- **Market socialism**: state ownership of major industries plus a modified price system, with the planner setting some prices. Hayek's objection: without real competition the government cannot know what prices to set.
- **"Nature's cure"**: the traditional view, defended by Lionel Robbins at LSE, that recessions cure themselves and need wage cuts and government retrenchment.
- **Keynes's rival view**: a lack of overall demand causes recessions, and more government spending can restore prosperity.
- **Chicago School**: the group Hayek and Friedman helped create at the University of Chicago.
- **"Liberal"**: Hayek always used it in the nineteenth-century laissez-faire sense.

## Mental Models
- Think of each price as a compressed message. Ask who reads it and what action it triggers.
- Use the Soviet record as the test of administered prices: effective for ramping up basic goods such as steel and wheat, unable to handle varied consumer demand.
- Separate Hayek the information theorist, whom Cassidy credits, from Hayek the alarmist, whom Alvin Hansen accused of "seeing hobgoblins under every bed".

## Anti-patterns
- **Ignoring market failures**: Cassidy says Hayek "neglected to account for some serious failures of the market system". Markets had failed to provide medical care, breathable air and retirement money, and Hayek "didn't even seem particularly interested in the question".
- **Equating the welfare state with totalitarianism**: the central overreach of *The Road to Serfdom*.

## Worked Example
**The moleskin price (Shmelev and Popov, *The Turning Point*, 1990).** Moscow raised the price it paid for moleskins. Hunters supplied more. Distribution centers filled with pelts that industry could not use, and they rotted in warehouses. The Ministry of Light Industry twice asked the price-setting body to lower the price. The question had "not been decided", because its members also had "to keep track of another 24 million prices". Read it as a signal that could not adjust: a single administered price, set once, produced a surplus that no one had authority or time to correct.

## Key Takeaways
1. Prices are information. This is the best argument for markets and the reason distorted prices are so damaging.
2. Hayek's argument is about coordination, not about stability. He lost the macroeconomic debate to Keynes in the 1930s.
3. Hayek's political influence came later: the 1974 Nobel Memorial Prize, then Margaret Thatcher, who said of *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960), "This is what we believe."
4. Hayek's own cycle theory treats booms as the cause of slumps. That sits uneasily with a self-correcting view of markets.

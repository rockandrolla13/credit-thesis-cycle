# Chapter 5: The Mathematics of Bliss

## Core Idea
Arrow and Debreu put the case for competitive markets on a rigorous footing in the early 1950s. The theorists who followed (1953 to 1973) then showed that the same theory gives no guarantee of a unique, reachable or stable equilibrium. Cassidy's conclusion: "The idea that general equilibrium theory amounts to a scientific endorsement of laissez-faire is a product of later popularizers."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **First fundamental theorem of welfare economics** (Kenneth Arrow, paper presented August 1950, published 1951): all competitive equilibria are Pareto-efficient. Proved with convex sets and the separating hyperplane theorem. Cassidy: "a modern mathematical version of Smith's invisible hand argument".
- **Second fundamental theorem of welfare economics** (Arrow, same paper): if the government redistributes resources by taxes and lump sum payments, society can select the Pareto-efficient outcome it prefers and the market will generate the prices to support it. In Arrow's words, any "optimal point can be achieved by a suitable choice of prices under a competitive system".
  - When to use: as the theoretical basis for combining redistribution with markets. It separates the equity decision from the efficiency mechanism.
- **Existence of equilibrium** (Arrow and Gerard Debreu, "Existence of an Equilibrium for a Competitive Economy", presented December 27, 1952). Used convex sets, game theory and fixed-point theorems, borrowing a fixed-point application from John Nash. Predecessors: Abraham Wald (solved Walras's equations with simplifying assumptions) and John von Neumann (1937 Vienna paper, at least one set of nonnegative solutions).
- **Four ways the result fails as a policy guide** (Cassidy's list, from the 1953-1973 literature):
  1. **Economies of scale are assumed absent.** Where they exist (cable television, software), bigger firms undercut smaller ones, a few giants dominate, and prices rise above marginal cost. The outcome is inefficient.
  2. **Non-uniqueness and indeterminacy.** There may be dozens of equilibria. Without extreme assumptions the most one can say is that the number is probably finite. Theory does not say which the economy settles on.
  3. **Time and computation.** Arrow (1953) assumed markets for every commodity at every future date in every contingency. Roy Radner (late 1960s) showed that people would then need infinite computational capacity. The model "breaks down completely in the face of limits on the ability of agents to compute optimal strategies".
  4. **Instability: the Sonnenschein-Mantel-Debreu theorem** (Hugo Sonnenschein, Rolf Mantel, Gerard Debreu, early 1970s). An economy in which all firms and consumers satisfy the Arrow-Debreu conditions can stay near equilibrium, act chaotically, or anything between. Individual rationality plus perfect competition do not determine aggregate behaviour. A doctoral textbook titles the section "Anything Goes".
  - When to use: against any claim that microfoundations guarantee macro stability. "The whole could not be derived from the parts."
- **Arrow's impossibility theorem** (Arrow's doctoral thesis): in general no consistent "social" ordering can be built from individual preference orderings.

## Key Concepts
- **Convex set**: a solid body with no holes or kinks. A straight line between any two points stays inside it.
- **Bliss point**: Frank Ramsey's phrase for the best attainable social outcome.
- **Cowles Commission**: research institute founded by the investor Alfred Cowles in the early 1930s. Based at the University of Chicago from 1936 for twenty years, then at Yale. Research director from 1948: Tjalling Koopmans.
- **Contingent markets**: markets for delivery of a good at a given date in a given state of the world.
- **Chaos**: wild ups and downs of varying length and amplitude. It arises when a rise in demand generates more demand.
- **Economies of scale**: the ability of firms to cut unit costs simply by raising output.

## Mental Models
- Follow Donald G. Saari: "we should be suspicious about models which are always stable", because economics "so effortlessly offers the needed ingredients for chaos".
- Use Arrow's 1986 verdict as the default: "In the aggregate, the hypothesis of rational behavior has in general no implications."
- Note who gives the policy advice. Arrow and colleagues rarely did. When they did, it was usually to advocate government intervention to correct market shortcomings.
- Copycat behaviour gives aggregate demand more structure, but it works against stability.

## Anti-patterns
- **Counting equations and unknowns** (Walras's method): two parallel lines have two equations and two unknowns but no common solution. Solutions could also include negative prices.
- **Textbook "bliss point" diagrams**: Cassidy says few of the theory's creators talked in these terms.
- **Assuming a complete set of futures markets**: many do not exist, and the mental demands are impossible.

## Worked Example
**The Condorcet cycle from Arrow's 1972 Nobel lecture.** A government chooses among X (more defense spending), Y (more health care spending) and Z (tax cuts). One third of voters rank them XYZ. One third rank them YZX. One third rank them ZXY. Then a majority prefers X to Y, a majority prefers Y to Z, and a majority prefers Z to X. No social ranking exists.

**Radner's investor.** A forty-five-year-old wants to hedge a 401(k) portfolio for retirement. Under complete contingent markets the investor needs a list of every possible state of the economy in 2030, a probability for each, and its effect on the stock market. The investor must also model everyone else's differing beliefs and how their actions move the market. Radner's formal result: this needs an infinite amount of computation.

## Key Takeaways
1. The efficiency theorems are conditional. Check for scale economies, missing markets, and bounded computation before relying on them.
2. Equilibrium may be multiple and unstable even when every agent is rational. This is the formal basis of Cassidy's **illusion of stability**.
3. The negative results "haven't made it into the textbooks". According to Duncan Foley, many of the brightest young theorists then left general equilibrium for game theory.
4. The second welfare theorem supports redistribution within a market system.

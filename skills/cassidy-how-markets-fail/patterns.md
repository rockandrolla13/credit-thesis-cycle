# Patterns: How Markets Fail

Analytical techniques from the book. Each is the author's or a named source's. Chapter references show where the detail is.

## Rational irrationality test
**When to use**: an industry or market does something collectively harmful while each participant has a sound private reason.
**How**: (1) List each actor's choices and payoffs. (2) Check for a dominant strategy. (3) Ask what happens to one actor that holds back alone: lost share, weaker stock price, job risk. (4) Check the horizon. Quarterly judgement makes the game one-shot. (5) Count players. More players make restraint harder. (Ch 11, 18, 21)
**Trade-offs**: the same structure is useful when it breaks cartels. Only compulsory, universal rules change the outcome (Conclusion).

## Three-illusions check
**When to use**: someone argues that a market or the economy will regulate itself.
**How**: test for harmony (do individual incentives add up to a good outcome?), stability (is feedback negative or positive?), predictability (does a stable distribution exist?). (Intro, Ch 5, 7)
**Trade-offs**: it is a screen. It does not say what policy to use.

## Market failure taxonomy
**When to use**: before trusting a market price as a signal.
**How**: check Bator's headings: uncertainty, imperfect information, market power and scale economies, public goods, spillovers. Add network effects, hidden information and strategic interaction. (Ch 9, 10, 11, 12)
**Trade-offs**: where brands, warranties and reputation work, Cassidy says leave well alone.

## Coase or Pigou
**When to use**: choosing between private bargaining and public action on a spillover.
**How**: few parties, measurable damage and clear rights point to bargaining. Many parties, long-lived damage or future generations point to a tax, auctioned permits, regulation or public provision. (Ch 9)
**Trade-offs**: government action has costs too. Coase said to compare real governments, not imaginary ones.

## Lemons and moral hazard scan
**When to use**: any credit, insurance or employment relationship.
**How**: ask who knows more before the deal and who can change behaviour after it. Look for screening, collateral, covenants, monitoring and retained risk. If the party that originates risk does not hold it, expect standards to fall. (Ch 12, 18, 19)
**Trade-offs**: every protection (deposit insurance, credit insurance) creates new moral hazard.

## Beauty contest and herd diagnosis
**When to use**: prices have left fundamentals and professionals stay invested.
**How**: look for career risk from deviating, funding risk from leverage and redemptions, and weak private information. Surveys in which insiders call a bubble while holding the asset confirm it. (Ch 13, 14)
**Trade-offs**: riding a bubble can be optimal for one manager. Timing the exit is not reliable.

## Minsky regime classification
**When to use**: judging how fragile a credit boom is.
**How**: classify borrowers as hedge (cash flow covers interest and principal), speculative (interest only, must roll over) or Ponzi (not even interest). Track the share of each, and track interest-only and negative-amortization lending. A long calm, rising leverage and "new forms of money" mark the drift. (Ch 16, 18)
**Trade-offs**: it gives direction, not timing.

## Kindleberger staging
**When to use**: locating a bubble in time.
**How**: name the displacement, then place the market in boom, euphoria, peak or bust. Add Cassidy's three features: complacent policymakers, financial innovation, New Era psychology. (Ch 17, 18)
**Trade-offs**: stages are clear mainly in hindsight.

## Cheap money plus lax oversight
**When to use**: assessing macro-financial policy risk.
**How**: compare the policy rate with a rule-based benchmark and note how long the gap lasts. Check whether leverage and product rules are being loosened at the same time. Watch total and financial-sector debt to GDP. (Ch 17)
**Trade-offs**: either factor alone is less dangerous. Cassidy blames the pairing.

## Demand-response test
**When to use**: deciding whether a market is stable.
**How**: ask whether higher prices reduce demand or raise it. Collateral-based lending, extrapolation and leverage targets all give an upward-sloping demand curve. (Ch 2, 18, 22)
**Trade-offs**: none stated.

## Securitization stress read
**When to use**: evaluating a pooled or tranched credit product.
**How**: (1) Find the common risk factor. (2) Measure the subordinate cushion against a correlated loss. (3) Check who paid for the rating. (4) Check whether the seller keeps a share. (5) Read a higher yield at the same rating as a warning. (Ch 19, 20)
**Trade-offs**: securitization of conforming prime loans worked for decades.

## Risk-model audit
**When to use**: a firm cites value-at-risk or similar numbers.
**How**: ask for the data window, the distribution assumed, the correlations assumed, and what happens to capital when measured risk falls. Ask what fundamental evidence was set beside the number. (Ch 7, 20)
**Trade-offs**: VAR is valid for normal daily conditions.

## Incentive table
**When to use**: explaining a boom or designing reform.
**How**: build three columns: decision-maker, distorted incentive, result. Then trace how the rows reinforce each other. For pay, check asymmetry, time horizon and leverage of the firm. (Ch 21)
**Trade-offs**: it explains behaviour without needing villains, and so may understate fraud.

## Loss spiral arithmetic
**When to use**: estimating forced selling.
**How**: apply a price fall to a balance sheet with a leverage target and mark-to-market. Compute the asset sales needed to restore the target. Repeat for all firms acting together. (Ch 22)
**Trade-offs**: the threshold at which liquidity fails is not known in advance.

## Liquidity or solvency triage
**When to use**: choosing crisis policy.
**How**: if markets reopen once information arrives, lend. If capital is gone, acknowledge the scale, remove bad assets and recapitalize viable banks. Assess firms by interconnection, not size. (Ch 23)
**Trade-offs**: rescues create moral hazard, so they must be followed by tighter rules.

# Chapter 7: Week 4: The Age of Wonder (March 23 to 31, 2020)

## Core Idea
Unlimited QE on Monday March 23 turned the portfolio. In the four trading days from March 20 the month went from a deep intra-month loss back to a record. The author did little trading: he held the plan, added where he had capacity (MXN, TIPS, long-dated oil), and slowly gave up on the one trade that was not coming back, April Eurodollars. The chapter states his split between portfolio risk management and position risk management.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Portfolio risk management versus position risk management**:
  - *Portfolio*: "rigorous rules". When a portfolio loses money "it becomes smaller, and smaller portfolios can sustain smaller positions". Reduction after significant losses is "unavoidable".
  - *Position*: how much you are willing to lose on one trade, usually by stop losses or options. He treats this "as a matter of strategy", not rule.
  - He still has position limits, set by "stress tests and risk parameters to ensure that a given position can't damage the portfolio too much". That makes them portfolio risk management.
  - Verdict: "too many traders focus on position stops, while portfolio risk management is paramount."
- **Against universal stops** (TNPT): stop-losses are "invaluable to some trading strategies and completely detrimental to others". Traders are stopped out of correct positions through "a mismatch between the reasoning behind a trade and the strategy of execution". Managing losses on a single trade "is not a sufficient or even a necessary condition for profitability".
  - His hypothesis on origin: tight stops come from a time when information spread slowly and prices adjusted gradually. Now adjustment is near instant, markets overshoot more often, and "tightstoppers" are whipsawed.
  - What survives of "cut your losers, let winners run": it is "not so much a matter of controlling losses as one of going with the trend". "Certain asset classes tend to trend."
- **Two different reasons to sell the same loser**: (1) forced, to control drawdown under risk limits (March 19); (2) voluntary, because the odds have changed ("the odds of the contract recovering in the few trading days before expiration were bleak"). He marks the second as a different state of mind.
- **Decide when you are winning**: "The easiest time to make good risk-management decisions is when you are making money."
- **Normalization anchor for long-dated assets**: "Sooner or later, one way or another, the world would go back to normal." Current suppression of an activity "had no bearing on where travel would be two years later". Buy the long-dated contract priced off the present shock.
- **Relative value inside a cheap asset class**: pick the issue that is cheap to its own curve. "That in itself would be a source of value."
- **Correlation regime check**: his book assumed he was "the perpetual negative beta guy". In the dark days Treasuries fell with stocks and metals cratered. By March 24 he was rooting for risk assets, and "good news" was "good news" again.

## Key Concepts
- **Unlimited QE (March 23)**: "a comprehensive range of asset classes eligible for purchase".
- **TII 0.75 2/15/42**: the TIPS issue he used, chosen because it "traded very cheap relative to the rest of the TIPS curve".
- **Duration versus notional**: he tracked the P/L per 1 bp, not notional. Premium bonds moving toward negative yields had extended modified duration through convexity.
- **White hole**: his image for central bank liquidity after the singularity.
- **Fastest bear market ever**: completed intraday on March 23, where the S&P 500 bottomed.
- **Throwing in the towel in slow motion**: his description of the EDJ0 exit.

## Mental Models
- When the boat is on course there is no course correction. Sparse chat and little trading mark the best days.
- Think of LIBOR as a lagging, stubborn price: it "missed the memo" and kept rising for days after the funding crisis eased.
- Use yield, not price, to see cheapness in TIPS: higher yield is cheaper. He expected the yields to head "deeply into negative territory".
- Know your exposure in risk units. A wrong notional in your head is embarrassing; a wrong DV01 is dangerous.

## Anti-patterns
- **Position stops as the main risk tool**: whipsaw in fast, overshooting markets.
- **Holding a short-dated loser into expiry on hope**: he exits EDJ0 once the odds are "bleak".
- **Trading on a forecast you cannot evaluate**: a friend who claimed to be psychic named March 23 as the equity bottom. It was. "I didn't trade based on her forecast."
- **Assuming a hedge correlation is permanent**: Treasuries and gold both failed as hedges for about two weeks.

## Key Takeaways
1. Risk rules belong at the portfolio level. Position exits are strategy.
2. Stops fit trending assets. Elsewhere they convert a right view into a loss.
3. After forced reduction ends, re-ask whether the trade still has odds before its deadline. If not, sell it voluntarily.
4. Use recovered capacity first on the cheapest long-horizon assets.
5. Buy long-dated contracts on the normalization view when the spot shock is priced far along the curve.
6. Watch for the day "good news" becomes "good news" again. It marks the correlation regime turning.
7. Low activity is the sign of a portfolio that is working.

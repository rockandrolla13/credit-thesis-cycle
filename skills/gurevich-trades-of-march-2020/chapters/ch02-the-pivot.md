# Chapter 2: The Pivot

## Core Idea
The Fed did not "pivot" in 2019. It followed the response pattern it always follows, and the gap between the last hike and the first cut is short enough that markets are slow to price the cut. The author owned that view through Eurodollar calls, was right on direction, and still lost value on the options when the Fed declared its July to October 2019 easing mini-cycle complete. He then found a cleaner expression: buying March 2020 Fed Funds futures (FFH0) as a "free lunch" on a surprise ease.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Fed's consistent response system**: trade what central banks *will* do, not what they *should* do. For as long as employment statistics ran hot, tightening continued. "When markets got wobbly, the Fed would pause. When a crisis hit, they would ease to add liquidity." He cites Grant Williams: "The Fed always tightens till they break something."
- **Six-step cycle-end script** (his tweet of February 21, 2019; recovered from PDF p. 46): "Every cycle end the Fed policy progresses in the same fashion: 1. We are overheating: hike! 2. Stocks aren't the economy, stay the course. 3. Stocks Are the economy. We're watching the market closely. 4. Numbers are mixed: pause and resume. 5. Just one cut. 6. Low for longer."
  - When to use: to locate the current Fed rhetoric on the path from last hike to easing.
- **Short hike-to-ease interval**: "the average time interval from the last hike in a tightening cycle to the start of the easing cycle is surprisingly short. In the moment, we tend to feel there is merely a pause in the rate hikes. Consequently, the market is slow to price the impending rate cut." (Table below.)
- **Hold-to-expiry rule for options**: "if I bought an option because of where I thought the underlying price would be at expiration, then I hold it to the expiration."
- **Free lunch** (TNPT Chapter 14): "a trade with clear big upside and little to no downside". An opportunity "to profit hugely in the event that something widely regarded as unlikely happens, without paying any price for that option."
- **Fed surprise asymmetry**: "they occasionally do surprise eases but never surprise hikes." The last unexpected tightening was 1994, and the bond market crash that followed taught them not to repeat it. "Tightening policy is never urgent enough to risk disappointing the markets."
  - How to trade it ("fade the tightening"): buy Fed Funds futures when they price a small chance of a hike shortly before a meeting.
  - Rule: "when the market prices something like a 5 percent to 10 percent chance of tightening shortly before the meeting, the real chances are actually zero, and one can buy the corresponding futures contract with impunity."
  - Paradox: "the less money that could be made, the more attractive the trade looked to me, as the probability of surprise fell disproportionately."
  - Why it works: "market risk aversion puts a meaningful premium on the unlikely outcome, where it should be almost none."
- **Positive risk symmetry**: "Regardless of the odds, I try to choose trades that have at least as much upside as downside." He enters a carry trade only if he sees potential for mark-to-market appreciation. This keeps him from becoming a wing seller.
- **Black-swan carry on a Fed Funds future**: over a twenty-three-year career he estimated a black swan at "about 1 percent to 2 percent in any given month". With a resulting 100-bp move, that is worth "at least one extra basis point" on a randomly chosen Fed Funds contract.
- **Accepting the noise**: a trade with a very good return profile and some randomness is acceptable. "Lose a little, or win a little, or win a lot."

## Key Concepts
- **FFH0**: March 2020 Fed Funds future, "the second most fateful contract of my career". Price = 100 minus expected average effective rate for the month. Contract size $5,000,000; each price point is $4,167.
- **EDH0**: March 2020 Eurodollar future.
- **Interest rate floor**: OTC derivative paying when the rate is below the strike at the end of each period.
- **Vega**: sensitivity of option value to a shift in implied volatility.
- **Wing selling**: collecting premium on low-probability outliers while risking massive loss in the worst case.
- **Mid-cycle adjustment**: the 1998 pattern, a few cuts then a stop (and resumed tightening in 1999). His "nightmare" for a long-options book.
- **IOER**: interest on excess reserves. A small signaled increase moved the expected March funding rate up a few bp without a change in target range.
- **Delta-covered call**: an option with its delta hedged, "a pure volatility bet".

## Mental Models
- Think of a "pause" as the usual first stage of an easing cycle. The feeling that it is merely a pause is why the cut is underpriced.
- Use the 60/40 versus 90/10 test before fading an event: near 50/50 the market is probably right and there is no value; at 90/10 on an outcome the institution has no reason to choose, the true odds are near zero.
- Think of wing selling as selling lottery tickets without a casino's diversification: a handful of positions cannot absorb one payout.
- When long options, picture the exact price path that hurts you (the contract freezing just past the strike) and act on it when you foresee it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing the "pivot" story**: traders and pundits "were talking nonsense". The Fed proceeded "exactly as it had in the past".
- **Right on direction, wrong on instrument**: "I was right about the market direction, but I was not going to make money on my options. Had I taken a plain-vanilla directional position instead, my gains would have been substantial."
- **Not acting on a foreseen scenario**: he had envisioned the contract freezing just after going through his strikes and did not exit his vega exposure.
- **Selling out cheap options after the damage**: pointless; they were "already too cheap" and the bid-offer to shed the remaining volatility exposure was "prohibitive".
- **Becoming a wing seller through carry**: a steady revenue stream that "can bring down their entire portfolio" with one payout.

## Worked Example
**Trade A: EDM0 98.375 and 98.25 calls, plus longer-dated rate floors (2018 to 2019). Verdict: right view, flawed exit.**
- March 5, 2019: the 98.375 calls traded at 3¼ ticks (0.0325%), near the lows of fall 2018. The future had stabilized higher, but implied volatility and time decay cut the option price. Consensus was no change by the Fed for the foreseeable future.
- Trigger to add: a call with John Burbank (Passport Capital) in the first week of 2019 on the short hike-to-ease interval. He concluded the options were "insanely cheap" and in the following weeks increased exposure, holding 98.25 and 98.375 calls and longer-dated floors.
- Weaker inflation data in spring 2019 turned perception. The rally carried through "the easing mini-cycle of July 2019 to October 2019". August 2019 stock volatility brought "another big gain". Long gold and short Australian dollar were also working.
- Risk action: with a potential best year in hand and pricing extended in his favor, he cut to "about 50 percent of my maximum risk" going into September, but "kept most of my options exposure".
- The miss: after the October 2019 cut, Powell signaled the mini-cycle was complete. EDM0 converged toward the 98.375 strike and volatility died. The options lost value rapidly.
- His own verdict: the logic for options was "ex ante correct". The error was strategy, "not at all about risk management": he missed the chance to exit vega and simplify. Because of the earlier risk cut, the drawdown in the last four months of 2019 was "very moderate" and the year was still "excellent".

**Trade B: EDH0 98.50 call, bought November 7, 2019, delta covered. Verdict (at this point): regretted within weeks.**
- Thesis: the market priced "maybe 10%" for a December cut. He said 50/50, conditional on a confluence of weaker data and a sell-off in risk assets. He later writes: "The probability estimate was likely incorrect."
- By end of January 2020 (Ch 3) these options traded at 2¼ bp, below his purchase price.

**Trade C: long FFH0, December 6, 2019 to January 17, 2020. The "free lunch".**
- Entry: 98.475 (1.525% yield) against a 1.50%-1.75% target range. No further easing priced, "which in my opinion gave me a free option".
- Path: the contract moved against him to as low as 98.415 (1.585% yield) on the IOER adjustment. "The option was not entirely free."
- Action: he "added to the position, continuously staying on the bid".
- Outcome: stated in Chapters 4 and 8. The chapter closes: "The table was set. The portfolio was ready for a crisis I couldn't possibly anticipate."

## Key Takeaways
1. Model the Fed as a rule: hike while employment is hot, pause when markets wobble, ease in a crisis.
2. In the table, the gap from last hike to first ease ranged from 1 to 18 months. Price the first cut earlier than the "pause" narrative does.
3. Central banks surprise with eases, not hikes. A small priced probability of a hike just before a meeting is premium to collect, and it is not wing selling because the outlier runs in your favor.
4. Require at least as much upside as downside on every trade, including carry.
5. Options can lose money on a correct view. If you foresee the price path that kills the option, exit the vega then.
6. Cutting total risk after a strong run protected the year even though the option strategy erred.
7. Accept small mark-to-market noise in a free-option trade and keep bidding.

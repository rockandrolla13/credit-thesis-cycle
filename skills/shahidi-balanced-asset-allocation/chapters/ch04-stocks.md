# Chapter 4: Viewing Stocks through a Balanced Portfolio Lens

## Core Idea
Investors hold many stocks because stocks have the highest average return. That reason is weak on three counts. Seen through the balanced lens, equities are a rising-growth, falling-inflation asset with high volatility. They belong in the portfolio, at a much lower weight.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Conventional rules for the equity weight** (what the author rejects):
  - 100 minus your age.
  - Emotional risk tolerance: could you hold after a 20% loss?
  - Liquidity: fewer stocks if you may sell within five years.
- **Three major oversights**:
  1. *Long-term underperformance.* "The long run may be too long for you to wait." Stock cycles last 15 to 20 years. Cycle turns are random and include mini-cycles, so you cannot tell that the cycle turned until much later.
  2. *Stocks are only prepackaged to offer higher returns.* Other asset classes have a similar return-to-risk ratio. Leverage, or added cash, can change the package.
  3. *The approach produces imbalanced portfolios.* Every conventional mix is highly correlated to equities.
- **The two-input lens** for any asset class:
  1. *Economic bias*: tells you **when** the asset beats or misses its average.
  2. *Volatility*: tells you **how much** it moves around its average.
  - The author calls these two inputs "far more reliable" than the asset's absolute return over any forward period.
- **Why equities have their bias**:
  - *Rising growth.* More spending raises company revenues. Revenues above the discounted level raise profits and prices.
  - *Falling inflation.* Profit has two parts, revenue and margin. Lower input costs and lower borrowing costs widen margins.
  - Both together is the best environment. The author uses it to explain the 1980s and 1990s.
- **Dominant factor rule**: when growth and inflation send mixed signals, the factor that deviates more from expectations has the greater effect on price.
- **Method for classifying growth and inflation in the data** (note to Table 4.5): compare the current growth or inflation rate with the average of the trailing 12 months. Higher means "rising". The logic given is that most people expect the future to look like the recent trend.

## Key Concepts
- **Return-to-risk ratio**: average excess return divided by average volatility.
- **Secular bear market**: a long period, often a decade or more, of near-zero or negative excess returns.
- **Correlation**: ranges from +100% to -100%. The author treats anything above 80% as very high.
- **Full market cycle**: the common 3 to 7 year idea. The author says the real cycles are 15 to 20 years.
- **Balanced portfolio lens**: view by economic bias and volatility, not by expected return.

## Mental Models
- An asset that earns 0% for 99 years and 1,000% in one year averages 10%. The average is not useful.
- Picking a start date for a stock-heavy portfolio is a coin flip. "Heads you win and tails you lose."
- The two bias boxes matter more than the label "equity". A different asset with the same boxes should be treated the same.
- Even a very conservative investor should hold some equities. Without them the portfolio is exposed to the environments where equities win.

## Anti-patterns
- **Expecting to sidestep bear markets**: investors are "predisposed to jump in, not sell" before them. Signs of a late bull market in the text: widespread overconfidence, heavy risk taking, excess leverage, talk of "this time is different".
- **The business-school correlation error**: assuming a 60/40 mix must be less than 60% correlated to stocks because 40% sits in a low-correlation asset. The actual figure is 99%.
- **Predicting exact volatility**: "Do not attempt precision." Use broad categories.
- **Taking the argument too far**: owning no equities is also imbalance.

## Worked Example
*Rolling 10-year equity returns since 1927.*
- About 20% of all 10-year windows had equity returns below cash. A "one in five chance" of a very bad decade.
- Nearly half of the windows were below the 5.6% average.
- The last windows with negative excess return started in 1998 to 2000 and ended in 2008 to 2010.

*Why the 60 drives 60/40.* The stock side is four times as volatile as the bond side (19.2% against 4.8%). With the larger weight also on stocks, bond returns "don't move around enough" to matter.

*Volatility example.* Two assets share the same bias. One returns plus or minus 30%. The other returns plus or minus 3%. At the same weight, only the first moves the portfolio.

## Key Takeaways
1. Stocks spent 57% of 1927-2013 in secular bear markets by the author's cycle count.
2. Do not buy equities mainly for their return. Buy them to fill the rising-growth and falling-inflation boxes.
3. A "conservative" 30/70 mix is still 88% correlated to stocks.
4. Rely on the cause-effect link from growth and inflation to equity returns. Do not rely on the equity return itself.
5. A balanced portfolio has "no inherent bias to growth and inflation".

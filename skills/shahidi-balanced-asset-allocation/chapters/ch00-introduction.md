# Introduction (with Foreword)

## Core Idea
The most common and costly investor mistake is a poor asset allocation. The fix is a "truly balanced portfolio" that does not depend on forecasting the economy. The book credits most of its concepts to Ray Dalio and Bridgewater Associates.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two ways to make money in markets** (Introduction):
  1. *Trade* investments. This is a zero sum game. Time is not on your side.
  2. *Hold* markets. This is not a zero sum game. Time is your friend.
  - The book covers only the second: "efficient asset allocation".
- **Markets are discounting machines** (Introduction): current prices reflect expectations of the future. To profit from a forecast you must guess the future correctly, and your guess must differ from the majority view that set the price.
- **Diversification over conviction** (Introduction): have "greater confidence in the benefits of diversification than in your investment convictions". Chapter 12 turns this into the "golden rule".
- **The Balanced Portfolio** (Introduction, built in Ch 9 and Ch 10):
  - 20% Equities
  - 20% Commodities
  - 30% Long-Term Treasuries
  - 30% Long-Term TIPS

## Key Concepts
- **Poor balance is normal**: portfolios have been imbalanced for so long that imbalance became the convention.
- **Big four scenarios** (Foreword, Bill Lee): rising growth, falling growth, rising inflation, falling inflation.
- **Risk parity** (Foreword): the foreword calls the concept "pioneered by Bridgewater Associates". Chapter 10 separates the book's method from generic risk parity.
- **Correlation under stress** (Foreword): correlation is "one of the most difficult statistics to rely upon", most of all in market stress. Balancing to the four scenarios replaces reliance on correlation estimates.

## Mental Models
- Treat a strong forecast as a hazard. The author says the greatest losses come when investors are most confident that the loss is nearly impossible, because that is when they "maximize their bets".
- Read the book as logic first and data second. The author says he argues from concepts "rather than by attempting to convince you of the merits by backfilling historical data".

## Anti-patterns
- **Trusting your own market timing**: very few participants show success at picking tops and bottoms, and those who do cannot easily separate skill from luck.
- **Acting on a consensus view**: a correct view that matches the majority is already in the price.

## Key Takeaways
1. The book is about holding markets, not trading them.
2. A forecast pays only when it is right and different from consensus.
3. The target portfolio is 20/20/30/30. The author later calls it an oversimplification meant to teach the concept (Ch 12).

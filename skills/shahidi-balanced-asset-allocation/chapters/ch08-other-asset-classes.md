# Chapter 8: Even More Balance: Introduction to Other Asset Classes

## Core Idea
Any asset class can be placed in the framework with two questions. What is its bias to growth and to inflation, and why? How volatile is it: low, medium or high? This chapter applies the test to global assets, corporate bonds, emerging market bonds, municipal bonds, real estate, private equity and hedge funds.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two-step deconstruction of any asset class**:
  1. Find its bias to shifts in growth and inflation. Know the *rationale*, so you can reuse the logic.
  2. Assign a volatility bucket: low, medium or high.
  - Then make sure each of the four climates is represented with roughly equal impact.
- **Volatility buckets** (Table 8.4 note):

  | Bucket | Volatility range |
  |---|---|
  | Low | 4-6% |
  | Medium | 8-12% |
  | High | 14-20% |

- **Corporate bonds as a hybrid**: structured like Treasuries, biased like equities. "They are technically bonds, they act more like stocks."
  - *Credit spread*: corporate yield minus Treasury yield. It is "effectively the excess return that investors receive for taking risk (minus the expected default rate of these bonds)".
  - Treasuries, not cash, are the base, to match duration.
  - Bias logic: corporate bonds do better as the odds of default fall. Rising growth raises profits. Falling inflation lowers input and borrowing costs, and raises the chance of lower rates.
  - Deflation: corporate bonds "can get absolutely crushed" if inflation nears deflation. Earnings have collapsed and credit takes "a catastrophic hit". Treasuries do well in the same environment.
  - Volatility: between stocks and similar-duration Treasuries. High yield is higher still.
- **Emerging market bonds**: between Treasuries and US corporates. Bias is rising growth and rising inflation, like commodities. The rationale is that these economies sell goods, services and commodities abroad. Check how much of the volatility is currency.
- **Municipal bonds**: high quality acts like Treasuries (falling growth, falling inflation). Lower quality acts like corporate bonds.
- **Commercial real estate**: rising growth. Inflation bias is *mixed*. A real asset with slow supply helps. Leverage and higher interest rates hurt. Lease terms decide the rest.
- **Private equity**: same boxes as public equity. Low measured volatility is a "measurement shortfall".
- **Hedge funds**: as a group, overweight equity risk, like 60/40. Analyze each fund on its own.
- **Equity subsets**: growth, value, large and small share the general equity bias. Differences matter "at the margins". Small caps carry slightly more risk.
- **Global diversification**: one of the easiest enhancements. Global stocks and bonds share the US biases but spread risk across economies.

## Key Concepts
- **Credit spread**: yield above Treasuries for default risk.
- **High yield (junk)**: bonds rated BB and below.
- **REIT**: a public company that manages real estate. It brings equity market risk with the property exposure.
- **Alternative investment**: a label the author says overstates how different private equity is.
- **Measurement shortfall**: low reported volatility that comes from infrequent pricing.

## Mental Models
- To find a bias, ask what makes conditions better for the asset. For a bond with default risk, the answer is whatever lowers default odds.
- The analysis "goes both ways". An equity subset that holds up in weak growth will lag in strong growth, and may not offset losses elsewhere.
- Label the asset by its boxes, not by its legal form. A corporate bond sits with equities.

## Anti-patterns
- **Plugging appraisal volatility into the model**: do not take private equity's low calculated volatility at face value.
- **Counting corporate or high-yield bonds as the bond hedge**: they share the equity boxes.
- **Using REITs as pure real estate**: same drawback as commodity stocks.
- **Assuming hedge funds diversify**: most carry equity-like bias.

## Worked Example
*Classifying corporate bonds from first principles.*
1. What pays the investor? A yield above Treasuries, in return for default risk.
2. What lowers default odds? Stronger growth, through higher profits. So: rising growth.
3. What else helps? Falling inflation, through lower input costs, cheaper borrowing and the prospect of lower rates on a fixed coupon. So: falling inflation.
4. What breaks it? Deflation. The economy has collapsed and earnings have plummeted.
5. Result: same two boxes as equities. Volatility medium to high.

## Key Takeaways
1. Know the *why* behind each bias. New instruments will appear, as TIPS did.
2. Corporate credit belongs on the equity side of the balance sheet of biases.
3. Emerging market bonds are one of the few extra candidates for the rising-inflation box.
4. Adjust illiquid assets' volatility upward before weighting them.
5. Global versions of the four core assets are the simplest upgrade.

# Chapter 6: Why TIPS Are Critical to Maintaining Balance (Despite Their Low Yield)

## Core Idea
Stocks and Treasuries cover both growth outcomes, but both lose when inflation rises. TIPS are biased to rising inflation and falling growth. That is the exact opposite of equities, so the author calls TIPS possibly the most influential asset class in the balanced portfolio.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **How TIPS pay**: a TIPS bond with a 2% yield to maturity returns 2% plus actual CPI inflation if held to maturity. With 3% inflation the return is 5%.
- **Break-even inflation rate**: nominal Treasury yield minus the TIPS yield at the same maturity.
  - Example: 10-year Treasury at 4% and 10-year TIPS at 1% means the market discounts 3% inflation.
  - Rule in the text: expect inflation above break-even, choose TIPS. Expect inflation below it, choose the Treasury.
- **Real yield curve**: TIPS excess returns depend on how *real* cash rates move against the path in the real yield curve.
  - Real yield = nominal yield minus inflation.
  - Example points: one-month nominal rate 1% with 3% inflation gives a real yield of -2%. A 10-year yield of 3% with 3% expected inflation gives 0%.
  - A steep real curve means the market expects real yields to rise fast. If they rise less, TIPS beat real cash.
- **Low real cash, not low TIPS yield, is the oddity**: a low or zero cash yield "simply lowers the hurdle".
- **Two offsetting effects when rates rise from inflation**:
  - Higher cash rates hurt TIPS if the rise exceeds what the curve discounts.
  - Higher inflation helps TIPS.
  - The net can be positive or negative. Treasuries lose either way.
- **Why TIPS have their bias**:
  - *Rising inflation.* Returns are adjusted by actual inflation. "Purest inflation hedge".
  - *Falling growth.* Falling interest rates lift the fixed-rate component.
- **Which boxes are hard to fill** (the scarcity argument):
  - Rising growth: many asset classes.
  - Falling inflation: easy. Both stocks and bonds.
  - Falling growth: "a handful".
  - Rising inflation: "quite limited".
- **TIPS timing mismatch**: TIPS respond to *actual* inflation. The other three asset classes respond to *expected* inflation.
  - Short run: TIPS prices move on growth.
  - Longer run: inflation flows through.
  - Effect: if inflation expectations jump, TIPS lag and the balanced portfolio may underperform "for a short time".
  - Mitigation in the text: hold commodities, which react at once to inflation expectations.

## Key Concepts
- **TIPS**: Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities. US government bonds indexed to CPI. First issued in 1997.
- **Nominal bond**: a bond with no inflation link.
- **Deflation floor**: newly issued TIPS use zero inflation if inflation turns negative.
- **Simulated TIPS series**: Bridgewater's pre-1997 TIPS returns, built from actual Treasury returns, actual inflation and a proprietary method.

## Mental Models
- TIPS are "arguably safer than Treasuries" because they remove inflation risk.
- Inflation does not need to be high for an inflation hedge to pay. It needs to be higher than discounted.
- When TIPS do badly, it is most likely the environment that is best for equities. The text cites 2013: growth beat expectations, inflation fell, TIPS lost and equities soared.
- A two-asset portfolio of equities and TIPS covers all four boxes. The author says it would "probably be more balanced than conventional portfolios". The timing mismatch is the reason to add more assets.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rejecting TIPS for a low yield**: same error as with Treasuries.
- **Holding zero TIPS**: the standard 60/40 has none.
- **Using short-duration TIPS**: the portfolio needs the more volatile version to get enough exposure.
- **Trusting the simulated numbers**: "you should not depend overly on the data, particularly since most of what is represented did not actually happen".
- **Assuming contained inflation continues**: the outcome depends on how much money the Fed prints.

## Worked Example
*Why TIPS can lose to cash with positive inflation.* Excess returns come from the outcome against expectations. If the market discounts high inflation and inflation falls, the excess return can be negative. The author says this can hold "even if there was positive inflation" and "could even be true if there was high inflation".

*Choosing between the Treasury and the TIPS bond.* Treasury yields 4%. TIPS yields 1%. Break-even is 3%. The two cases below apply the chapter's rule with this skill's arithmetic.
- Realized inflation 4%: TIPS return 5% and win.
- Realized inflation 2%: TIPS return 3% and lose.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compute the break-even to see what inflation the market discounts.
2. TIPS cover the two environments the author says are most often underweighted: falling growth and rising inflation.
3. Use long-duration TIPS for the same reason as long Treasuries.
4. Expect TIPS to lag a sudden rise in inflation expectations.
5. Treat the 4.6% average as a concept check, not as a forecast.

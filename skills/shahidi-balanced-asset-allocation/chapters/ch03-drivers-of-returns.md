# Chapter 3: The Fundamental Drivers of Asset Class Returns

## Core Idea
Asset class return equals cash plus an excess return. Three kinds of surprise make the excess return volatile. Only one of them, shifts in the economic environment, can be diversified. The book exists to diversify that one.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Asset Class Volatility Framework** (Figure 3.3):
  - Total return = return of cash + excess return above cash.
  - Cash return is fairly stable. Excess return is highly volatile.
  - Three drivers of excess return volatility:

  | Driver | Diversifiable | Why | Frequency and length |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Shifts in the economic environment (unexpected growth and inflation) | Yes | Hits all asset classes at once but in *different* directions | "Occurs all the time and can persist for long stretches" |
  | Shifts in risk appetite | No | Hits all asset classes in the *same* direction | Infrequent and short |
  | Shifts in expectations of future cash rates | No | Cash is the discount rate for every asset | Infrequent and short |

  - Pay: the investor is compensated for the two undiversifiable risks. The investor is *not* compensated for the diversifiable one. So leaving it in is "foolhardy".
- **How to read what the market expects for cash rates**: look at the Treasury yield curve.
  - Upward slope: the market expects the Fed to raise rates.
  - Inverted: the market expects cuts.
  - Steeper slope, positive or negative: faster expected change.
  - Rule: if cash rates follow the path in the curve, the change does not matter for returns. Only the *unexpected* change matters.
- **Risk appetite mechanics**: return is the sale price minus the purchase price. When risk appetite falls, the price falls until the forward return is high enough to pay for the risk. Greed "follows" good periods and fear comes "after" severe downturns. Emotion is reactive.
- **Economic bias of the four asset classes** (Figure 3.4, restated from the text):

  | Asset class | Growth bias | Inflation bias |
  |---|---|---|
  | Equities | Rising | Falling |
  | Long-term Treasuries | Falling | Falling |
  | Long-term TIPS | Falling | Rising |
  | Commodities | Rising | Rising |

- **All Weather and risk parity**: the chapter says Dalio named the approach All Weather. Other firms that adopted the concepts call their products risk parity.

## Key Concepts
- **Risk premium**: the excess return above cash. The premium for taking risk.
- **Risk-free rate**: the cash rate, set largely by the Fed. It has never been negative in nominal terms in the data. It can be negative in real terms.
- **Rolling returns**: returns over every possible start date for a fixed window. Used to smooth short swings.
- **Discount rate**: the cash yield sets the rate used to value future cash flows of every asset.
- **Return of capital versus return on capital**: what investors care about in a panic versus in normal times.
- **Economic environment**: unexpected changes in growth and inflation.

## Mental Models
- Buying a bond instead of holding cash is the lender's side of the fixed versus floating mortgage choice.
- A bond that yields 6% when cash yields 3% already prices rising rates. Rates rising is not bad for the bond. Rates rising *more than priced* is.
- Risks stack. In a weak economy with falling risk appetite, stocks take a "double whammy". Treasuries take the risk-appetite hit but gain from falling growth.
- "You don't have to know what the future holds, but you should know how different asset classes will react."

## Anti-patterns
- **Over-weighting total return**: 10% from stocks means little when cash paid 8%.
- **Assuming more risk guarantees more return**: "If returns were guaranteed, then these investments would not be risky."
- **Timing risk premiums with cash**: "You don't even know how you are going to feel in the future, so how can you presume to know the collective mind-set of everyone else?"

## Worked Example
*A cash-rate shock.* An investor expects stocks to earn 5% above cash. Cash yields 1%. The expected total return is 6%.
- The Fed suddenly lifts cash to 10%.
- Nobody holds risky stocks for 6% when cash pays 10%.
- The stock price must fall until the forward excess return is back near what was first expected.

*Historical cases named in the chapter.*
- 1994 to 1995: the Fed raised rates by 3 points in one year on inflation fears. All asset classes were hit.
- 2013: the Fed did not move. *Expectations* of faster hikes rose, long rates jumped, and all asset classes fell together.
- 1982 to 1999: equities averaged about 13% excess return a year. Growth beat expectations and inflation fell more than expected.
- 1963 to 1981: rates rose from 4% to 15%. Stocks earned the same as cash for over 18 years.
- 1929 to 1949: depression and deflation. The stock market fell 84% in one 33-month run.

## Key Takeaways
1. Break every return into cash plus excess return first.
2. Ask which of the three drivers moved. Only the economic one differs by asset class.
3. Use the yield curve as the market's forecast of cash rates. Trade only on deviation from it, if at all.
4. An unexpected cash-rate rise and a risk-appetite collapse hurt everything. No mix protects against them.
5. Excess return and volatility are "somewhat reliable" over time. Timing and direction are not.

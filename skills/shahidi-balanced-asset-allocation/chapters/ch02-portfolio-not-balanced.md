# Chapter 2: Your Portfolio Is Not Well Balanced

## Core Idea
A 60/40 portfolio is not balanced. Its results depend almost fully on the stock market. The fault is in the thought process that built it, which looks at returns and ignores volatility and economic bias.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Definition of good balance**: a return stream that is "as steady as possible" over a very long period. The aim is stable returns through all long-term periods, not a return that depends on picking the right entry date.
  - Test: compare Portfolio A and Portfolio B with the same 30-year return. A earns it all in the first 15 years. B earns it evenly. B is balanced.
- **Split total return into cash plus excess return**: judge a portfolio on the excess return above cash. Cash could be held with no risk.
- **Two factors set an asset's impact on a portfolio**:
  1. How volatile the asset class is.
  2. How much of the portfolio is weighted to it.
  - Rule: the more volatile asset should get the *lesser* weight.
- **The flawed conventional thought process**:
  1. Stocks have a higher expected return than bonds.
  2. So a longer horizon means more stocks.
  3. Bonds are there only for stability.
  4. Size the split by age, cash needs and emotional risk tolerance.
  - Result: 30% stocks for conservative investors up to 75% for aggressive ones.
  - What is missing: any thought on how asset class *volatility* shapes the portfolio, and any thought on *economic bias*.
- **The new lens**: think of an asset class "not as something that offers returns" but as something that offers "different exposures to various economic climates". Each class has a bias to economic conditions and a level of volatility.
- **Three fixes for the prepackaged asset class**:
  1. Add leverage to a low-risk, low-return class.
  2. Own more of the low-volatility class (twice as many bonds as stocks).
  3. Lengthen bond duration to raise bond volatility.

## Key Concepts
- **Excess return**: the return above cash. The pay for taking risk.
- **60/40**: 60% stocks and 40% bonds. The portfolio most investors call balanced.
- **Timing risk**: the risk of entering a portfolio at the start of a long bad period.
- **Prepackaged**: the form in which an asset class is normally sold, with a fixed risk level.
- **Economic bias**: the environments in which an asset class tends to beat or miss its average.

## Mental Models
- Half the time you love 60/40 and half the time you hate it. The author calls this "bipolar".
- A good or bad year for bonds sits close to the bond average. So bonds barely move a 60/40 result.
- Risk-to-return ratios are "roughly similar" across asset classes. If they were not, leverage could exploit the gap.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling 60/40 balanced because it holds two different assets**: both stocks and bonds are biased to do well when inflation falls.
- **Judging by total return**: high cash yields flatter the 1970s and 1980s numbers.
- **Assuming 15 years smooths everything**: the long-cycle table shows it does not.

## Worked Example
*Why the 60 drives the 40.*
- Stocks earn 10% and bonds 5%. The 60/40 return is 8%.
- Stocks earn 20% and bonds 0%. The return is 12%.
- Stocks lose 20% and bonds earn 5%. The portfolio loses 10%.
- Headline number: the 60/40 allocation is "99 percent correlated to the stock market". The author says this has held since 1927 over short and long periods.
- *Leverage version.* Suppose stocks offer 6% above cash at 20% volatility and bonds 3% at 10%. Doubling the bond position with leverage gives a similar risk and return to stocks.

## Key Takeaways
1. Measure balance by the stability of long-run excess returns.
2. 60/40 beat cash by 4.1% a year on average since 1929, but it lost to cash in 1965-1982 and in two of the last four decades.
3. 60/40 has 50% more stocks than bonds, and the stocks are far more volatile. The weights point the wrong way.
4. Fix the thought process, not the historical result. Results could have differed. The logic flaw would remain.

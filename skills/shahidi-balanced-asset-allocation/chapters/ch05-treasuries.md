# Chapter 5: The High Value of Low-Yielding Treasuries within the Balanced Portfolio Framework

## Core Idea
A low Treasury yield is not a reason to cut Treasuries. Excess return depends on the path of cash rates against the path priced in the yield curve. Long-term Treasuries cover falling growth, the environment behind the worst equity bear markets. Cutting them, or cutting their duration, makes a portfolio riskier.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Conventional case for underweighting Treasuries** (Figure 5.1, as described in the text): yields are low, so rates must rise, so Treasuries will lose, so cut the allocation or shorten duration.
- **Three major flaws**:
  1. Rates must rise *more than expected* to hurt Treasury *excess* returns.
  2. Long-term Treasuries *can* give large gains over short periods from a low starting yield.
  3. Cutting Treasury exposure *reduces* balance and makes the portfolio *more* risky.
- **Yield curve test for Treasuries versus cash**: ask whether you expect rates to rise faster than what the curve prices.
  - Yes: underweight Treasuries relative to the neutral mix.
  - No: hold more.
  - "The current yield by itself does not matter." A 4% long yield means one thing with cash at 0% (steep curve) and another with cash at 6% (inverted curve).
- **The right question at zero rates**: ask *how long* short rates may stay near zero given the economy. Do not conclude they must rise "just because they are low".
- **Low cash, not low Treasuries, is the oddity**: "low cash yields lower the total returns of all asset classes". Excess returns are unchanged.
- **Reply to "the Fed makes yields artificially low"**: rates are low because the economy is depressed. The chapter says that the first few times Fed purchases stopped after 2009, rates *fell* because the economy weakened. When purchases restarted, rates rose. The author says rates could even be artificially *high*.
- **Constant duration versus hold to maturity**: the book means a constant long-maturity position that is rolled, not a bond held to maturity.
- **"Duration is key"**: lengthen duration "not because they expected interest rates to fall, but to improve the balance in the portfolio".
- **Why Treasuries have their bias**:
  - *Falling growth.* The Fed cuts more than was discounted. Also a flight to quality.
  - *Falling inflation.* It raises the odds of Fed cuts.
  - *Deflation.* Treasuries carry some deflation protection because rates are cut sharply.
- **Hedge by adding, not by cutting**: it is "far better to hedge the interest rate risk by adding asset classes that are biased to do well during environments in which rates rise than to significantly reduce Treasury exposure".

## Key Concepts
- **Flight to quality**: frightened investors move to assets with no credit risk.
- **Disinflation**: prices still rise, but more slowly.
- **Deflation**: prices fall. The chapter treats it as its own case outside the four-box grid.
- **Quantitative easing**: the Fed prints money and buys bonds to lower long rates.
- **Duration**: the source of both the volatility and the diversification value of a bond holding.
- **Efficient, neutral mix**: the balanced starting allocation against which over and underweights are measured.

## Mental Models
- Treasuries are "a longer-duration version of risk-free cash". So their average excess return is low.
- Think of deflation protection as insurance. You rarely need it. Do not overvalue it.
- More volatility is preferred in Treasuries because the returns arrive "when you need them".
- If rates rise from inflation, TIPS and commodities are biased to help. If rates rise from growth, equities and commodities are.
- The consensus is wrong about half the time. It mostly extrapolates the last 6 to 12 months.

## Anti-patterns
- **Replacing Treasuries with lower-quality bonds for yield**: lower quality has a different economic bias. Default risk rises in weak economies.
- **Shortening duration to dodge rate rises**: short bonds "don't move enough to make a difference".
- **Judging inflation cover by current yield**: a yield below expected inflation does not mean total return will be below actual inflation.
- **Trusting the rate consensus**: the three best years for Treasuries came when they were most out of favor.

## Worked Example
*2007 to 2013.* The 10-year yield fell below 4% in late 2007 for the first time since the 1950s. Most investors called yields too low and cut Treasuries or duration.
- Long-term Treasuries then beat cash by 44.1% from October 2007 to December 2013. The S&P 500 beat cash by 36.1%.
- Why: everyone expected rates to rise fast, so the curve priced it. Growth missed the discounted level, and rates fell.

*Three months in 2011.* The 30-year yield was 4.4% on June 30, 2011 and 2.9% three months later. The excess return was 30.9%. The S&P 500 fell 14.1%.

*Out-of-favor years.* Excess returns of +32% in 1982, +39% in 2008 and +35% in 2011.

*Other numbers.* The correlation between starting yield and future excess Treasury return since 1927 is "just 10 percent". US rates sat near zero from 1933 to 1948. Japanese rates approached zero in 1995 and stayed there. In deflation, the chapter says stock prices "collapse an average of 90 percent".

## Key Takeaways
1. Read the whole curve, not the yield level.
2. Falling growth is "perhaps the most significant" environment to cover. The text lists 2008-2009, 2000-2002, 1973-1974 and 1929-1932.
3. In falling growth, long Treasuries averaged 5.5%, close to the 5.6% all-period equity average.
4. Short-duration Treasuries share the bias but not the payoff.
5. There are "very few, if any, true substitutes" for Treasuries within the United States.

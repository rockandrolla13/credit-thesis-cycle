# Chapter 9: How to Build a Balanced Portfolio: Conceptual Framework

## Core Idea
Building a portfolio takes two questions. Which asset classes? How much of each? Pick asset classes so that all four economic environments are covered. Weight them so that exposure, which is weight times volatility, is roughly equal across environments.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three portfolio objectives**:
  1. Steady returns over the long run.
  2. Minimize the risk of major drawdowns (*severity*).
  3. Minimize prolonged underperformance (*longevity*).
- **"Know where neutral is"** (attributed to Ray Dalio): know the portfolio you would hold with no opinion on market direction. This is the strategic asset allocation. Deviate tactically "only if you are smart enough to bet against other market participants".
- **Question one: which asset classes?**
  - Rule: make sure "all the possible environments are covered".
  - Assets that share one bias cannot be balanced "no matter how many of these asset classes you include".
  - The four used: equities (rising growth, falling inflation), long-term Treasuries (falling growth, falling inflation), long-term TIPS (falling growth, rising inflation), commodities (rising growth, rising inflation).
  - Two of the four are biased to outperform in each environment.
- **Why cash is left out**:
  - Over time it does worse than the asset classes.
  - Periods when cash beats everything are rare, short and hard to predict.
  - A constant cash holding "merely lowers expected returns over time".
- **Question two: how to weight?**
  - "Exposure comes from two factors: how much you own and the volatility of what you own."
  - Offsets are measured against each asset's *average excess return*, not against zero. Winners and losers net out "at some positive excess return above cash".
  - Coverage needs both parts. A 5% bond holding covers falling growth in name only.
- **The arrows method** (Figures 9.2 to 9.5, described in the text):
  1. Divide the portfolio into 100 boxes. One box is a 1% position.
  2. Give each asset arrows in its two bias boxes. Three arrows for high volatility, two for medium, one for low.
  3. Add up the arrows in each of the four environments.
  4. Adjust weights until rising growth roughly equals falling growth, and rising inflation roughly equals falling inflation.
- **Two ways to equalize**:
  - Own more of the low-volatility assets and less of the high-volatility ones.
  - Or equalize volatility with leverage. The book does not use leverage. It extends bond duration.
- **Disclaimer on All Weather**: Dalio built All Weather as the permanent mix for his family's inheritance money. Bridgewater launched the strategy in 1996. The author says his framework "is not meant to reflect exactly how Bridgewater actually manages its strategy".

## Key Concepts
- **Neutral allocation**: the mix with no bias to any economic climate.
- **Exposure**: the return impact a portfolio gets when an environment occurs.
- **Unit**: a 1% position in the box diagram.
- **Good and bad environment**: where an asset beats or misses its own average excess return.
- **Efficient, neutral allocation**: the author's name for the truly balanced mix.

## Mental Models
- The key to long-term investing is surviving economic troughs. Anyone makes money when growth is rising.
- "The same environment that causes one asset class to underperform its mean simultaneously helps another outperform."
- You need to know what would *cause* an asset to do well. You do not need to know *when*.
- Two units of a low-volatility asset give about the same exposure as one unit of a medium-volatility asset with the same bias.
- A slightly bad environment for equities means 3.6% instead of 5.6%. It does not mean a loss.

## Anti-patterns
- **Counting holdings as diversification**: ten assets with the same bias are one bet.
- **Holding the right asset in too small a size**: the exposure is not there.
- **Using short bonds to balance stocks**: the weight needed would drag down expected return.
- **Seeking precision**: the method "is clearly not an exact science, but it doesn't have to be".

## Worked Example
*The arrows count for a two-asset portfolio.*
- One unit of equities: three arrows in rising growth and three in falling inflation.
- Add one unit of long-term Treasuries: two arrows in falling growth and two in falling inflation.
- Totals: rising growth 3, falling growth 2, falling inflation 5, rising inflation 0.
- Verdict in the text: not balanced. It does a little better in rising growth. It does "terribly" in rising inflation, because it holds nothing for that climate.

*The 1-year versus 30-year Treasury.* Both have the same bias. Put 30% in the 1-year bond. In falling growth it beats its average, but not by much, because it is not volatile. The same 30% in 30-year bonds gives far more exposure. A smaller weight in the long bond gives the same exposure and frees money for other assets.

## Key Takeaways
1. Select by bias. Weight by volatility.
2. Cover all four environments first. Size comes second and matters just as much.
3. Long duration is the book's substitute for leverage.
4. Leave cash out of the strategic mix.
5. The neutral mix is the base. A tilt away from it is a bet.

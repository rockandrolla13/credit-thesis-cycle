# Chapter 7: Owning Commodities in a Balanced Portfolio

## Core Idea
Commodities look bad alone: low average excess return and high volatility. In a balanced portfolio they are valuable because they pay off in rising inflation and rising growth, and because they are volatile. The author compares them to insurance that you do not have to pay for.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two ways to get commodity exposure**:
  1. *Commodity stocks* (miners, oil producers). Problem: you add stock market risk. The firm may be mismanaged or may hedge the commodity price. The author says these "may not be an ideal fit".
  2. *Commodity futures*, through a diversified index fund. This is the method the book uses.
- **Real asset versus financial asset**: commodities are tangible. Supply cannot be raised quickly by financial engineering. Price moves come mostly from demand shifts, and demand shifts come from the economic cycle.
- **Conventional objections and the author's replies**:

  | Objection | Reply |
  |---|---|
  | Low return and high risk | They outperform "when you really need them to" |
  | No income, so no fair value | "Value is less important than its economic bias" |
  | Futures can diverge from spot | The futures curve is the market's price forecast. Profit depends on the outcome against it. "A nonissue" |

- **Futures curve logic** (parallel to the yield curve):
  - Futures above spot: the market expects the price to rise. You profit only if it rises *more*.
  - Futures below spot (inverted): the market expects a fall. You profit if it falls *less*.
- **Why commodities have their bias**:
  - *Rising inflation.* Commodity prices are part of the CPI calculation directly. They are also input costs for other CPI items.
  - *Rising growth.* More income means more demand. Supply set for the old expectation is short.
- **Volatility as an asset**: commodities are "more valuable to the balanced portfolio *because* they are highly volatile".

## Key Concepts
- **Spot price**: the current price of the commodity.
- **Futures price**: the price agreed today for a future date.
- **Hedgers**: usually producers who want to fix a future sale price.
- **Commodities index fund**: a preset, diversified basket of futures.
- **No-risk commodities**: the author's hypothetical asset with the commodity average return and zero volatility.

## Mental Models
- Every asset class is protection against some environment. "Even stocks are there to protect you against periods of rising growth and falling inflation."
- Commodities are "the true team player".
- Judge an asset by its "contribution to the total mix rather than its own merits".
- High optimism raises futures prices and commodity production. So even a good economy must beat expectations to lift prices.

## Anti-patterns
- **Screening each asset class as a stand-alone investment**: this rejects commodities at once.
- **Using commodity equities as the inflation hedge**: the stock price can fall while the commodity rises.
- **Skipping commodities because fair value cannot be computed**: value screens leave the rising-inflation box empty.

## Worked Example
*Futures arithmetic.*
- Spot oil is $100. The three-month future is $102. Oil rises to $105. The buyer earns $3.
- Same contract. Oil rises to $102. The buyer earns nothing, although oil rose.
- Inverted case. Spot is $100. The three-month future is $98. Oil falls to $99. The buyer earns $1.

*The no-risk commodities test.* Regular commodities averaged 2% excess return with years of +60% and -50%. The hypothetical version earns 0.17% every month. Swap it into the balanced portfolio at the same weight. The portfolio return falls from 4.3% to 4.0%. The author's reason: the strong returns of the volatile version arrive when other parts of the portfolio are weak.

## Key Takeaways
1. Average volatility of a diversified commodity basket since 1927 is about 17%. Average excess return is 2%.
2. Rising inflation occurs about half the time relative to expectations. In those periods commodities averaged 8.4%.
3. Read the futures curve the way Chapter 5 reads the yield curve.
4. Lower volatility would make commodities *less* useful to the portfolio.
5. Coverage of the four environments matters more than the appeal of each part.

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

The author credits most concepts to Ray Dalio and Bridgewater Associates. He says his framework draws on All Weather but "is not meant to reflect exactly how Bridgewater actually manages its strategy" (Ch 9).

### 1. Surprise against expectations drives returns (Introduction, Ch 1, Ch 3)
- Markets are discounting machines. A forecast pays only if it is right *and* different from the majority view that set the price.
- Asset returns depend on growth and inflation relative to what was discounted. A bigger gap gives a bigger price move.
- Each outcome is about fifty-fifty against expectations. Growth beats expectations about half the time. So does inflation.

### 2. The economic machine (Ch 1)
- Spending = money + credit.
- Short-term business cycle: 3 to 7 years, driven by Fed rate moves, *self-correcting*.
- Long-term debt cycle: 50 to 75 years, *self-reinforcing* both up and down. It ends when debt limits are reached.
- Deleveraging: rate cuts stop working, credit contracts, the central bank prints. Printing offsets deflation. It causes inflation only if too much is printed.
- A depression comes from a debt-cycle reversal. A recession comes from Fed tightening.

### 3. Asset Class Volatility Framework (Ch 3)
Total return = cash + excess return. Three surprises move the excess return.

| Driver | Diversifiable | Effect across asset classes |
|---|---|---|
| Economic environment (growth, inflation) | Yes | Different directions |
| Risk appetite | No | Same direction |
| Expected future cash rates | No | Same direction |

The first is frequent and long-lasting, and the investor is not paid for it. The other two are infrequent and short, and the investor is paid for them.

### 4. Each asset class is a package of economic biases (Ch 3 to Ch 8)

| Asset class | Growth | Inflation | Volatility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equities, private equity | Rising | Falling | High |
| Long-term Treasuries, high-quality municipals | Falling | Falling | Medium |
| Long-term TIPS | Falling | Rising | Medium |
| Commodities | Rising | Rising | High |
| Corporate bonds | Rising | Falling | Medium/High |
| Emerging market bonds | Rising | Rising | Medium/High |
| Real estate | Rising | Mixed | Medium/High |
| Hedge funds | Rising or mixed | Falling or mixed | Mixed |

- Bias says **when** an asset beats its average. Volatility says **how much**.
- Corporate bonds "are technically bonds, they act more like stocks" (Ch 8).
- Deflation sits outside the grid. Treasuries hold up. Stocks, credit, commodities and inflation-linked bonds lose (Ch 5, Ch 8).

### 5. Read level against the curve, not level alone (Ch 3, Ch 5, Ch 6, Ch 7)
- Treasuries: "The current yield by itself does not matter." Ask whether rates will rise faster than the yield curve prices.
- TIPS: same test on the real yield curve. Break-even inflation = nominal yield minus TIPS yield.
- Commodities: same test on the futures curve.
- Low cash yields lower the total return of *every* asset class. Excess returns are the focus.

### 6. Why 60/40 is not balanced (Ch 2, Ch 4, Ch 10)
- 60/40 is 99% correlated to equities since 1927. A 30/70 mix is 88%.
- Stocks are about four times as volatile as core bonds and also carry the larger weight.
- Both assets favor falling inflation. Nothing covers rising inflation.
- 60/40 is "a clear bet" on growth above and inflation below expectations (Ch 12).

### 7. Building the balanced portfolio (Ch 9, Ch 10)
- Two questions: which asset classes, and how much of each.
- Select so all four environments are covered. Weight so exposure is equal. Exposure = weight × volatility ("weighted volatility").
- Step 1: balance within each of four economic portfolios. Step 2: balance across them (20% rising growth, 30% falling growth, 25% rising inflation, 25% falling inflation).
- Result: **20% equities, 20% commodities, 30% long-term Treasuries, 30% long-term TIPS**.
- Long duration replaces leverage. Cash is left out.
- "Not exactly risk-parity": balance economic exposure, not asset risk.

### 8. The historical record, 1927 to 2013 (Ch 11)
- Excess return 4.3% against 4.4% for 60/40. Volatility 8.2% against 11.7%.
- Rebalancing adds about 1% a year above the weighted average of components.
- Underperformance against cash over 5 years or more traces to two causes: surprise cash-rate jumps (early 1970s, 1980-1982) and deleveragings (1929-1932, 2008-2009).
- Worst drawdown against cash: -50% in 1929-1932.

### 9. Implementation and the golden rule (Ch 12)
- Neutral mix is the "efficient starting point". Index funds. Rebalance yearly.
- Three ways to try to beat it: tilt, active managers, or both. All are zero sum and need "exceptional skill".
- "Diversification always trumps conviction." Never tilt an asset to zero. Halving is "arguably" too much.
- Hedge by adding offsetting assets, not by removing the one you dislike.

## Trust Notes

- **All 44 images are lost.** Every figure is gone. These tables were images and are also gone: 4.3, 7.1, 10.3, 10.4, 10.6, 11.2, 11.4. ch10 rebuilds 10.3 and 10.4 by arithmetic and labels them as rebuilt. Do not quote them as printed.

- **Foreword.** The catalog page says the foreword is by Ray Dalio. The foreword in this file is signed by Bill Lee, CIO of Kaiser Permanente. Do not attribute it to Dalio.
- **Dated view.** Written in December 2013. Statements about "today" (zero rates, deleveraging, low inflation) describe that date.
- **US only.** All data is US assets. The author notes this in Ch 8.

## Use for Credit and Macro Questions

- **Credit spreads**: gives a definition and a classification, not a valuation model. The spread is the excess return for risk "minus the expected default rate" (Ch 8). Corporate bonds share the equity boxes, rising growth and falling inflation, and are "crushed" near deflation. Table 5.2 shows high yield falling in every equity drawdown of 2007-2013. No spread thresholds.
- **Debt-financed investment booms**: gives the economy-wide long-term debt cycle and its self-reinforcing mechanics (Ch 1). It says nothing on sector booms, corporate capex, or issuance supply.
- **Inflation and Fed regimes**: gives the yield curve as the market's cash-rate forecast, the rule that only unexpected moves matter, break-even inflation, and three dated shock cases: 1980, 1994, 2013 (Ch 3, Ch 5, Ch 6, Ch 11).
- **Issuer screening**: nothing. The book works at asset-class level only.
- **Best use**: as a check on "priced in". State the growth or inflation outcome the market discounts, then ask what happens to each asset class if the outcome differs.

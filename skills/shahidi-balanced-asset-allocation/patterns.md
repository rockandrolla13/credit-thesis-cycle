# Patterns: Balanced Asset Allocation (Shahidi)

## Cash plus excess return split
**When to use**: before judging any asset or portfolio return. (Ch 2, Ch 3)
**How**: (1) Subtract the cash return for the period. (2) Judge only the excess return. (3) Ask which of the three drivers moved it: economic environment, risk appetite, or expected cash rates.
**Trade-offs**: total returns from high-cash eras look worse. That is the point.

## Outcome against the curve
**When to use**: deciding whether a yield, a real yield or a futures price is "too low" or "too high". (Ch 3, Ch 5, Ch 6, Ch 7)
**How**: (1) Read the curve as the market's forecast: yield curve for cash rates, real yield curve for real rates, futures curve for commodity prices. (2) State what path it discounts. (3) Ask whether the outcome will be above or below that path. (4) Ignore the level alone.
**Trade-offs**: the author puts the odds at about fifty-fifty. The pattern tells you what you are betting on, not who wins.

## Two-step deconstruction of an asset class
**When to use**: classifying any asset, including a new instrument. (Ch 8)
**How**: (1) Ask what improves conditions for the asset. Derive its growth bias and its inflation bias, with a reason for each. (2) Assign a volatility bucket: low 4-6%, medium 8-12%, high 14-20%. (3) Raise the bucket for illiquid assets whose measured volatility is low.
**Trade-offs**: coarse. The author says do not attempt precision. Some assets are "mixed" (real estate inflation bias, hedge funds).

## Coverage check
**When to use**: first test of any portfolio. (Ch 9)
**How**: (1) List each holding's two bias boxes. (2) Check that rising growth, falling growth, rising inflation and falling inflation each have at least one holding. (3) If one is empty, no weighting can fix it.
**Trade-offs**: coverage in name is not exposure. Follow with the weighting step.

## Arrows method
**When to use**: a quick visual balance test. (Ch 9)
**How**: (1) One box per 1% of the portfolio. (2) Give each asset three, two or one arrows in each of its bias boxes for high, medium or low volatility. (3) Sum arrows per environment. (4) Shift weights until rising and falling growth match, and rising and falling inflation match.
**Trade-offs**: ignores correlation. Meant for concept, not for final weights.

## Two-step weighted-volatility balancing
**When to use**: setting the neutral weights. (Ch 10)
**How**: (1) Build four economic portfolios of two assets each. (2) Within each, split so weight × volatility is equal for both assets. (3) Sum the weighted volatilities to get each portfolio's total. (4) Across the four, set shares so share × total is equal. (5) For each asset, multiply its within-share by its portfolio's share and add its two entries.
**Trade-offs**: uses rounded volatilities (15% and 10%) and a simple sum. The author says the true total is lower because of correlation. With the four core assets the answer is 20/20/30/30.

## Three-factor portfolio diagnosis
**When to use**: finding the hidden bet in an existing portfolio. (Ch 10, Ch 12)
**How**: (1) For each holding record bias, volatility and weight. (2) Compute weighted volatility per environment. (3) The heaviest environment is the bet. For 60/40 it is rising growth and falling inflation.
**Trade-offs**: tells you under what conditions the portfolio wins or loses. It does not tell you when.

## Hedge by adding
**When to use**: you fear one asset class, for example Treasuries at low yields. (Ch 5, Ch 12)
**How**: (1) Name the environment in which the asset loses. (2) Split it by cause: rates rise from growth, or from inflation. (3) Confirm the portfolio holds assets biased to each cause. (4) Keep the feared asset.
**Trade-offs**: you still take the loss on the feared asset. The offset is elsewhere.

## Tilt from neutral
**When to use**: you hold a view that differs from consensus. (Ch 12)
**How**: (1) Start at the neutral mix. (2) State the view as a growth or inflation surprise. (3) Pick the pair that isolates it. Equities over Treasuries isolates growth, because both are falling-inflation assets. (4) Size small. Never to zero. Halving is "arguably" too large. (5) Implement with index funds.
**Trade-offs**: active risk. Zero sum before costs. If the view matches consensus it has no value.

## Substitute an asset class
**When to use**: you reject an asset, for example commodities over the risk-premium doubt. (Ch 12)
**How**: (1) Name the two environments the asset covers. (2) Find another asset with the same boxes. (3) If none exists, keep the original.
**Trade-offs**: reliable rising-inflation assets are scarce (Ch 6).

## Cause check after a loss
**When to use**: an asset you added has fallen. (Ch 12)
**How**: (1) Ask what happened to growth and inflation against expectations. (2) Ask whether the asset's move fits its bias. (3) If it fits, the asset did its job. Do not sell on the result.
**Trade-offs**: needs a view of what was discounted, which the book reads from curves and recent trend.

## Move along the balance spectrum
**When to use**: a full switch is too different from peers. (Ch 12)
**How**: (1) Place the current mix between a single-asset portfolio and the balanced portfolio. (2) Take one step toward balance. (3) Observe the cause-effect response. (4) Repeat.
**Trade-offs**: slow. Leaves imbalance in place meanwhile.

## Rolling-window stress test
**When to use**: testing a mix against history. (Ch 11)
**How**: (1) Compute excess returns for every start month over 3-month and 1, 3, 5, 7 and 10-year windows. (2) Count the share below cash. (3) List every drawdown of at least 10% with months to recover. (4) Attribute each to a cash-rate shock, a risk-appetite collapse, or an economic shift.
**Trade-offs**: history is one path. The author says to trust the logic over the data.

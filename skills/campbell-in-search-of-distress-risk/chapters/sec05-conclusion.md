# Section 5: Conclusion

## Core Idea
The paper claims two contributions. A reduced-form model predicts bankruptcy and failure better than Shumway (2001), Chava and Jarrow (2004), the Z-score, the O-score and distance to default. Stocks with high failure risk earned anomalously low returns over 1981-2003. The second result challenges the idea that size and value effects proxy for a distress premium.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Reduced-form over score and structure**: "While Altman's Z-score and Ohlson's O-score were seminal early contributions, better measures of bankruptcy risk are available today." Failure risk "cannot be adequately summarized by a measure of distance to default". The authors believe the DD result is robust to other DD measures, though theirs is not identical to Crosbie and Bohn (2001) or Vassalou and Xing (2004).
- **Negative alpha under every leading model**: distressed portfolios have low returns but high standard deviations, market betas and loadings on the small-cap and value factors. They do poorly when implied volatility rises. "From the perspective of any of the leading empirical asset pricing models, these stocks have negative alphas."
- **The wider challenge**: the result is "a challenge to standard models of rational asset pricing in which the structure of the economy is stable and well understood by investors".

## The three candidate explanations, as the conclusion ranks them
1. **Valuation errors**: investors overprice distressed stocks. "This simple story may be correct." Two findings cut against it. Errors are not corrected at earnings announcements. Poor returns are not tied to the "more subtle and less obvious" variables.
2. **Characteristics some investors want**: private benefits of control (no direct evidence) or positive skewness (found, in both concentrated and diversified portfolios).
3. **Unexpected developments in the sample**: more power to debtholders in bankruptcy, or more equity owned by institutions that prefer safe stocks. Distressed stocks did worst when institutional ownership grew fastest. If this is the cause, the effect "is a transitional phenomenon that could disappear or even reverse if institutional ownership stabilizes".

## How the result sits with prior work (Sec 5)
| Study | Distress measure | Finding as the authors report it |
|---|---|---|
| Dichev (1998) | Z-score, O-score | Distressed stocks underperform. Consistent. |
| Garlappi, Shu and Yan (2006) | Moody's KMV | Consistent. |
| Vassalou and Xing (2004) | Distance to default | Some evidence of higher returns. It comes "entirely from small value stocks". |
| Da and Gao (2004) | Critique of the above | Those returns are biased up by one-month reversal and bid-ask bounce. |
| Griffin and Lemmon (2002) | O-score | Distressed growth stocks have particularly low returns. |

The authors' own measure gives underperformance in all size and value quintiles, "more dramatic among small stocks and growth stocks".

## Anti-patterns
- **Using Z-score or O-score as the distress measure by default**: the authors say better measures exist.
- **Assuming investors understand and price a risk because it is real**.
- **Extrapolating the anomaly**: the authors themselves allow that it may reverse.

## Key Takeaways
1. Prefer a reduced-form hazard model with market inputs for failure prediction.
2. Do not assume equity prices pay for failure risk.
3. Keep the sample period in view: returns are measured over 1981-2003 only.

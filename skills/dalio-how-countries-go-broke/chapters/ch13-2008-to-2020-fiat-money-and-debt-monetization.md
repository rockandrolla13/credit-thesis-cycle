# Chapter 13: 2008 to 2020—Fiat Money and Debt Monetization

## Core Idea
Historical case study of Monetary Policy 2. When rates hit 0% in late 2008 the central bank replaced private lenders as the big buyer of debt, for the first time since 1933. MP2 lifted financial assets, favored those who held them and fed populism on both sides. Greece shows the same nine stages for a country that could not print the currency it owed.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Monetary Policy 2 (MP2)**: central banks "buy large quantities of debt and provide credit funded with their balance sheets." The central bank supplies money and credit to the government and the market "to make up for an inadequate amount of private market lending."
  - Trigger: rates at 0% and inadequate free-market demand for debt.
  - Why it can continue: the central bank "doesn't mind having losses" and "doesn't worry about getting squeezed." It "is willing and able to lose lots of money and have a negative net worth."
  - Side effects: higher financial asset prices, lower yields for lender-creditors, cheap money for borrower-debtors, gains concentrated among the rich.
  - Diagnostic: MP2 is "symptomatic of being in the late phase of the long-term debt cycle."
- **QE vs debt monetization** (footnote): in QE the central bank buys bonds from private investors. In debt monetization it buys directly from the government. The difference "normally doesn't make much of a difference, though it can when the banking system is impaired."
- **The nine stages applied to a can't-print case (Greece)**: see Worked Example.
- **From crisis to populism chain**: debt crisis, bank bailouts and asset reflation, perception that the system favors the rich, plus job losses to imports and technology, then populism of the right (Tea Party) and of the left (Occupy Wall Street), then the 2016 election.

## Key Concepts
- **Ugly deleveraging**: debt-to-GDP rises during the adjustment because austerity fails and GDP falls. Greece's debt rose another roughly 70% of GDP this way.
- **Negative net interest margin (central bank)**: arises when the bank bought fixed-rate debt with floating-rate reserves and is then forced to raise short rates by currency flight or inflation.
- **The Troika**: the ECB, the IMF and the European Commission, which paired Greece's restructuring with a bailout.
- **Hollowing out of the middle class**: the combined effect of imports and technology on jobs.
- **Speed of response**: the Fed acted much faster than in the Great Depression, so "the markets and economy rebounded quickly."

## Mental Models
- In MP2 the creditor of last resort has no solvency constraint, so the constraint moves to the currency and to the politics.
- The US "kick[ed] the can down the road": it managed private debt problems while public debt kept rising.
- Each of the two short-term cycles inside 2008-20 needed more debt creation and more monetization than the one before.
- The author reads Trump's first-term shift (aggressive, top-down, nationalistic, protectionist, militaristic) as analogous to the periods before World War I and World War II and to the early 1930s.
- Explicit restructuring is "usually the more painful, drawn-out path." Countries that can print take the devaluation path.

## Anti-patterns
- **Austerity for a debtor that cannot monetize**: in Greece it deepened the depression and "made government finances worse as tax receipts dried up."
- **Treating a currency union as removing risk**: joining the euro made Greek debt "seem much safer (no devaluation risk, backstop from the ECB)" and drew in the capital that built the bubble.
- **Assuming stage 6 always appears**: it did not in Europe, because there was no inflation problem or currency flight to force rates up.

## Worked Example
**Greece through the nine stages.** The author restates each stage and then what happened.

| Stage | What happened in Greece |
|---|---|
| 1. Deep in debt | In the 10 years before 2008 total debt rose about 90 points, from 160% to 250% of GDP. Euro entry made its debt seem safe and capital flowed in |
| 2. Private crisis, government borrows more | Stimulus and larger deficits after 2008. It could not monetize, so the crisis worsened into a deep depression |
| 3. Debt squeeze | Acute public debt crisis in late 2009. The government revealed it had substantially underreported debt and deficits |
| 4. Selling, tightening, weakness | Foreigners sold. Austerity deepened the depression. Short-term rates spiked to over 70% by 2012. Debt rose another roughly 70% of GDP |
| 5. Central bank prints and buys | The ECB expanded its balance sheet as the Fed had. It "wasn't nearly enough" and became politically toxic among creditor countries |
| 6. Central bank loses money | Not seen. The ECB was never forced to raise rates and had no negative net interest margin |
| 7. Restructuring | The Troika's 2012 restructuring cut debt burdens by about 50% of GDP |
| 8. Extraordinary taxes and capital controls | Bank run, new taxes, capital controls in 2015 |
| 9. Return to stable currency | Greece stayed in the euro and recovered over years |

Use this as the template for scoring any sovereign with debt in a currency it cannot print.

## Key Takeaways
1. Zero rates plus weak private demand for debt mark the switch from MP1 to MP2.
2. MP2 raises asset prices and widens wealth gaps. Expect political consequences.
3. For a can't-print debtor, expect default, restructuring and depression in place of devaluation.
4. Stage 6 needs inflation or currency flight. Without either, a central bank can hold a large bond book without loss.
5. Virtually all developed central banks, and many emerging ones, followed the US into MP2.

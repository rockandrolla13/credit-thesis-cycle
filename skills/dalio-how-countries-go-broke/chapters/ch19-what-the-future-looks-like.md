# Chapter 19: What the Future Looks Like to Me

## Core Idea
The author's forward view as of early March 2025, stated as conjecture. The world order is about 90-95% through the Big Cycle that began in 1945 and is in Stage 5. He puts the probability of an unwanted major restructuring or monetization of reserve-currency debt at "something like 65% over the next five years and something like 80% over the next 10 years." Technology will be the strongest positive force ever and still not enough to offset the other forces.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **How the author bets on the future**:
  1. Model timeless and universal cause/effect relationships. Causes precede effects, so better models give earlier reads.
  2. Look for "big, unsustainable conditions" and bet they will not be sustained.
  3. Prefer outcomes more likely than most people think, because they are "not reflected in the price."
  4. Build the logic into back-tested systems. Compare results with expectations. When they differ, diagnose and improve the system.
  5. Assume being wrong often: "at least one-third of the time relative to what the markets are expecting."
  6. Control risk "through diversification of my good risky bets rather than by avoiding risky bets." The "Holy Grail of Investing" is "15 or more great uncorrelated bets."
- **Cycle positioning, March 2025**:
  | Cycle | Position stated |
  |---|---|
  | Overall Big Cycle from 1945 | about 80 years in, about 90-95% through, Stage 5 of six |
  | Short-term debt cycle (the 13th since 1945) | about five years in, about two-thirds through |
  | Closest historical analogues | 1905-14 and 1933-38 |
  | Policy uncertainty | "the maximum point of not knowing" |
- **Stage 5 profile**: countries are "overindebted, inefficiently run, divided, and threatened by other countries." Leaders with "populist, nationalistic, protectionist, militaristic, and autocratic approaches" emerge. The author says all the major powers are there now.
- **The next big red flag**: "significant selling of government debt assets (e.g., bonds) by existing holders," arriving together with heavy new issuance. The central bank must then choose between much higher nominal and real rates or printing to buy long-term debt.
- **The popular-meme test for markets**: there is always a most popular belief, it is in the price, and it is "bound to be wrong in some way." In March 2025 the meme was "American exceptionalism": things are good, AI will improve them and the new administration will fix inefficiencies. His verdict: it "has merit" and is "more than reflected in the prices."
- **Country selection criteria** ("the best places to be"): (1) educate people well so they are skilled and civil, (2) earn more than they spend, with strong income statements and balance sheets, (3) internal order, (4) low risk of international war, (5) low risk of harmful acts of nature, (6) benefit most from technology. "Having great human capital will matter most."
- **Limits-of-power rule**: in a test between the executive and the judiciary, "the judicial branch will lose because the executive branch has much more control over the powers of enforcement."

## Key Concepts
- **New paradigm from March 2022**: the Fed and other G7 central banks moved from giving "money and credit for free" to policy that is slightly tight, then roughly neutral.
- **Debt service lag**: payments trail rate changes because fixed-rate debt reprices only at maturity. Expect debt service to keep rising to catch up with current rates.
- **Government-to-private wealth shift**: government and central bank balance sheets deteriorated so household and corporate ones could improve.
- **Divergence**: unusually large gaps between companies. Over-levered, cash-short, rate-sensitive and bubble companies were hurt. Cash-flush, rate-insensitive and hot-tech companies did well.
- **10 / 30 / 60 split**: the top 1% plus the next 9% "are doing great," the next 30% "so-so," and the bottom 60% "terribly." The author says the bottom 60% on average read below a sixth-grade level and receive more in public assistance than they pay in taxes.
- **Thucydides Trap**: the great power conflict dynamic the author says his US-China gauges show.
- **Friendshoring and onshoring**: supply security now outranks cost efficiency "for the first time since World War II." It is costly and "typically leads to more indebtedness."

## Mental Models
- "He who lives by the crystal ball is destined to eat ground glass." Success comes from "knowing how to deal with what I don't know."
- Rates look "about right" judged by inflation and growth alone. They are "not high enough ... given the fiscal supply-and-demand dynamic."
- Radical monetary changes were made by "much more conventional leaders" than the present one. Roosevelt and Nixon are named.
- "The strong prey on the weak." The author names Europe as "weak and easy prey."
- Alliances can flip. He would not be surprised by US-Russia alignment, China more isolated, or Europe closer to China than to the US.
- "By and large, intellectual property protections don't work." Assume good ideas shown openly are replicated in about six months.
- "Globalization is an unstoppable force." Deal-making is more international than ever even as governments turn nationalist.
- "If you're not worried, you need to worry—and if you're worried, you don't need to worry."

## Anti-patterns
- **Extrapolating the popular meme** without checking what is priced.
- **Taking central bank independence for granted**: "the independence of the central bank should not be taken for granted."
- **Counting on technology alone**: productivity gains "were normally squelched when the other forces of the Big Cycle turned negative."
- **Ignoring second-order effects of rapid cuts**: the author cites school lunch programs.

## Worked Example
The five-force forward scan, as the author runs it:
1. **Debt/money**: the US, other G7 countries and China are over-indebted and rely on MP3. Watch for holders selling government bonds. The UK, France and Brazil already show supply-and-demand problems. Expect debt assets to become "relatively undesirable" versus stores of wealth that hold value when money falls. Expect "big fundamental changes in the monetary system."
2. **Internal order**: an orderly transfer of power, then consolidation by executive order. Expect more fights between branches and levels of government, most won by the president. Polarization measures are at records. Track "how those in the bottom 60% are doing and feeling."
3. **External order**: multilateralism gives way to great power conflict. A "type of world war" is violent locally and not between the US and China. Taiwan could be "a negotiating chip." Expect China to build ties in the Global South.
4. **Nature**: more frequent and costly events. Indebted states will under-prepare and pay afterward. Migration will destabilize politics.
5. **Technology**: the strongest it has ever been. "The days of people making decisions in their own heads are ending." Returns on the investment are unclear.
6. **Net**: four forces worsening, one improving. The improving one does not offset the rest. "The most important factor for the years ahead is that the other forces are managed well."
7. **Action**: "play the probabilities, diversify well, and stick with sound fundamentals."

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat the 65% and 80% figures as the author's conjecture conditional on no fix. He says he is "not fully sure of anything, except death and taxes."
2. Selling by existing holders of government bonds is the trigger to watch.
3. Check what the popular meme has priced before buying what has done well.
4. Neutral, financially strong, orderly countries are doing best: India, Indonesia and most other ASEAN countries, and the Gulf Cooperation Council countries are named.
5. The extreme actions listed in ch08 are "more unlikely possibilities than high probabilities." Stay aware of them.
6. The best policy path in his view is the 3% 3-part solution with a beautiful deleveraging.

# Chapter 8: The Overall Big Cycle

## Core Idea
Five forces interact to produce the Overall Big Cycle, which ends in radical changes to monetary, domestic and world orders. The author calls this the most important chapter and places the present in the late-cycle stage he calls Stage 5, "just before the depression and war stage."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Five Big Forces**:
  1. The debt/credit/money/economic cycle.
  2. The internal order and disorder cycle.
  3. The external geopolitical order and disorder cycle (the changing world order).
  4. Acts of nature (droughts, floods, pandemics).
  5. Human inventiveness, most importantly new technologies.
  - A footnote adds a demographic force: many expensive non-working old people, a shrinking workforce in developed countries, large population growth in less developed ones.
  - When to use: as the top-level scan before any theme. Over 1 to 10 years the short cycles, "especially the debt and political cycles," dominate. Beyond 10 years the long cycles and the productivity trend dominate.
  - How: grade each force as early or late in its cycle. When debt, internal order and external order are all late, "it is typically just before big conflicts and revolutionary changes."
- **Design rule for decision systems**: "Decision-making systems should be based on timeless and universal relationships," explaining all big developments in all time frames and all countries, though not precisely. A failure to explain one "indicates that an important influence is missing and needs to be added."
- **Internal order cycle**: short political swings of about 6 years, give or take 3, add up to shifts in domestic order of about 80 years, give or take 25.
  - Rule for when fights occur: a fight happens when (a) a powerful group wants to change the order and (b) it is unclear which side is stronger. No fight if no such group exists, or if the challenger is overwhelmingly stronger.
  - Leader cycle in democracies: honeymoon (the first 100 days), promises prove hard to deliver, disappointment, bolder critics, then "more extreme actions" to hold power. The outcome "depends mostly on the economy."
- **How democracies become autocracies** (from history and Plato's *Republic*):
  1. Preconditions: large gaps in wealth and values, bad and worsening conditions, weak fragmented leadership.
  2. Compromise breaks down. Sides fight "to win at all costs": hard right, weak middle, hard left.
  3. A demagogue rises, promising easy solutions and using propaganda and bullying. From the right, backed by nobles or capitalists. From the left, backed by the unprivileged masses.
  4. The transfer takes place "within the rules of the democracy" and grows more extreme over about three to five years.
  5. The new leaders change monetary, political and geopolitical orders and typically become "nationalistic, militaristic, expansionist, and autocratic."
  - Examples named: Caesar (49 to 44 BCE), Napoleon (1799 to 1815), Mussolini (1922 to 1943), Hitler (1933 to 1945), Japan's consortium of leaders (1931 to 1945), Franco (1936 to 1975), Erdogan (2016 until now).
- **External order cycle: multilateralism vs unilateralism**: multilateralism worked only "after wars when people were sick of fighting and there was a dominant power to enforce." The author states that "multilateralism is now fading into irrelevance and unilateralism is rising."
- **Extraordinary actions taken under excessive debt** (past cases, including the US):
  1. Great pressure on other countries to buy the country's debt (the British).
  2. Selectively freezing debt or taking assets of "enemy" countries (US to Japan in 1941, Russia more recently).
  3. Defaulting or restructuring by extending maturities and/or monetizing (Germany after Hitler came to power).
  4. Confiscatory taxes and capital/foreign exchange controls.
  5. Revaluing or managing government assets.
  6. Creating new types of money.
  - The author says he is not predicting these. He lists them as possibilities past cases show.

## Key Concepts
- **Order**: a way of operating (monetary, domestic political, geopolitical) that changes when the system breaks down.
- **Stage 5**: the late-cycle stage before depression and war.
- **Losing the mandate of heaven**: the Chinese term (dated in the text to 1046 BCE) for an order losing legitimacy when it no longer meets the needs of a large share of the people.
- **Demagogue**: Plato's term for the leader of the change from democracy to autocracy.
- **Run**: turning in debt assets, which have no intrinsic value, for real money the bank lacks.
- **Peace of Westphalia (1648)**: the agreement the text cites as establishing borders after the Thirty Years' War.

## Mental Models
- Think of the present as "a movie that I have seen many times before," with modern clothes and technology.
- A strong CEO and a demagogue can be hard to tell apart. Ask what controls bind them: a strong board and regulators for a company, oversight functions and separation of powers for a government.
- Alliances change fast when "winning is more important than loyalties."
- "Civility when taken too far creates weak decadence that eventually loses to strong barbarism."
- Technology booms funded by unsustainable credit end in bubbles and busts while still delivering real gains. Cases named: South Sea Bubble (1720), Railway Mania (1830s and 1840s), electricity and utilities bubbles (1870s and 1890s), dot-com and telecoms (1990 to 2001).
- Acts of nature "have killed more people than wars and toppled more orders" than the other forces. With nearly all nations in debt, money for mitigation is short.

## Anti-patterns
- **Analyzing the debt cycle in isolation**: its path depends on the other four forces.
- **Assuming the unimaginable cannot happen**: the shift to unilateralism "is at first shocking, it quickly becomes normalized."
- **Impractical idealism**: the author cites Wilson's League of Nations as a naive design that failed.

## Worked Example
The author's reading of March 2025 conditions, force by force:
1. Debt: "great overindebtedness in the US and in all other major countries."
2. Internal order: "increasingly nationalistic and fragmented internal orders," with political shifts "mostly toward the hard right."
3. External order: "increasingly contentious relationships between countries." Statements on Greenland, Canada and the Panama Canal went from unimaginable to normal within months.
4. Nature: "adverse and expensive acts of nature," most recently the Los Angeles wildfires.
5. Technology: "amazing new technologies," above all artificial intelligence.
Conclusion: three of the cycle forces are late together, which the template places just before large changes in orders.

## Key Takeaways
1. Check all five forces. A model that cannot explain a large event is missing an influence.
2. Late debt cycle plus internal and external conflict is the configuration that precedes order changes.
3. Economic stress drives political change, and the election cycle roughly tracks the economic cycle.
4. Plan for the six extraordinary actions as tail scenarios, not base cases.
5. "The biggest and most important force is how people deal with each other."

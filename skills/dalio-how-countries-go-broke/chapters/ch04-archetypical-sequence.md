# Chapter 4: The Archetypical Sequence

## Core Idea
From 35 major debt crises over the last 100 years in which central governments and/or central banks went broke, the final phase follows nine stages. The list doubles as a risk gauge: the more of the unhealthy conditions present, the higher the probability of a debt/currency crisis. This chapter is short and has no worked example of its own. Chapters 5 to 7 supply the data.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Nine Stages of the Final Crisis**:
  1. The private sector and government get deep in debt.
  2. The private sector suffers a debt crisis, and the central government gets deeper in debt to help it.
  3. The central government experiences a debt squeeze: free-market demand for its debt falls short of supply. Then either (a) fiscal and monetary policy shift to rebalance supply and demand for money and credit, or (b) self-reinforcing net selling produces a debt liquidation crisis. "Big net selling of the debt is the big red flag."
  4. Selling of government debt causes, at once, (a) a free-market tightening of money and credit, (b) a weaker economy, (c) declining reserves, (d) downward pressure on the currency. The central bank then typically eases and the currency devalues. Market signature: rates rising, led by long rates rising faster than short rates, with the currency weakening at the same time.
  5. Rates cannot be lowered (0%, or long rates limit short-rate cuts). The central bank "prints" money and buys bonds. In practice it borrows reserves from commercial banks at a very short-term rate.
  6. Selling continues and rates rise. The central bank loses money because it pays more on liabilities than it earns on bonds. This is notable. It becomes "a big red flag" when the central bank has significant negative net worth and must print to cover negative cash flow. That is the central bank's death spiral and what "going broke" means.
  7. Debts are restructured and devalued, ideally as a beautiful deleveraging.
  8. Extraordinary policies such as extraordinary taxes and capital controls are commonly imposed.
  9. Deleveraging reduces debt burdens and returns the system to equilibrium, often through an inflationary depression, asset sales to raise reserves, and a strictly enforced move to a stable currency linked to a hard currency or hard asset.
  - When to use: as a checklist to stage any sovereign. Stages can occur out of order and with large variations.
  - How: score each stage as present or absent, then watch the stage 3, 4 and 6 flags most closely.
- **Hard money vs fiat money cases**:
  - Hard currency cases: the government promised money it cannot print (gold, silver, or another currency seen as hard, such as the dollar). It "almost always" reneges, and money and debt values "tumble at the moment the promise is broken."
  - Fiat cases: the same over-indebtedness, but the devaluation comes "more gradually."
  - How: classify the case first. It sets the speed and shape of the loss.

## Key Concepts
- **Fiat monetary system**: currency value rests on "the faith and incentives that the central banks provide." Tools: interest rates, debt monetization, tightness of money.
- **Regime oscillation**: Big Debt Cycles move currency regimes back and forth between hard and fiat. Hard regimes break by big devaluation. Fiat regimes break by loss of faith in debt/money as a storehold of wealth.
- **Risk gauge**: the nine-item checklist read as a count of unhealthy conditions.
- **Credibility restoration (stage 9)**: very tight money and very high real rates, "very painful but required for a while," to reward lender-creditors and penalize borrower-debtors.

## Mental Models
- Think of going broke as a heart attack with many possible causes: chronic overspending, costly wars, droughts, floods, pandemics, or a mix.
- Use three questions to predict how painful stage 9 will be: Can the central bank create the currency the debt is in? Are debtors and creditors mainly domestic? Is the currency a widely used reserve currency?
- Treat reserve status as a privilege that gets abused. Governments with it tend to borrow enough to lose it, "which makes their decline more abrupt and painful."

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading central bank losses as an immediate crisis**: losses alone are "notable but not a big red flag." The flag is negative net worth plus printing to cover negative cash flow.
- **Expecting the exact sequence**: the stages "don't necessarily transpire in the exact sequence."

## Key Takeaways
1. Stage the sovereign with the nine-step list before forming a view on its bonds or currency.
2. Bear steepening with a falling currency is the visible mark of stage 4.
3. Fiat devaluations are slow. Japan since 2013 is the author's live example.
4. Domestic-currency, domestically held debt gives policy makers control and makes the adjustment less painful.

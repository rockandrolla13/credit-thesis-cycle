# Chapter 6: The Crisis Spills Over to the Central Bank (Stages 5-6)

## Core Idea
When the government cannot fund itself at a rate it can afford, the central bank buys the debt and the balance sheet hit moves "from the government to the central bank and the holders of the currency." The central bank then faces a bank's own problem: it pays a floating rate on reserves and earns a fixed rate on bonds. Large cash-flow losses funded by printing are the route to a currency spiral.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The author's red-flag ladder for central bank involvement**:
  | Observation | Rating in the text |
  |---|---|
  | Central bank produces a lot of money and credit to buy bonds | "a red flag, but not yet a big red flag" |
  | Sustained rate rises or sustained QE to balance bond supply and demand | "early-stage red flags" |
  | Government shortens debt maturities | "early-stage red flag" |
  | Total debt and central bank holdings of government debt both rise for lack of free-market demand | "early-stage red flag" |
  | Central bank has losses and negative net worth | "a moderate red flag at first", "another red flag" |
  | Significant negative net worth and forced printing to cover negative cash flow | "a big red flag", the death spiral |
  - When to use: to grade how far a fiat sovereign has moved through stages 5 and 6.
  - How: move down the table. The top rows can persist a long time: "it can happen a lot before the supply-and-demand imbalance becomes a problem."
- **How a central bank loses money (mechanics)**:
  1. It buys a bond from a bank and pays with a new deposit (reserve) at the central bank.
  2. It pays interest on that deposit at a very short-term rate.
  3. If the deposit rate rises above the yield on the bonds it holds, cash flow turns negative.
  4. Marked to market, bond losses can exceed capital, giving negative net worth.
  5. Small or moderate losses do not hinder operations. Very large losses create cash needs "that can only be met with a lot of money printing."
- **Central bank "going broke"**: it cannot make debt service payments without printing. It does not default. In large amounts this "devalues the money and creates inflationary recessions or depressions."
- **Hallmarks of stage 6**: (1) losses and negative net worth, (2) forced monetization of its own and others' losses, (3) expanding balance sheet, (4) reserve sales, (5) asset mix shifting from hard assets (gold, FX reserves) to soft assets (claims on government or financials), (6) falling ratio of reserves to money.

## Key Concepts
- **Debt monetization / quantitative easing**: the same act. "It was previously called debt monetization and has this time around been called quantitative easing."
- **Hard vs soft central bank assets**: gold and FX reserves versus claims on the government or financial sector.
- **Recapitalization squeeze**: where the government must put capital into the central bank, it taxes, cuts or borrows to do so, "which adds to the squeeze."
- **Guarantees and backstops**: off-balance-sheet transfers of purchasing power to stressed debtors. Examples named: the Emergency Banking Act of 1933 and the Bank of Amsterdam's backstop of the Dutch East India Company. Both "ultimately required monetization."
- **Propensity to spend printed money**: whether printed money leaves the currency. In the loss cases it was "High" because savers "had been burned before and were still facing low real rates."

## Mental Models
- Treat the central bank as a leveraged bank with a printing press. Its risks match those of commercial banks and investors. The difference is the power to print and "to account for their losses in ways that make them less apparent."
- The stage 6 bind: policy must be easy enough for a weak economy and a fiscally weak government, yet tight enough to keep savers in the currency. The text calls this "a hallmark of an unsustainable situation."
- Pegged currencies start with more backing but hit trouble sooner as reserves to money falls. Fixed-rate cases average close to 50% backed and fall to about one-third backed at devaluation (Description 33).

## Anti-patterns
- **"Central bank losses do not matter"**: true for small losses. "No one cares much, but ... they should."
- **Reading negative central bank equity as the end**: "all these red flags don't signal the end of the Big Debt Cycle—they just show signs of the fading financial health of the system."

## Worked Example
A loss spiral, assembled from the chapter's mechanics and the table:
1. The government faces "financing rates higher than it can afford" and turns to the central bank.
2. The central bank buys bonds with new reserves. Its bond holdings rise as a share of GDP (the archetype chart axis runs 3% to 13% of GDP).
3. Savers start to leave. To hold them, the central bank raises the rate it pays on its liabilities.
4. It "runs up a large interest bill on its liabilities ... but earns little on its assets ... and ends up printing the difference."
5. Cash-flow losses reach roughly 2.6% to 3.5% of GDP a year, the range in all five historical cases.
6. Printed money leaves the currency. The five cases ended with cumulative currency falls of 42% to 100%.

## Key Takeaways
1. Central bank bond buying and maturity shortening typically start "nearly a decade before the crisis and reverse after it."
2. Track central bank cash-flow losses as a percent of GDP. Around 3% a year marked every listed spiral.
3. Check who pays for the losses: printing or fiscal recapitalization.
4. Check reserves to money (M0) against its own 20-year average.
5. Several central banks have negative net equity today without operational harm. Size is what matters.

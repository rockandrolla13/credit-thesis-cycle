# Chapter 12: 1971 to 2008—A Fiat Money, Interest-Rate-Driven Monetary Policy

## Core Idea
Historical case study of Monetary Policy 1. After 1971 the only brake on credit was the interest rate. The 1970s monetized debt and punished creditors. The 1979-82 tightening reversed everything and punished debtors, above all dollar borrowers abroad. Then 27 years of lower highs and lower lows in rates let debt climb until rates hit 0% in 2008 and MP1 ended.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Monetary Policy 1 (MP1)**: fiat currencies managed through interest rate changes. Two differences from MP0: (a) the amount of money and credit is driven mainly by its cost, (b) it is not restrained by a hard link.
  - End condition: rates reach 0%. "That ended the MP1 monetary era."
- **Decade-reversal pattern**: each decade is "more opposite from than similar to" the one before, because the debt, political and geopolitical pendulums reach extremes together and are reversed together.
  - How: at a decade's end, list what the consensus extrapolates, then ask which fundamentals have reached an intolerable extreme.
- **Monetary inflation vs monetary deflation in a dollar squeeze**: countries with debts in a currency they could print saw the currency plunge (inflation). Countries with debts in dollars they could not print defaulted (deflation in the owed currency). Many emerging countries had both at once: an inflationary depression at home and deflationary default on foreign debt.
- **The technology bubble sequence**: invention, excitement, bubble (1995-99), overheating and inflation, central bank tightening, burst (March 2000), short downturn, easing.
- **Bridgewater's zero-rate rule**: "if there was a debt contraction crisis and short-term Treasury and fed funds interest rates nearly hit 0%, we would bet on a bad contraction until the central government and the central banks became very stimulative in the ways they became stimulative in March 1933."
  - Derived from studying 1918 to 1945. The author says it "served us well in 2008."
- **Surplus recycling loop**: an exporter (Japan, then China) earns dollars, places them in reserves and buys Treasuries. The US runs larger deficits "without too much consequence (at least so far)," goods inflation stays low, policy stays easier, stocks rise. Capital owners gain and displaced workers lose.

## Key Concepts
- **Stagflation**: economic stagnation with inflation, the result of unrestrained money and an accommodating Fed after 1971.
- **Eurodollar market**: dollars held abroad, especially in Europe, that "had to be lent out" and went to commodity-producing emerging countries.
- **Lost decade**: the 1980s inflationary depressions and bank workouts in over-indebted emerging countries, ending in 1991 with local debt devalued and foreign debt restructured.
- **Plaza Accord (1985)**: agreement to bring the dollar down. The author says it would have fallen anyway.
- **Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM)**: linked European currencies with separate monetary policies. It broke. The author calls that "one of the great trades of the 1990s."
- **Labor's share**: rose from 68% in 1965 to a US high of 74% in 1980, then was cut under Reagan, Thatcher and Kohl.
- **Average interest rate proxy**: the average of the 3-month bill and 10-year bond rates. Yield curve proxy: 3-month minus 10-year.

## Mental Models
- Fiat money creation is "the easiest and subtlest way for governments to alleviate debt burdens and confiscate wealth."
- A currency can stay the reserve currency while being "such a terrible storehold of wealth." The dollar did in the 1970s.
- "Everything seems bigger up close." Events that felt giant are barely memorable later.
- Great companies are not great investments at any price. "Bubbles are made when there is a lot of thinking in that way and a lot of borrowing."
- Low oil prices from tight money and a strong dollar (about $20 per barrel from 1986 to 1991) hurt the Soviet Union and contributed to its fall. Debt and money conditions change world orders.
- On China and US trade: "Americans got the goods and the Chinese got Americans' debt and I'm still trying to work out who got the better deal."

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying only equity-like assets**: investors who have only seen positive real equity returns assume it always works. The author calls that "a mistake."
- **Ignoring capital flows**: the author's 1982 error (see Worked Example).
- **A currency union of separate monetary policies**: "a system of separate currencies held together with separate monetary policies doesn't work."

**Sequence of regimes inside MP1**

| Period | Regime | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| 1971 to 1982 | Stagflation, two oil shocks (1973, Iran 1979), two short-term debt cycles | Borrower-debtors |
| 1979 to 1982 | Volcker tightening, the highest rates "since the birth of Jesus Christ" (attributed to Helmut Schmidt) | Lender-creditors |
| 1982 to 1990 | Disinflationary growth, high real rates, emerging market debt busts | Lender-creditors at first, shifting to slightly good for borrowers by the early 1990s |
| 1990 to 2000 | Globalization, dot-com bubble, Asian crisis 1997-98, euro and ECB 1999, Clinton surplus | — |
| 2000 to 2008 | Nasdaq down around 80% from March 2000, re-leveraging, mortgage, bank and corporate bubble in 2006-07 | — |

From 1981 to 2008: four short-term debt cycles in 27 years. Every cyclical high and low in rates was lower than the one before.

## Worked Example
The author's 1982 mistake:
1. View: the emerging market debt crisis would cause big problems for American banks, the stock market and the US and world economies.
2. What happened: US markets rallied through the 1980s.
3. Why he was wrong, in his words: he "failed to anticipate how forceful the change in global financial flows away from emerging markets and into American markets would be and how well the Federal Reserve and the regulators would protect the American banks."
4. Lessons he drew: (a) watch capital flows and learn how to measure them, (b) diversify to cut risk without cutting return, (c) be humble.
5. Consequence: "I decided to become an expert on capital flows."

## Key Takeaways
1. In a monetizing decade, own what debtors own and avoid what creditors hold. Bonds lost about 40% real in 1971-81.
2. High real rates with falling inflation and falling nominal rates are close to ideal for stocks and bonds, "a capitalist's delight."
3. A Fed tightening exports the bust to whoever borrowed dollars without dollar income.
4. Bubbles most typically burst when central banks tighten.
5. Falling rates from 1981 to 2008 moved conditions back in favor of borrowers and let debt-to-income rise, setting up MP2.
6. Political swings follow economic pain: left to right around 1980 across the US, UK and Germany.

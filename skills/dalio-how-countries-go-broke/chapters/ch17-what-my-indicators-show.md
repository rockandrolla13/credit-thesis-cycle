# Chapter 17: What My Indicators Show

## Core Idea
A dashboard of government debt, holders, reserves and reserve-currency status across 19 economies, plus four risk gauges for the US: long-term and short-term, for the central government and for the central bank. Reading in March 2025: US central government long-term risk is very high (2.4z, "nearing the point of no return") and short-term risk is very low. US solvency "hinges on maintaining its existing reserve currency status."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Sovereign debt-risk dashboard** (per country):
  1. Government assets versus government debt, % GDP.
  2. Government debt, % GDP, now and projected 10 years forward.
  3. Who holds it: central bank, other domestic players, abroad.
  4. Whether a significant share is in hard (foreign) currency.
  5. Government interest, % of government revenue.
  6. Liquid reserves: FX reserves and sovereign wealth assets, % GDP.
  7. Other health: total debt % GDP, current account 3-year moving average.
  8. Reserve currency status: share of world trade transactions, external debt, global equity market cap and central bank reserves in the country's currency.
  - When to use: to compare sovereigns on long-term default or devaluation risk.
  - How: read debt and interest as the risk, own-currency denomination and reserves as mitigants, reserve status as "a great risk mitigator, especially if the country is a good place to invest."
- **Long-term vs short-term risk**: long-term risk is like the risk of a heart attack. Short-term risk is like the attack happening. Short-term risk can rise without long-term vulnerability (pandemic, war) and "can change very quickly—e.g., overnight."

- **Five bases of the central bank gauge** (footnote): (1) size of the central bank's exposures, (2) balance sheet size and cash-flow sensitivity to rates, (3) balance sheet strength, such as months of reserve sales before running out, (4) the debt/currency as a storehold of wealth, (5) the currency's shares of world reserves, trade, capital flows and capital markets.
- **Two big red flags to watch for in the US**: (1) "another round of quantitative easing to increase liquidity and force real interest rates down" and (2) "the central government gaining control over the central bank."

## Key Concepts
- **Point of no return**: debt and debt service levels that "cannot be reduced without great losses to debt investors" because a death spiral starts.
- **Borrowing need if roll problems**: the deficit plus maturing debt that must be refinanced, as a percent of revenue.
- **Unbacked money**: money stock not covered by reserves, as a percent of GDP.
- **Z-score**: standard deviations above or below the mean.
- **Sovereign wealth assets**: the table counts only the top 20 sovereign wealth funds and excludes pension and quasi-government assets, because their liabilities would have to be added too.

## Mental Models
- An economy "in an excellent equilibrium level" by growth, inflation, real rates and monetization "can create the mistaken impression that all is now good." The government debt supply-and-demand picture "is growing like a cancer."
- Reserve-currency supports "can deteriorate very quickly as they did for prior world powers." The pound and the guilder are the references.
- A debt crisis that raises Fed losses also raises political scrutiny. Lost confidence in independence would feed a negative cycle through the value of money.
- The gauges cover only the debt and financial force. "What we don't know is very large relative to what we do know."

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading low short-term risk as safety**: the US has very high long-term risk at the same time.
- **Counting a state's pension or postal-bank assets as reserves** without their liabilities.
- **Treating the gauges as precise**: they are "very imprecise," like leading indicators of a heart attack.

## Worked Example
The author's reading of three sovereigns from the dashboard:
1. **US**: very large central government debt (risk), low liquid reserves at 3% of GDP (little protection), dominant reserve currency (great mitigant), which the US "is undermining by a number of things it is doing." Verdict: very high long-term risk, very low imminent risk.
2. **Japan**: very large debt (risk), in its own currency (mitigant), relatively large FX reserves at 32% of GDP (mitigant).
3. **China**: relatively big debt (risk), own currency (mitigant), relatively big reserves (mitigant), a currency not widely held as a storehold of wealth (little support), and foreign use of its capital markets "falling fast."
4. **Singapore, Norway, Saudi Arabia**: good income statements and far more liquid assets than debts.

For the Fed: the balance sheet "looks about as vulnerable as it has ever been" because money is large and reserves are 3% of GDP. The income statement is only mildly negative. Net of reserve status the long-term gauge is 1.0z: small now, "in a place where it could accelerate very quickly."

## Key Takeaways
1. Separate the long-term gauge from the short-term gauge. They can point in opposite directions.
2. For the US the roll is the exposure: 239% of revenue to finance if rollovers fail, 254% in ten years.
3. Short-term calm rests on moderate growth and inflation, low credit spreads, adequate real rates and a healthy private sector that can be taxed.
4. A fall in demand for new debt or rollovers, or selling of debt assets, "would quickly raise the short-term risk gauge."
5. Watch for renewed QE and for loss of Fed independence as the two big red flags.

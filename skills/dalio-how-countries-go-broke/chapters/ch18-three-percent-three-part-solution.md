# Chapter 18: My 3% 3-Part Solution

## Core Idea
Cut the US budget deficit to 3% of GDP from the roughly 6% the CBO projects, using three levers: spending cuts, tax increases and lower interest rates. The interest rate is by far the strongest lever, so "the biggest influence on the government's deficit is ironically not Congress ... it is the Federal Reserve." Do it now, while the economy is strong, with monetary policy offsetting the fiscal drag.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 3% 3-part solution**:
  - Target: deficit of 3% of GDP.
  - Three sources: spending cuts, tax increases, interest rate cuts.
  - Balanced version: about a 4% cut in spending, a 4% increase in taxes and a 1% cut in real interest rates, each worth about 1% of GDP.
  - Phase the spending and tax changes in over three years.
  - Without the Fed: about a 6% spending cut and a 6% tax increase, also about 3% of GDP.
  - If the 2017 tax cuts are extended: the deficit is larger by an estimated 1.5% of GDP, so the cut must be over 4% of GDP.
  - Stated effect: debt about 17% lower in 10 years than the current path ($9 trillion) and 31% lower in 20 years ($26 trillion).
- **Top-down budgeting with a fallback**: (1) agree first on the size of the deficit cut needed to stabilize debt, (2) agree on a fallback that triggers automatically if no deal is reached, such as equal percentage cuts to all cuttable spending and equal percentage increases to all raisable taxes, (3) then negotiate anything better.
  - When to use: any negotiation where fights over particulars make failure more likely than an attainable outcome.
- **Relative power of the levers** (direct effects on debt-to-income over 20 years):
  - A 1% fall in interest rates is about four times as effective as a 1% increase in tax revenue.
  - A 1% increase in tax revenue is 1.2x as effective as a 1% cut in spending.
  - Second-order effects widen the gap: rate cuts raise asset prices, capital gains receipts, growth and inflation. Spending cuts and tax increases both reduce activity and income tax.
- **Fiscal tightening with monetary easing**: the two reduce the deficit together and offset each other on growth and inflation. A credible deficit cut "would trigger a rally in bonds and a decline in interest rates."
  - Why it fits now: the main imbalance is between an over-indebted central government and a private sector "in relatively good shape."
- **The deficit-cut principle**: when large, fast-growing government debts need large deficit cuts, (1) cut by enough to rectify the problem, (2) cut when conditions are good so the cuts are counter-cyclical, (3) keep monetary policy stimulative enough to keep the economy strong.
- **Three conditions for a painless fiscal tightening** (from 40 cases since 1960): (1) growth was strong, (2) monetary/currency policy was easy, (3) debts were in a currency the central bank could print.

## Key Concepts
- **Mandatory vs discretionary spending**: the text says about 70% of non-interest spending is "mandatory." Discretionary is roughly 30%, reauthorized yearly, with defense almost half of it. The author finds the distinction "a bit arbitrary."
- **Budget shares including interest** (from the EPUB long-description text, Description 131; image not opened): mandatory 61% ($4.3tn), discretionary 26% ($1.8tn), net interest 13% ($1tn). Social Security $1.6tn, Medicare $1.1tn, Medicaid $666bn, defense $862bn.
- **Primary structural deficit**: the measure used in the 40-case table.
- **Painless vs painful adjustment**: the author's split of the 40 cases into 21 and 19.
- **Other ideas the author favors "if these things can be done well"**: marking government assets to market, a US sovereign wealth fund, and exploring a US-backed stablecoin.

## Mental Models
- Stabilizing debt is like a Rubik's Cube: "changing one lever changes the impact of all the others."
- If fiscal tightening proves too depressing, "the Fed will respond by lowering interest rates because that's what the central bank does."
- Bondholders get a lower real yield but a price gain and "a safer bond."
- Uncertainty argues for more conservatism. "The worst thing possible would be to have its finances in bad shape during difficult times."
- The debt cycle is at the stage where borrowing is needed to service existing debt, so delay compounds.

## Anti-patterns
- **Bottom-up budgeting**: arguing over which cuts and taxes are best before agreeing the total.
- **Single-lever solutions**: 11% more tax, 12% less spending (47% of discretionary), or about 3% lower rates alone. Each "is way too large."
- **Waiting for bad times**: cutting in a crisis is pro-cyclical.
- **Tightening with hard-currency debt into a weak economy without easier financial conditions**: the profile of the painful cases.

## Worked Example
Building the 3% from the three levers:
1. Gap: CBO deficit about 6% of GDP. Target 3%. Need 3% of GDP.
2. Single-lever costs: taxes +11%, or spending −12%, or rates about −3%.
3. Split evenly: spending −4% (about 1% of GDP), taxes +4% (about 1% of GDP), real rates −1% (about 1% of GDP).
4. Check on the grid: income +3% and spending −3% with CBO rates gives 589%. With real rates 1% lower it gives 512%, below today's 583%.
5. Offset: the two fiscal moves depress growth and inflation. The rate cut stimulates. Net effect is intended to be neutral for the economy.
6. Phase in over three years.
7. Precedent: the US went from a 4% of GDP deficit to a 1% surplus in 1993-98, "like cutting the deficit by $1.5 trillion today."
8. If discretionary items alone carried the 4% spending cut, they would need 15% cuts on average.

## Key Takeaways
1. The Fed's rate matters more for the deficit than any vote in Congress.
2. A fiscal tightening of 3% of GDP is "not very large by historical standards." The median case was 5.7%.
3. None of the 21 painless cases had significant hard-currency debt, and 17 of 21 tightened into a strong economy.
4. Bond yields fell 1.2% on average in painless cases and rose 0.6% in painful ones.
5. Two Social Security changes (retirement age 67 to 70, chained inflation) would give about a tenth of the required spending cuts.
6. The author's stated worry is political, not technical: "there is so much arguing over the exact ways to prevent the disaster that it won't be prevented."

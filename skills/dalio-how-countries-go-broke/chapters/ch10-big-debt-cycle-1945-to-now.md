# Chapter 10: A Brief Review of the Big Debt Cycle from 1945 to Now

## Core Idea
The US has spent 80 years in the leveraging-up phase of one Big Debt Cycle and has not yet entered the deleveraging. The chapter gives the indicator set the author watches (real yield, breakeven inflation, yield curve, credit spread, debt service to revenue, monetary base, central bank profit) and the levels he treats as neutral.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Real bond yield as the master indicator**: "the real bond yield is the most important number to watch in the financial world." It is the return one can certainly get free of inflation and default risk, and "the single best indicator of whether it is better to be a borrower-debtor or a lender-creditor."
  - Neutral level: it "has averaged about 2% over the last 100 years," neither too low for debtors nor too high for creditors.
  - How: read large gaps from 2% as excessively cheap or excessively expensive credit.
- **Two-part bond yield**: nominal yield = expected (breakeven) inflation + expected real yield. Watch and trade the two parts separately. Use breakeven inflation as "a naïve but pretty good estimate" when you have no better one.
- **Tight vs easy money matrix**:
  | Real yields | Yield curve | Environment |
  |---|---|---|
  | High | Nearly flat or inverted | Money tight. Good for lender-creditors, bad for borrower-debtors |
  | Low | Relatively positive | Money easy. Good for borrower-debtors, bad for lender-creditors |
- **The author's proposed monetary policy rule**: keep the long-term real rate stable.
  - Real Treasury bond yield around 2%, varying the target by about 1%.
  - Short rate about 1% below the long rate, give or take about 2%.
  - Short rate divided by long rate about 70%, give or take about 50%.
  - Why: fewer extreme swings, so both sides can plan.
- **Three beliefs needed to hold government debt when debt service exceeds revenue**: the government can (1) roll over maturing debt, (2) sell the new debt for the deficit, (3) count on existing holders not selling.
- **Four underpinnings of foreign demand for Treasuries that could weaken**: (a) the US handles its debt and policy irresponsibly, (b) the US threatens to sanction holders by withholding payments, (c) returns from holding the debt are bad, (d) the US loses economic and geopolitical prominence.

## Key Concepts
- **Yield curve**: the 10-year nominal yield versus the nominal short rate, measured both as a difference and as a ratio. Typically short rates are about 1% below and about 70% of long rates.
- **Breakeven inflation rate**: nominal yield minus the real yield on inflation-indexed bonds, the inflation rate the market is betting on.
- **Credit spread**: the chapter charts the Baa corporate spread since 1920.
- **Monetary base to GDP**: the gauge of "big money printing." Two waves: after 1933 and after 2008, each when rates hit 0%.
- **Fed mark-to-market loss**: around $700 billion or 2.5% of GDP if its bonds were marked to market. "A red flag," minor against its funding capacity.

## Mental Models
- "Each decade was more likely to be more opposite than similar to the decade before it," yet at each decade's end markets expected more of the same. Those were the times to bet against the crowd on logically probable surprises.
- Government debt rises when private debt falls. US private debt to GDP peaked in 2008 and declined as the government and central bank stepped in. The text calls this "typical of the beginning of the Big Cycle's late stage."
- From 1945 to 1981 each cyclical high and low in bond yields was higher than the last. From 1981 to 2020 each was lower, until nominal rates neared 0% and real rates were significantly negative.
- From 1980 to 2008 falling rates kept debt service affordable as debt rose. That lever ended at zero.
- Think of debt service at 150% of revenue as owing each year 50% more than you earn. "It's unthinkable."

## Anti-patterns
- **Watching the short-term wiggles**: they "don't even show up in these charts."
- **Extreme, volatile use of interest rates by the central bank**: the author says the Fed "should not be as extreme and volatile as it has been."
- **Assuming foreign buyers will keep buying**: large holders have losses, are over-allocated and some fear being treated as Japan was before World War II.

## Worked Example
How the author built a synthetic US real yield before TIPS existed:
1. Problem: David White of the Rockefeller Foundation had to give away 5% a year and asked for the surest way to fund it.
2. No US inflation-indexed bond market existed.
3. Solution: buy non-US inflation-indexed bonds, hedge the currency, and apply leverage to create a synthetic equivalent of a US inflation-indexed bond.
4. Outcome stated: Bridgewater became the largest global inflation-indexed bond manager, and the author worked on TIPS design with Larry Summers at the Treasury.
5. Use: real yields became both an indicator and a storehold of wealth. He calls inflation-indexed markets "much underappreciated and underused."

## Key Takeaways
1. Start any rates view from the real yield and its distance from 2%.
2. Split every nominal yield into breakeven inflation and real yield.
3. Read the curve as both a spread and a ratio.
4. The US is late in the leveraging phase. The deleveraging has not begun.
5. A big sale of US debt would force either (a) an intolerable rise in rates and a contraction, or (b) large printing and devaluation. It would also call central bank independence into question.
6. Countries with debt in a reserve currency that is not their own would fare "much worse."

# Chapter 5: The Private Sector and Central Government Debt Crisis (Stages 1-4)

## Core Idea
Stages 1 to 4 have measurable markers. Chronic deficits and foreign borrowing build the stock of debt, a private crisis moves it onto the government, private demand for government debt then falls short, and a failed currency defense ends in devaluation. Fixed-rate cases break visibly. Floating cases adjust gradually.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Stage 1 markers: private sector and government get deep in debt**
  - Large and growing government debt from chronic deficits. 31 of the 35 cases had large, persistent deficits going in.
  - Spending shifts toward consumption and the social safety net, away from productivity-enhancing investment. Cutting the safety net becomes "a political third rail (e.g., today in Brazil or the US)."
  - Private borrowing the state must back, and implicit guarantees for banks, "might as well be public sector balance sheet problems."
  - Rising current account and twin deficits "typically lead the crisis by several years."
  - Net international investment position falls. Liquid external assets are very low versus external debt service by the time of devaluation.
- **Stage 2 marker: government debt soars while private debt plunges**, about a year before the crisis. Seen in 15 of the 21 cases with both balance sheets. "When private debt is falling sharply and government debt is rising sharply, it is a short leading indicator of trouble."
  - Government debt also rises relative to (1) revenues, (2) reserves, (3) the quantity of money.
- **Stage 3 markers: the debt squeeze**
  - Debt service at a very high percent of income (the text's example is 100%) is a red flag. Government debt service to revenue accelerated into the crisis in 25 of 35 cases.
  - At devaluation the projected path of debt and interest is "indefinitely increasing."
  - Net selling of the debt is "a big red flag."
- **Eight ways governments paper over a financing gap** (stage 3):
  1. Policy and development banks for off-balance-sheet financing (Japan, Asian financial crises).
  2. Debt guarantees instead of direct spending (Peru 1980s, Turkey 2017 loan guarantee program).
  3. Requiring or heavily incentivizing domestic banks, pensions and insurers to finance the government (Turkey and Brazil recently, US yield-curve management in World War II). "Effectively backdoor monetary financing."
  4. Patriotic campaigns (Turkey dollars-for-lira, WWII bond drives, Korea's 1990s gold campaign).
  5. "Paying" with future cuts and tax increases that may never come (Brazil's spending-limit amendment).
  6. Calling in favors from international creditors or geopolitical financing deals (Turkey recently, the UK's Sterling Area).
  7. Shortening debt maturities.
  8. Capital controls.
- **Stage 4 markers: selling, failed defense, devaluation**
  - Foreign purchases of local bonds plunge while real short rates spike.
  - Issuance maturity shortens. The share of debt maturing within one year peaks at the crisis (Description 20).
  - Growth falls below potential, slack widens, unemployment rises.
  - Inflation rises going in, limiting the room to ease.
  - Hard-currency debtors see sovereign credit spreads rise. Risky assets sell off.
  - Reserves are sold. In the most severe cases reserves are already low versus central bank liabilities.
- **The 19 late-stage flight behaviors**, by actor: see Reference Tables.

## Key Concepts
- **Doom loop**: higher rates weaken the economy and raise borrowing needs, which widens the bond supply-demand gap and raises rates further. The central bank then prints and buys. The text calls that quantitative easing (QE).
- **Twin deficit**: current account plus fiscal deficit (charted as % of GDP).
- **Net IIP**: assets owned abroad minus liabilities owed to the rest of the world.
- **Liquid savings**: reserves and sovereign wealth funds. Watch size, speed of drawdown and distance from zero.
- **Reserve sale as tightening**: selling foreign currency for local currency shrinks the money supply.
- **"Damned if it does, damned if it doesn't"**: once inflation rises in a weak economy, printing brings currency collapse and not printing brings very tight money.

## Mental Models
- Treat a current account deficit as neutral by itself. It reflects capital coming in. It becomes a vulnerability when the country must issue a lot of debt and money quickly.
- Foreign-currency borrowing raises default risk. Borrowing in the country's own currency from foreigners raises devaluation risk.
- In fixed-rate cases high inflation makes the currency more expensive in real terms "right when they need a devaluation." Floating currencies sell off gradually into weakness.
- A central bank quits defending when "the prospect of having no reserves is scarier than the prospect of the currency falling."
- Late in the cycle more debt sits with holders who cannot sell (banks). Liquidity dries up and pushes out large foreign investors. Reserve managers who cannot sell stop adding instead.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading the public balance sheet alone**: guarantees and backstops stay hidden until losses appear, so they "can misleadingly seem 'free'."
- **Defending the currency with rate hikes into a weak economy**: "a classic failed attempt."
- **Treating regulatory zero-risk weights as safety**: they make "a risky instrument seem risk-free."

## Worked Example
The median currency defense, start to finish:
1. Start: reserves are about 5% of GDP, covering about a tenth of the money stock and of government debt.
2. Defense: the central bank tightens and sells reserves for roughly two years (median 23 months).
3. Peak: in the worst six months it spends 49% of the reserves it held at the start of that period.
4. Total: it spends over half of initial reserves (median 62%). The currency generally still falls, and gold gains 42% versus local cash.
5. Capitulation: reserves now back about 6% of the money stock and 3% of government debt. The bank stops.
6. Aftermath: the currency sells off and gold gains another 51% until the currency bottoms.

## Key Takeaways
1. Count the stage 1 to 4 markers. No single one is decisive.
2. Private debt falling fast while government debt rises fast is a short leading indicator.
3. Outflows from foreigners "are classic and tend to lead the devaluation."
4. Watch issuance maturity. Shortening signals stress and raises rollover need.
5. The author states that stages 3 and 4 have not yet happened in the US, Europe, Japan or China. US stage 3 "is moving toward happening."
6. Government interest service in Europe, Japan and China is around half the US level as a percent of GDP. Japan's 215% of GDP debt is exposed to refinancing at higher rates.

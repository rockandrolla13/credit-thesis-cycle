# Chapter 15: China's Big Cycle from 1945-49 Until Now in a Tiny Nutshell

## Core Idea
Historical case study. China rose faster over 50 years than any country in history, built a local-currency debt bubble that burst in 2021, and now sits in the part of the Big Debt Cycle "most analogous to Japan in the 1990s" while locked in a great power conflict with the US. The chapter is narrative and personal. Its one transferable diagnostic is the "pushing on a string" checklist in the appendix.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **China's six-step path under reform** (as the author watched it from 1984):
  1. A classic unproductive communist country.
  2. An effective "socialist market economy."
  3. Development of capital markets and its own version of capitalism.
  4. A classic debt bubble.
  5. A classic debt bust "of the type that those who have their debt denominated in their own currency and have most of the debtors and creditors as their own citizens have."
  6. A classic great power conflict.
- **Five-force scorecard for China** (the template from ch08 applied):
  | Force | Reading in the chapter |
  |---|---|
  | Debt/economic | Debt rose relative to incomes, and relative to liquid assets only from 2009. Local government, corporate and real estate debt became a bubble that burst in 2021. Deleveraging began. Slow to deal with it |
  | Internal order | Tighter controls, more fear, slower decisions, weaker capital and people flows. "About halfway back toward Maoist-Marxist communist policies" |
  | External order | Classic great power conflict with the US, hurting trade, capital and people flows |
  | Acts of nature | COVID from late 2019 through 2022 strained satisfaction with leadership. China also became the most cost-effective producer of solar and wind power |
  | Technology | Mixed: behind the US in the most advanced chips, strong in inexpensive AI, advanced manufacturing and robotics |
  - Summary in the text: four of five forces "have become increasingly threatening to China."
- **"Pushing on a string" diagnostic** (appendix, as of late February 2025):
  1. Nominal government bond rates approaching zero.
  2. Real bond yields about 0.5%: unattractive in normal times, attractive against deflation and falling asset prices, unattractive against US dollar bond rates.
  3. Inverted yield curve, which makes cash relatively attractive.
  4. Liquidity measures (total social financing, money supply, total loans) keep rising "without producing a rebound in real economic activity."
  - When to use: to test whether monetary easing is still transmitting in a late-cycle, own-currency debtor.
  - Implication stated: "other 'non-conventional' fiscal and monetary policies will likely have to be used."
- **Conditions for a manageable deleveraging**: debt mostly in local currency and debtors and creditors mostly domestic. China has both. Its handicap: the renminbi "is not a widely held reserve currency, so it isn't an effective storehold of wealth."

## Key Concepts
- **Century of Humiliation**: the period from the First Opium War in 1839 to 1949. The chapter calls it both a century and a "110-year period."
- **One China policy**: the 1971 UN General Assembly recognition of the People's Republic as "the only legitimate representative of China."
- **Reform and open door**: Deng Xiaoping's policies from 1978. "It doesn't matter whether a cat is black or white as long as it catches mice."
- **Common prosperity**: the goal Deng deferred and Xi re-emphasized in 2021.
- **Core leadership**: the consolidation of power around Xi late in his first term.
- **Window company**: CITIC, the only firm allowed to deal with the outside world in a capitalist way in 1984.
- **Da tong** (footnote): "big/grand unity," with Confucianism (around 500 BCE) and legalism (around 250 BCE) as the governing traditions.
- **LGFVs**: grouped with non-financial businesses in the appendix debt chart (Description 91). The text does not expand the acronym.

## Mental Models
- "The enemy of my enemy is my friend." The Soviet threat brought the US and China together in 1972. A common view of the US as a threat later aligned Russia and China.
- Those who win a civil war suppress the opposition to consolidate power.
- Chinese leaders know the Big Cycle from their own long history. Xi's phrase is "a big 100-year storm on the horizon."
- Western war is head-on, "Mediterranean." Chinese conflict is subtler and deceptive, in the manner of Sun Tzu's *The Art of War*.
- Chinese policy swings widely "as a way of creating fear, cleaning things out, and then rebuilding."
- A deleveraging done badly can "just shift the debt burdens, worsen the long-term central government debt burdens, and/or so severely undermine the value of the currency as to do great damage."

## Anti-patterns
- **Rigid communist economics in isolation**: under Mao, policies "didn't reward hard work and didn't allow savings and wealth creation."
- **Delay in recognizing losses**: the author judges China "has been slow to deal with" its debt, the mistake Japan made.
- **Arbitrary action against property rights**: the 2021 measures "were not consistent with the type of rule of law and traditional property protections that investors thought were important."

## Worked Example
The author's parallel reading of China and the US in March 2025, force by force:

| Force | China | United States |
|---|---|---|
| Debt | A big debt crisis while turning to more traditional communist policies | "The big debt issue" |
| Internal | Conflict removed by stricter autocratic policies | "More strict, semi-autocratic policies to overpower the opposition" |
| External | Growing role in reshaping the world order | From global leader to "America First" nationalist participant |
| Nature | Climate change likely to have a big effect | The same |
| Technology | A technology war it cannot afford to lose | The same |

His conclusion: the squaring-off "looks a lot like what we saw in the 1930s," and "is shaping up to be the greatest great power conflict ever." It has not become a military confrontation.

## Key Takeaways
1. China has the two conditions for a beautiful deleveraging. Whether it uses them is not yet known.
2. Rising liquidity without rising activity, an inverted curve and near-zero rates together signal that rate policy has stopped working.
3. China's debts are reaching new highs while the economy is weaker than desired, as in Japan.
4. Sectors free of debt are highly competitive. The debt problem and the technology strength coexist.
5. Taiwan combines the sovereignty dispute with control of most advanced chip production.

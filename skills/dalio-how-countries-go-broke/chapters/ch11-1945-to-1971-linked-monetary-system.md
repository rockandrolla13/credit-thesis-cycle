# Chapter 11: 1945 to 1971—A Linked (i.e., Hard) Monetary System

## Core Idea
Historical case study of Monetary Policy 0. The Bretton Woods gold link lasted 27 years and failed the usual way: the US issued far more paper claims on gold than it held, holders ran, and on August 15, 1971 the promise was broken. The same thing happened in 1933 for the same reason with the same market effect.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Monetary Policy 0 (MP0), a linked or hard monetary system**: currency buys a designated hard asset, most often gold, at a set price, so its supply is "supposedly limited."
  - Intended discipline: if too much currency is issued, people convert it, which worsens the imbalance. Fear of that doom loop is meant to restrain money creation.
  - Why it fails: "it has never worked in the long term." Governments still create more money and debt than they should, producing more claims than convertible gold. The result is almost always a run and a broken promise.
  - When to use: to classify any pegged or commodity-linked currency and predict its end state.
- **How an MP0 run unfolds** (US 1965 to 1971):
  1. Chronic overspending financed by borrowing ("guns" in Vietnam and "butter" in social programs).
  2. Foreigners at first accept paper dollars as savings, so the overspending is easy.
  3. Competitors recover (Germany, Japan) and the balance of payments worsens.
  4. Holders turn paper in for gold. Reserves fall steadily.
  5. A large holder calls it out (de Gaulle, 1965). The run accelerates. Spending does not slow.
  6. Debt selling drives rates up and the currency down while the economy weakens.
  7. The link is cut. Money is devalued, inflation rises and debts become easier to pay.
- **The devaluation principle**: "when there is a big debt problem that is intolerably painful, central banks will 'print money' and distribute it to make it easier for debtors to pay their debts, which will devalue the money and debt relative to other assets."
- **What matters to markets**: "the money that investments earn." Political shocks "don't matter much until they start to affect those cash flows."

## Key Concepts
- **Bretton Woods system (1944)**: dollars exchangeable for gold at a fixed rate, other currencies exchangeable for dollars at agreed and changeable rates. Only central banks could buy gold.
- **Gold ownership ban**: in the US and most countries citizens could not own gold, because governments "didn't want debt assets to have to compete with gold."
- **Reserve currency privilege**: great buying power from others wanting to save in the currency, "that it eventually abused."
- **Century of Humiliation**: China's roughly 100 years from 1839 to the end of World War II.
- **Brinkmanship diplomacy**: both sides step back while claiming victory, as in the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962.

## Mental Models
- Treat dollars under MP0 as "checks with no intrinsic value" and gold as the real money.
- Bad finances that grow slowly do not seem to matter "until they led to the blowup."
- Most global threats "sound more threatening than they actually are" because leaders usually step back. Buy insurance against the rare case when they do not, without expecting it.
- Small seeds matter later: the term artificial intelligence (1956), the first satellite (1957), the technical foundations of the internet (mid-1950s), Kissinger's 1971 visit to China.
- The UK is "the most recent classic case study of the decline of a reserve currency." Read the US against it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Expecting stocks to fall on a devaluation**: the author expected a large drop after Nixon's announcement. "That Monday was the best day for the market that year—stocks were up more than 3%."
- **Trading headlines**: the Cuban Missile Crisis and the Kennedy assassination had only brief market effects.
- **Long-only equity as the default**: US stocks peaked in inflation-adjusted terms in 1968 and did not regain that level for 25 years.

## Worked Example
1933 and 1971 side by side, as the author sets them out:

| | Roosevelt, 1933 | Nixon, 1971 |
|---|---|---|
| Action | Defaulted on the promise to exchange dollars for gold | The same |
| Reason | More promises for gold than gold, during a bank run | The same |
| Effect | Devaluation, big rallies in stocks and gold | The same |
| Medium | Radio | Television |

In both cases the de-link freed central bankers to create money and credit. Equity, gold and commodity prices rose and the economy picked up.

## Key Takeaways
1. A hard link limits money only until the claims outrun the reserves.
2. The break is stimulative. Expect stocks, gold and commodities up and money down.
3. The period after 1968 had "terrible stock and bond market performance and terrific gold and other inflation-hedge asset performance."
4. Geopolitical realignments (China and the Soviet Union, then China and the US) reshape all five forces over decades.
5. Taiwan matters now because it is "the center of chip production, which today is even more important than oil production was in the last cycle."

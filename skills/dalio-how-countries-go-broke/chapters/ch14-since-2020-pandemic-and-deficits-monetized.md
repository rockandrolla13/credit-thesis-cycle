# Chapter 14: Since 2020—Pandemic and Big Fiscal Deficits Monetized

## Core Idea
Historical case study of Monetary Policy 3, taken up to March 2025. The pandemic forced coordinated deficits and monetization. That produced a bubble, an inflation surge, a classic tightening and a modest easing. The author judges the internal order to be at the same stage as the 1930s.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Monetary Policy 3 (MP3)**: "coordinated moves between the central government and the central bank, where the government runs large deficits and the bank monetizes them."
  - Trigger: MP1 and MP2 no longer help most people, conditions are very bad and wealth gaps are large. Capitalism supplies capital to those who can borrow and not "to those who have the least and suffer the most."
  - Purpose: not only to create money and credit but "to get it into the hands of specific people and organizations."
  - Timing: typically late in the long-term debt cycle. Here it came in two big rounds.
  - Note: the independence of monetary from fiscal policy is one of the "sacrosanct" rules that "starts to break down later in the Big Cycle."
- **The 2020-25 short-term cycle, as a causal chain**: pandemic, economic contraction, huge coordinated stimulus (MP3), higher inflation and asset prices with wealth redistribution, inflation surge, tightening, lower inflation, modest easing.
- **Bubble indicator readings**: the author's gauge (described in ch01) stood at 18% in 2010, 75% at the end of 2020 ("a significant bubble") and 35% after the tightening ("not in a bubble").
  - Bubble definition used here: companies and assets with little or no profits, funded by selling equity or borrowing on promises, with speculative buying fever.
  - Analogues named: the Nifty Fifty of 1970-72, Japan in 1989-90, dot-com in 1999-2000.
- **Why the tightening hurt the economy less than usual**: the central government took the debt and the central bank took the losses, in place of the private sector. Inflation also showed up in wages, not only in prices.

## Key Concepts
- **Cash from "trash" to "attractive"**: the shift in short-term nominal and real rates that lowers the present value of other assets and lifts the currency.
- **Paper wealth**: household financial assets minus household liabilities (Federal Reserve data).
- **Private marks stand-off**: private equity, venture capital and real estate "were not marked down commensurately." Sellers and buyers cannot agree and transaction volumes plunged.
- **Private credit**: "the modern-day version of the junk bond market of the late 1970s and early 1980s (though more customized, not securitized, more illiquid, and inclusive of early-stage companies)." Its inflows held credit spreads down and funded speculation.
- **Nonviolent civil war**: the author's term for the US conflict between right and left at the time of writing.

## Mental Models
- On long charts back to 1945, "the weekly, monthly, and even annual changes seem trivial." Hold both scales: "the small short term affects the big long term as much as the big long term affects the small short term."
- A fast-tightening central bank makes its cash attractive against most assets, other currencies' cash and gold. Slower central banks see their currencies weaken.
- Rate declines after 2008 did a large share of the equity work. The author estimates they raised stock prices about 75% more than otherwise, measured against the pre-crisis peak. Profit margins also roughly doubled on average.
- AI is "the new hot thing," compared with the technologies that drove the industrial and digital revolutions "and led to financial bubbles."

## Anti-patterns
- **Extrapolating free money**: "Money and credit became essentially free and plentiful" and led to "an orgy of borrowing and new bubbles forming."
- **Trusting unmarked private valuations** after a rate shock.
- **Reading the 2022-23 resilience as proof tightening is harmless**: the debt sat with the government and the losses with the Fed.

## Worked Example
The 2020-25 cycle step by step, with the author's numbers:
1. Shock: COVID contraction, large wealth gaps, and a political move left in the 2020 elections.
2. MP3: spending and deficits far above what free-market lenders would buy. The Fed monetized. Banks and Japanese institutional investors also bought heavily.
3. Bubble: real yields at −1%, bubble indicator at 75%.
4. Inflation: the surge was worsened by supply chain problems and external conflicts.
5. Tightening: balance sheet runoff, nominal yields to over 4%, real yields to about 2.5%, the curve inverted. Real yields returned to the long-run average of about 2%.
6. Damage: bubble stocks −75%, financial wealth −18% real. The Fed lost money on its bonds.
7. Easing: inflation fell, prices stayed high, central banks eased, asset prices recovered and AI became the leading theme.
8. Politics: inflation "and other factors" helped Trump win decisively in 2024, with "a mandate to undertake a big renovation."

## Key Takeaways
1. MP3 is the late-cycle regime. Expect its inflation to follow with a lag.
2. Compare the real bond yield with 2% to judge whether a tightening has reached normal.
3. Bubbles defined by absent profits and equity-funded growth are the first casualties of a rate rise.
4. Check where the debt sits before estimating the economic damage of a tightening.
5. The author's summary of March 2025: debt growing, internal conflict intensifying, US-China conflict intensifying, climate worsening, AI advancing. "This looks quite like the 1930s period."

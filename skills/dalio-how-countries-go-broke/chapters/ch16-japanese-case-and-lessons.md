# Chapter 16: The Japanese Case and the Lessons It Provides

## Core Idea
Historical case study with the most reusable arithmetic in Part III. From 1990 to 2013 Japan did "exactly what not to do." From 2013 it monetized on a huge scale, which cut debt held by the public, cut interest service by more than half and improved the government's net position in dollars. Bondholders paid: −6% real, −45% versus US bonds, −60% versus gold. "Don't own government bonds when there are extreme amounts of debt monetization."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **What not to do in a deleveraging (Japan 1990 to 2013)**:
  1. Do not leave debts unrestructured. Japan waited nine years, until 1999, creating "zombie institutions."
  2. Do not keep rigid employment and cost policies that block adjustment.
  3. Do not leave interest rates above nominal growth and inflation. Japan did for 23 years.
  4. Do not delay monetization until after deflation sets in and rates are near zero (1995).
  - Result: continuous deflation and stagnation until mid-2013, despite Japan having every condition for a beautiful deleveraging: local-currency debt, domestic debtors and creditors, and net creditor status to the world.
- **The "three arrows" (Abe and Kuroda, from late 2012 / early 2013)**: (1) increase the money supply, (2) boost central government spending, (3) economic and regulatory reforms. The author calls these "classically the best policies to negate deflationary, depressionary forces."
- **The five dynamics of a reserve-currency, own-currency deleveraging**:
  1. Public deficit spending floods the private sector with cash and helps it delever.
  2. The central bank monetizes to keep long rates low, cut debt service and boost demand. Government debt net of central bank holdings falls as a percent of GDP.
  3. Currency depreciation acts as a tax on foreign holders of unhedged domestic bonds and on domestic investors who stayed home. It lowers the debt burden in foreign currency and gold.
  4. Domestic savers are taxed too, but less, because domestic buying power falls less than buying power abroad.
  5. The country becomes more competitive as assets and factors of production get cheaper.
  - When to use: to project winners and losers when a large own-currency debtor chooses monetization.
  - How: for each dynamic, measure it in three units: local currency, dollars and gold.
- **Rate-shock stress test for a monetizing central bank**: see Worked Example.

## Key Concepts
- **Zombie institutions**: banks and companies carrying unrestructured debt burdens.
- **Debt ex-CB**: government debt minus central bank holdings, the debt actually held by the public.
- **Tradables vs non-tradables**: the split that explains Japan's price experience. Non-tradables (housing, services, wages) were flat in yen. Tradable commodities rose 2x to 10x.
- **Pushing on a string**: Japan's last two decades: nominal rates below 0%, real rates slightly negative, heavy printing, a barely positive curve, low spreads, and inflation still below target.
- **Debt service threshold**: debt busts happened when central government debt service exceeded 150% of revenue. It has recently risen toward but stayed below 150%.
- **Strength gauge / Country Power Index**: the author's 18-measure gauge covering 24 countries, cited for neutral risers (India, ASEAN countries, the UAE, Saudi Arabia).

## Mental Models
- A depreciation that halves the foreign value of a bond "is not dissimilar to a default."
- The −45% return of JGBs versus US Treasuries was "almost entirely attributable to currency depreciation." Lower carry was offset by price gains of roughly 20% from falling yields.
- A weak economy stops currency declines from becoming domestic inflation.
- Measure wages in gold as well as currency. A Japanese worker earned 13 ounces of gold-equivalent a month and now earns 1.
- Japan's pre-1945 path followed the same script as others: reform and opening (Meiji, 1868), rise to great power, democratic dysfunction plus debt crisis, hard-right nationalism, sanctions and asset freezes, war, and the destruction of all money and debt.

## Anti-patterns
- **Holding the bonds of a heavy monetizer**, hedged or not, as a store of wealth.
- **Judging a debt load by gross debt to GDP alone**: Japan's gross debt rose while debt held by the public fell.
- **Assuming a profitable central bank is a safe one**: the BoJ is profitable only while short rates stay low.

## Worked Example
**Stress test: Japanese real rates rise 3%, from −0.3% to 2.7%.**
1. BoJ balance sheet: about a 30% of GDP mark-to-market loss on bonds.
2. BoJ cash flow: about −2.5% of GDP a year.
3. Government deficit: widens from roughly 4% to around 8% of GDP over 10 years from interest costs, before any outlay to cover BoJ losses.
4. Government debt: rises from 220% to 300% of GDP over 20 years, above the post-WWII peak.
5. Combined cash need: around 5-6% of GDP a year, "which is huge."
6. Funding options: debt issuance, printing and/or deficit reduction. If printed, it equals another round of QE in money-stock terms, before offsetting any private selling.
7. Resolution: "even greater write-downs in debt and devaluations of the currency," with the Japanese people relatively poorer, until the country is competitive enough to start a new cycle.

For scale, the Fed's recent losses were "up to 0.5% of GDP," and the BoJ's monetary base is around 5x the Fed's.

## Key Takeaways
1. Restructure early and get rates below nominal growth and inflation early. Japan's delay cost two decades.
2. Monetization works for the government's accounts. It moved the cost to creditors and to the currency.
3. Track debt held by the public and interest paid to the public, not gross figures.
4. The living-standard cost shows up abroad. In dollar GDP per capita, people in Japan went from richer than Americans to some 60% poorer.
5. Non-tradable essentials stayed affordable in hours of work (rent about 0.6 months of labor), which kept the adjustment tolerable at home.
6. A monetizer's central bank holds a large short-rate exposure. Stress it.

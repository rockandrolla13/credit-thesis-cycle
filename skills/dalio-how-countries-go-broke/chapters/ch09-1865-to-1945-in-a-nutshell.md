# Chapter 9: From 1865 to 1945 in a Tiny Nutshell

## Core Idea
Historical case study. The 80 years before 1945 ran one full Big Cycle: war, debt reduction by default and devaluation, a productivity boom, wealth gaps and a bubble, a bust, populism of left and right, and war again. The chapter is brief and has no charts. Use it as a pattern example, not as a source of method.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The broken-promise principle** (stated twice in the chapter): when there is too much debt relative to the money needed to service it, governments break their promises and do some combination of (a) raising the amount of money and credit, (b) reducing the amount of debt, for example by restructuring, and/or (c) preventing the free-market ownership and movement of hard money such as gold.
  - When to use: any time debt service outruns the supply of the money it is owed in.
  - How: expect "a run away from bad money to good money that the government wants to stop," which "often leads to prohibiting good money from being freely held and freely moved."
- **The post-war Big Cycle sequence**: war ends and debt burdens are reduced, then (1) great inventiveness and productivity, (2) debt- and equity-financed speculation, (3) big wealth and values gaps, (4) bubble and bust, (5) fights over wealth and power inside countries, (6) rising powers challenge established powers, (7) war, (8) winners set new monetary, political and geopolitical orders.
- **Two method principles from the Part III introduction**:
  - "When looking for the big things, pay attention to the big things." Precision on small events hides the large ones.
  - Build a template that explains past changes and matches what is happening. Where it does not match, resolve the discrepancy.

## Key Concepts
- **Greenbacks**: US paper money not backed by gold, printed during the Civil War.
- **Gilded Age / robber barons**: the era and the resented rich of the Second Industrial Revolution.
- **No-central-bank regime**: before 1913 the dollar was fixed to gold by commercial banks, so busts had no monetary easing and "some of the busts were very big and long-lasting."
- **Strongman unilateralism**: the 1930s move away from multilateralism and agreements.

## Mental Models
- Big Cycles "typically start after a war."
- A country that enters a war late and finances and supplies others can emerge as the leading creditor. The US after World War I is the example.
- "When countries are weak, opposing countries take advantage of their weaknesses to obtain gains."
- Before military war, expect economic warfare, freezing of financial assets and military buildups.

## Anti-patterns
- **Holding to hard money through a debt bust**: the gold-standard panics of 1873, 1893 and 1907 were deep because no money could be printed. The politics produced Bryan's "cross of gold" speech and, after 1907, the Federal Reserve in 1913.
- **Idealistic world governance without enforcement**: the League of Nations "failed at preventing the next major war."

## Worked Example
The US 1933 break, mapped to the principle:
1. Too much debt relative to money after the 1929 bust and depression.
2. (a) More money: convertibility of paper money into gold is ended.
3. (b) Less debt in real terms: the official gold price moves from $20.67 to $35, about a 40% devaluation.
4. (c) Controls on good money: private gold ownership is outlawed, Americans cannot take money abroad, foreign bank accounts are restricted.
The same three moves appear in the Civil War episode and again in 1971 (ch11, ch12).

## Key Takeaways
1. Both US devaluations in this period followed debt that could not be paid in hard money.
2. Democracies that were "inefficiently run, conflict-ridden" (Spain, Italy, Japan, Germany) turned to autocracies of the right in the 1920s and 1930s.
3. The author draws a direct parallel to the present: "Just as we are now seeing in the US and several other countries."
4. Overextended leading powers (UK, France, Netherlands) lost ground to rising ones.

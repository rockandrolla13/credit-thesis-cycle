# Patterns — How Countries Go Broke: The Big Cycle

## Transaction-based price estimate (P = $/Q)
**When to use**: forecasting any price, especially a government bond market.
**How**: list the big buyers and sellers. State each one's motive and constraint. Estimate total spending and total quantity sold. Flag any rate of buying or selling that cannot continue and compute the price at a normal rate.
**Trade-offs**: needs flow data by holder. It gives magnitude better than timing. (Ch 2)

## Four-ratio sustainability check
**When to use**: first pass on any sovereign or large debtor.
**How**: compute (1) debt to income, (2) debt service to income, (3) nominal rate versus inflation and versus nominal income growth, (4) debt and debt service versus liquid savings. Use government revenue as income, not GDP.
**Trade-offs**: sizes risk and severity. It cannot time the crisis. (Ch 3)

## Debt-dynamics identity
**When to use**: projecting a debt ratio or solving for a required lever.
**How**: future debt/revenue = [primary deficit + debt × (1 + i)] / [revenue × (1 + g)]. Rate that holds debt flat = revenue growth − primary deficit / starting debt. Solve in turn for the required rate, inflation, spending cut and revenue increase.
**Trade-offs**: assumes debt is rolled. It ignores holders selling. (Ch 3, 18)

## Death-spiral toy model
**When to use**: stress-testing a sovereign against rising rates or a buyer strike.
**How**: set income growth, spending share, starting debt, rate and share maturing each year. Path A: raise the rate each year and track debt, interest and debt service to income. Path B: cap private holdings, let the central bank buy the rest, and track M0 and the implied currency move.
**Trade-offs**: a toy. Growth is held flat and currency sensitivity is assumed. (Ch 3)

## Nine-stage staging
**When to use**: locating a country in the final phase of its Big Debt Cycle.
**How**: score the nine stages as present or absent. Weight three flags: net selling of government debt, long rates rising faster than short rates with a falling currency, and a central bank with negative net worth that prints to cover cash-flow losses.
**Trade-offs**: stages can occur out of order. Fiat cases show them slowly. (Ch 4 to 7, 13)

## Currency-defense tracker
**When to use**: a central bank is selling reserves to hold its currency.
**How**: track reserves as a percent of GDP, money and government debt, and the pace of drawdown. Benchmarks from the cases: reserves about 5% of GDP at the start, a defense of about two years, over half of reserves spent, capitulation near 6% of money and 3% of debt.
**Trade-offs**: medians across the 25 listed cases, with wide ranges. (Ch 5)

## Central bank loss monitor
**When to use**: a central bank holds a large fixed-rate bond book funded by floating-rate reserves.
**How**: compute cash-flow loss as a percent of GDP now and under a rate rise. Check who pays, printing or recapitalization. Check whether printed money stays in the currency. About 3% of GDP a year marked the historical spirals.
**Trade-offs**: small losses are harmless. Reserve-currency issuers have more room. (Ch 6, 16, 17)

## Debt-reduction attribution
**When to use**: projecting how a debt ratio falls after a crisis.
**How**: split the change into central bank purchases, inflation, real growth, primary surplus, restructuring and interest cost. Archetype: 89% to 70% of GDP, with inflation −38, growth −26, surplus −16, restructuring −8, purchases −7, interest +76.
**Trade-offs**: averages with large ranges. (Ch 7)

## Monetary-regime classification (MP0 to MP3)
**When to use**: inferring cycle stage from what the central bank is doing.
**How**: identify the regime: hard link, rate-driven fiat, monetization, or coordinated fiscal and monetary. Note the end condition of each: a run on reserves, rates at zero, monetization failing to reach those in need.
**Trade-offs**: regimes overlap and can be revisited. (Ch 1, 11 to 14)

## Real-yield and curve regime read
**When to use**: judging whether conditions favor borrowers or lenders.
**How**: compare the real 10-year yield with about 2%. Read the curve as short minus long (normal about −1%) and short divided by long (normal about 70%). High real yields with a flat or inverted curve favor lenders. Low real yields with a positive curve favor borrowers.
**Trade-offs**: estimated real yields before inflation-linked markets existed are rough. (Ch 10)

## Five-force scan
**When to use**: top-level view of a country or the world over more than a few years.
**How**: grade debt, internal order, external order, nature and technology as improving or worsening and early or late. When debt, internal and external conflict are all late, expect changes of order.
**Trade-offs**: qualitative. The author's own gauges are not published in the book. (Ch 8, 15, 19)

## Sovereign dashboard
**When to use**: comparing sovereign long-term risk.
**How**: tabulate debt, 10-year projection, holders, hard-currency share, interest to revenue, reserves, sovereign wealth, total debt, current account and four reserve-currency shares. Separate long-term from short-term risk.
**Trade-offs**: excludes pensions and quasi-government balance sheets. (Ch 17)

## Top-down fiscal fix with fallback
**When to use**: designing or assessing a deficit plan.
**How**: fix the total first. Spread it across spending, taxes and interest rates. Pair fiscal tightening with monetary easing. Act when growth is strong. Adopt an automatic equal-percentage fallback. Phase in over three years.
**Trade-offs**: needs central bank cooperation, which cannot be promised. (Ch 18)

## Monetizer's five dynamics
**When to use**: an own-currency debtor chooses heavy monetization.
**How**: expect public deficits to delever the private sector, debt ex-central-bank to fall, currency depreciation to tax foreign and domestic holders, domestic savers to lose less than foreigners, and competitiveness to rise. Measure each in local currency, dollars and gold.
**Trade-offs**: built on one case, Japan since 2013. (Ch 16)

## Unsustainable-condition betting
**When to use**: selecting themes.
**How**: find large conditions that cannot continue. Check they are not in the price. Hold many uncorrelated bets. Compare outcomes with expectations and revise the model.
**Trade-offs**: the author reports being wrong at least a third of the time. (Ch 19)

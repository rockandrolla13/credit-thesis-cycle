## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. The Big Debt Cycle (ch01, ch02)
Short-term debt cycles (about 6 years, give or take 3) each end with more debt than the last. They add up to a Big Debt Cycle (about 80 years, give or take 25) that ends when debt assets and liabilities are too large relative to money, goods and incomes. "A debt is a promise to deliver money." When promises exceed money, the central bank chooses between default and printing, and "in the end, the central bank always prints and devalues."
- Use the five stages to place a country: Sound Money, Debt Bubble, Top, Deleveraging, Big Debt Crisis Recedes.
- Rate sovereign debt on loss of value by default or devaluation. "Default or devaluation, I don't care."
- The key case variable: can the central bank print the currency the debt is in, and is it a reserve currency?

### 2. Transaction-based pricing, P = $/Q (ch02)
Price = total amount spent / total quantity sold. Estimate both from the motives of the big buyers and sellers. Find rates of buying or selling that cannot continue and price the return to normal. Use it on bond markets: ask who will buy how much, not whether "confidence" holds.

### 3. Debt sustainability arithmetic (ch03)
Measure against government revenue, not GDP. Watch four indicators: debt/income, debt service/income, nominal rate versus inflation and nominal growth, and debt versus liquid savings.
- Future debt/revenue = [(expenses excl. interest − revenue) + debt × (1 + i)] / [revenue × (1 + g)].
- Rate that keeps debt flat = revenue growth − primary deficit / starting debt.
- Change in debt/income = (i − g) × debt/income + primary deficit/income.
- With i 2% above g for 20 years, the ratio rises about 50%.
- US baseline in the book: debt 583% of revenue, primary deficit 12% of revenue, 35% of debt maturing each year.

### 4. Nine Stages of the Final Crisis (ch04 to ch07)
Derived from 35 cases over 100 years. Use as a checklist and risk gauge.
1. Private sector and government get deep in debt.
2. Private debt crisis. Government borrows more to help.
3. Government debt squeeze: free-market demand falls short of supply. Net selling is the big red flag.
4. Selling tightens money, weakens the economy, drains reserves and pressures the currency. Long rates rise faster than short rates as the currency falls.
5. Rates cannot be cut. The central bank prints and buys bonds.
6. Rates rise and the central bank loses money. Negative net worth plus printing to cover cash flow is the death spiral.
7. Debts are restructured and devalued.
8. Extraordinary taxes and capital controls.
9. Deleveraging restores equilibrium, often with a hard-currency link and very high real rates.

Fixed-rate cases break abruptly. Fiat cases devalue gradually.

### 5. Monetary policy regimes (ch01, ch11 to ch14)
Identify the regime to infer the stage.
- **MP0**: linked or hard money. US 1945 to 1971. Ends in a run on reserves.
- **MP1**: fiat, rate-driven. US 1971 to 2008. Ends when rates hit 0%.
- **MP2**: fiat with debt monetization (QE). US 2008 to 2020. Lifts financial assets and wealth gaps.
- **MP3**: large fiscal deficits monetized by the central bank. US from 2020.
- Then a big deleveraging, then a return to hard money.

### 6. Beautiful deleveraging and the four levers (ch02, ch07)
Four levers: austerity, defaults and restructurings, central bank printing and purchases, transfers. Austerity first is "a big mistake." Balance restructuring (deflationary) with monetization (inflationary) so nominal growth exceeds nominal rates. Spread a 50% reduction over years at 3% or 4% a year. In the archetype, government debt fell from 89% to 70% of GDP: inflation −38, real growth −26, primary surplus −16, restructuring −8, central bank purchases −7, interest +76.

### 7. What to own and what to avoid (ch07, ch10, ch16)
- Late in the cycle debt assets become undesirable versus equities, gold and other stores of value. Gold beat local cash by roughly 60% on average through devaluations.
- "Don't own government bonds when there are extreme amounts of debt monetization." Japanese bondholders since 2013: −45% versus US bonds, −60% versus gold.
- The real bond yield is "the most important number to watch." Neutral is about 2%. High real yields with a flat or inverted curve favor lender-creditors. Low real yields with a positive curve favor borrower-debtors.
- After the five steps of stage 9 are in place, the country's currency and debt are among the best things to hold.

### 8. The Five Big Forces and the Overall Big Cycle (ch08, ch19)
(1) Debt/credit/money/economy, (2) internal order and disorder, (3) external order and disorder, (4) acts of nature, (5) human inventiveness and technology. When the first three are late together, expect changes of monetary, domestic and world order. The author places March 2025 in Stage 5, analogous to 1905-14 and 1933-38.

### 9. Sovereign dashboard and US risk gauges (ch17)
Tabulate debt, its holders, hard-currency share, interest to revenue, reserves and reserve-currency shares. Separate long-term risk from short-term risk. US central government long-term risk read 2.4z ("above 2 is quite bad") with very low short-term risk. Two red flags to watch: renewed QE to force real rates down, and government control of the central bank.

### 10. The 3% 3-part solution (ch18)
Cut the US deficit to 3% of GDP with spending cuts, tax increases and lower interest rates: about −4%, +4% and −1% in real rates. A 1% rate cut is about four times as effective as a 1% tax rise. Tighten fiscal policy in good times and ease money alongside. Of 40 fiscal adjustments since 1960, the 21 painless ones had no significant hard-currency debt.

### 11. The broken-promise principle (ch09, ch11, ch19)
When debt is too large relative to the money needed to service it, governments raise money and credit, reduce debt, and/or prevent free ownership and movement of hard money. US examples: the Civil War, 1933 and 1971.

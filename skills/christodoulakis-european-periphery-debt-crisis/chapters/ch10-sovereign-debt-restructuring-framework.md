# Chapter 10: Sovereign Debt and its Restructuring Framework in the Eurozone

**Contributor**: Ashoka Mody (Princeton; Bruegel; formerly IMF). Part II. Written September 2014 ("at the time of writing, September 2014").

## Core Idea
A currency union without exchange-rate flexibility or usable inflation has one shock absorber left: the debt itself. Sovereign debt should be treated as an equity-like contingent claim. Because officials always postpone restructuring in a crisis, the only credible route is a contract that restructures automatically: "sovereign cocos" whose repayment terms ease when the debt ratio crosses an agreed threshold. Delay and ad hoc decisions, not restructuring, are what markets punish.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **High-debt-low-growth trap**. Three linked loops: (1) austerity curbs growth, so public debt ratios barely move; (2) private deleveraging is slow (eurozone household and corporate debt ratios were still rising in 2011 while US ratios fell or were flat); (3) weak banks must shed assets before lending, low growth erodes their assets and raises public debt. Banks and sovereigns are "joined at the hip".

- **Trilemma argument for recurrent crises**. A fixed-rate economy hit by a demand drop cannot depreciate. Speculation that would have hit the currency hits interest rates. Real rates overshoot both ways, giving booms and busts. One central bank cannot serve divergent members. So a credible restructuring mechanism must complement monetary policy.

- **Fiscal solvency (Bohn) equation**. Regress the primary balance/GDP on the lagged public debt ratio and the output gap (panel of advanced economies, 1995-2013, IMF WEO October 2012, corrected for autocorrelation, following Mendoza and Ostry 2007). A positive debt coefficient means the intertemporal budget constraint holds. Tables below.
  - Finding: in the Great Recession the eurozone's debt response (0.14) was far above the non-euro response (-0.01) and above its own earlier response (0.06). The eurozone "took the debt reduction business much more seriously", which with high multipliers meant slower growth and little change in debt ratios.

- **Two meanings of "no-bailout"**:
  - Official (public-to-public): TFEU Articles 123 and 125, a legal injunction.
  - Private (public-to-private): creditors are bailed out if repaid in full with official money. Avoiding it is a policy choice.
  - Logic: honouring the official rule requires private losses. Repaying private creditors forced official financing, which diluted the treaty rule. A credible private no-bailout would raise sovereign borrowing costs, reduce borrowing and give "a more stable equilibrium".

- **War of attrition**: exit is too costly and fuller union infeasible. With private default a last resort and growth absent, crisis management becomes reactive and ad hoc, each case "sui generis".

- **De facto subordination of official debt**. Easing official terms (OSI) frees resources to repay private creditors. Official credit becomes junior in effect. The option value of future growth accrues to private creditors, who are encouraged to lend again. Examples: Greek maturity extensions; the Irish Promissory Notes deal; ESM lending to Spain on a pari passu basis ("one-off"); OMT's pari passu stance against the ECB's possible "implicit" seniority.

- **Sovereign debt as a contingent claim** (Sims 2001, 2003; Cochrane 2005; Grossman and Van Huyck 1988; Rogoff 1999; Kovrijnykh and Szentes 2007). Nominal own-currency debt is like equity: surprise inflation is an automatic partial default that is less distorting than tax changes. Gold-standard countries suspended specie payment or extended maturities. The eurozone has no such vent, so "debt recontracting is the only vent".

- **Neglected risk** (Gennaioli et al. 2012): securities deemed safe ignore improbable states, are over-issued, and are held by leveraged institutions. Bad news then causes drastic revaluation and deleveraging. Mody calls this "a pithy and precise description of the eurozone crisis", with zero risk weights and ECB collateral treatment as the cause.

- **Sovereign cocos** (Barkbu et al. 2012). Design:
  1. Trigger: the debt-to-GDP ratio (lagged, to limit revision risk) crossing an agreed threshold. The introduction also allows the price of debt as a trigger. A request for official assistance can be an added trigger.
  2. First effect: automatic maturity extension. Higher thresholds can tie repayment to economic and financial developments.
  3. Each country negotiates threshold, extent of restructuring and price with its creditors, possibly per issue.
  4. No EU directive or treaty change is needed.
  - Expected effects: no debate on whether to restructure, less delay, debt priced without the bailout subsidy, so higher rates and less borrowing.
  - Moral hazard: present in any contingent debt. Only triggers outside both parties' control (commodity prices, earthquakes) remove it, and those correlate poorly with the debt burden.

- **Unified treatment of the debt overhang**: legacy sovereign debt through exchanges, with Buchheit et al. (2013) proposing an ESM Treaty amendment that denies holdouts access to the sovereign's assets in ESM territory. Bank debt through debt-to-equity conversion on explicit triggers. Underwater mortgages through repayment linked to the market price of the home (for example, a starting burden of 80% of current market value, with upside shared).

- **Event-study method** (after Ait-Sahalia et al. 2012): the abnormal change in the ten-year spread over Bunds is the actual change minus the average change over the prior 20 days, cumulated over five days after the announcement.

## Key Concepts
- **Legacy debt**: the debt stock from the ongoing crisis.
- **Debt overhang**: debt large enough to deter voluntary new lending (Krugman 1988).
- **OSI**: official sector involvement, which Mody calls a "dreadful euphemism".
- **Pari passu**: equal ranking with private creditors.
- **Preferred creditor status**: IMF first, then ESM, private creditors residual.
- **Pringle Decision**: the ECJ ruling that official lending is compatible with no-bailout if it safeguards eurozone financial stability and is repaid with "an appropriate margin".
- **SDRM**: Krueger's 2002 Sovereign Debt Restructuring Mechanism.
- **Excusable default / equilibrium default cycles**: creditors expect partial default in bad states and find forgiveness plus new lending in their interest.
- **Wake-up call contagion**: contagion from perceived similarity. Forbes (2012) finds weak evidence for it.

## Mental Models
- "By induction ... debt restructuring is never established policy": rules cannot be set in a crisis for fear of panic, and nobody wants them in calm times. Only pre-committed contracts escape this.
- Senior official lending raises the risk on remaining private debt. Mody (citing Chamley and Pinto 2011; Steinkamp and Westermann 2013) attributes the 2010 spread rise to the expansion of senior official debt after the first Greek package, not to Deauville.
- Default costs are low when the post-default policy regime is credible. Evidence cited: quick market return, output rising from the quarter after default, interwar defaulters with no worse later access, Philip II's lenders profiting across serial defaults.
- Haircut size matters: Cruces and Trebesch (2011) find larger haircuts bring higher later premia and longer exclusion. Early, even pre-emptive, restructuring with haircuts under 40% gives relief with limited downside.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a debt crisis as a liquidity crisis of the East Asian kind**: those recoveries relied on depreciation and strong world growth.
- **Waiting**: Greek debt was heading to 250% of GDP when written down in 2012. Cypriot bank credit reached about 900% of GDP before the depositor bail-in.
- **Forbearance on banks to protect them from sovereign losses**: it creates zombie banks.
- **Reading the autumn 2010 spread rise as proof that restructuring talk is dangerous**: "a superficial reading of the evidence".
- **Declaring each case exceptional**: Greece, the Spanish ESM loan and Cyprus ("not a template") were all labelled one-off, which maximizes policy uncertainty.

## Worked Example
Three announcements, read through abnormal spread changes (sect. 4.1):

1. Deauville, 19 October 2010 (private losses as a condition for ESM help after 2013, wider use of CACs).
   - Periphery average: about +40 bp cumulated by day five. Greece almost +150 bp.
   - Ireland peaked at +50 bp and Portugal at +45 bp on day three, then faded. Spain and Italy: trivial.
   - Scale check: the standard deviation of Portugal's four-day cumulative changes over the prior 20 days was 31 bp, so +45 bp is about 1.5 standard deviations. Irish yields averaged 6.44% in the five days after Deauville against 6.61% in the five days to 30 September.
   - Reading: no break with the past and no contagion. Spread moves were ordered by credit risk.
2. Euro Area summit, 21 July 2011 (Greek private debt to be restructured; official terms eased for Greece, Ireland and Portugal: rate cut from 5.5% to 3.5%, repayment from 7 to 15 years).
   - Abnormal spreads fell sharply for Greece, Ireland and Portugal and stayed lower.
   - Reading: markets were not frightened by restructuring and welcomed regaining seniority over official creditors.
3. 20 February 2012 (next steps on Greece unclear).
   - Spreads rose. Greece's rise was too large to chart. Portuguese spreads rose "considerably more than they had done after Deauville".
   - Reading: contagion showed up under uncertainty.

## Key Takeaways
1. In a currency union, treat sovereign debt as a contingent claim and ask what the contingency mechanism is. If none exists, expect delay and official money replacing private money.
2. Track the share of senior official debt. Its growth raises the risk premium on the remaining private debt.
3. A high primary-balance response to debt during a recession signals pro-cyclical policy, not safety.
4. Test "restructuring causes contagion" claims with a short-window event study scaled by recent volatility.
5. Watch for easing of official terms. It is restructuring by another name and shifts value to private creditors.
6. Mody's forecast: the crisis is unlikely to be resolved "without significantly more debt restructuring".

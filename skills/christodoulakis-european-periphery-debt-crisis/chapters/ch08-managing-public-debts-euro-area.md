# Chapter 8: How to Manage Public Debts in the Euro Area?

**Contributors**: Catherine Mathieu and Henri Sterdyniak (OFCE). Part II. Written mid-2014 ("as at the time of writing, mid-2014").

## Core Idea
The rise in public debt after 2008 came from the depth of the crisis, not from earlier fiscal laxity (Greece excepted). The Fiscal Pact's balanced structural budget rule "has no economic rationale". The authors reject both market discipline and rule-based fiscal federalism. They want member states to regain free use of fiscal policy, with public debt fully guaranteed by the ECB or by eurobonds for countries that join a negotiated, growth-targeted coordination process. They admit this may be "difficult, even impossible" to agree, and conclude that without it "the single currency will not survive".

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Stability gap** (Table 8.2): primary government balance minus the balance required to stabilize debt, where the required balance = net debt x (long-term interest rate corrected for trend growth).
  - When to use: testing whether a pre-crisis fiscal position was sustainable.
  - Finding: in 2007 the euro area's primary balance stood 1.8 points above the stabilizing level. Only France and Portugal were slightly below. Spain, Ireland and Greece were stable only because interest rates were far below growth, which made the stability fragile.

- **Box 8.1, a Keynesian perspective** (recovered from PDF p. 157):
  - Demand: `y = g + d + c*y - sigma*r + k*h`, with g government borrowing, d autonomous demand, r the interest rate, h public debt (y is read as the output gap).
  - Short-run full stabilization: `g = -d + sigma*r`. With perfect stabilization there is no ex post link between the deficit and the output gap, and this g would be counted as "structural" by OECD and Commission methods, "which makes no sense".
  - Long run: `g = 0` and `h = -(d - sigma*r) / k`. The long-run debt level depends on private agents' desired holdings at the optimal interest rate, which equals the growth rate.
  - Implication: a rule of the form `g = g0 - lambda*y - mu*(h - h_target)` cannot deliver full stabilization, and a government cannot fix a debt target regardless of private saving behaviour.

- **Debt arithmetic used against the rules**:
  - Desired debt 80% of GDP and nominal growth 3.5% (1.75% real, 1.75% prices) justify a deficit of about 2.8% of GDP.
  - A permanent 1% deficit with 3.5% nominal growth drives debt to about 28.5% of GDP (the text also prints 25%).
  - The Fiscal Pact (0.5% structural deficit above 60% debt, 1% below) with 3% nominal growth drives debt towards 33%.
  - Nominal growth of about 3.5%, an interest rate near 2.5% and a structural primary budget near balance cut the debt ratio by about 1 point of GDP a year.

- **Two priorities**: (1) recover the output lost since the crisis; (2) let members finance debt at an interest rate below nominal growth until the depression ends.

- **Three choices facing the euro area (sect. 4)**:
  1. Fiscal target or growth target. The authors choose growth: national stabilization policy, expansionary monetary policy, an ECB guarantee bringing rates near 2% everywhere, higher wages where the wage share fell, restored social spending, investment support.
  2. Autonomous fiscal policy or fiscal federalism. They choose autonomy. Transfer schemes based on output gaps or unemployment rest on vague, revisable measures, are small, and vanish in a general depression.
  3. Market discipline or unity of public debts. They choose unity. Markets "have no macroeconomic perspective", are pro-cyclical, self-fulfilling and "schizophrenic" in demanding consolidation and growth together.

- **Authors' proposal (sect. 5.3)**: a treaty-based coordination process targeting growth and full employment, covering inflation (around 2%), wages (real wages in line with productivity in the medium run), social contributions as an adjustment tool, and negotiated current account targets (surplus countries lower their targets or finance industrial projects in the south). Deficits that result are financed by debt guaranteed by all members and the ECB. A country outside the agreement loses the guarantee on new debt.

## Key Concepts
- **Fiscal Pact, Article 3.1**: balanced or surplus budget, respected if the structural deficit is below 0.5% of GDP, with an automatic correction mechanism. Ratified 2 March 2012.
- **Six-Pack**: Commission package of 29 September 2010 strengthening the SGP and adding the Macroeconomic Imbalance Procedure (MIP) scoreboard.
- **True golden rule of public finances**: borrowing is justified to finance public investment.
- **Target 2**: the settlement system whose balances finance national banking systems automatically through the ESCB. It does not finance governments directly.
- **ESM**: launched October 2012, lending capacity EUR 500 bn, conditional, senior to private debt.
- **Mercantilist / neo-mercantilist countries**: those pursuing competitiveness gains and external surpluses (China, Germany and other northern European economies).
- **Negative fiscal impulse**: the cumulated discretionary tightening, measured against pre-crisis trend output.
- **Blue debt / red debt**: mutualized debt up to 60% of GDP and national debt above it (Delpla and von Weizsacker).

## Mental Models
- Before 1999 a state, its banks and its central bank formed a unit. The central bank was lender of last resort to both, so public debt was safe. The euro broke that unit while making fiscal policy more necessary.
- Solidarity that is "instinctive" inside a nation is absent across the EU. Automatic transfers and mutualization are "not politically implementable" as such.
- Collective action clauses and ESM seniority turn euro-area sovereign bonds into speculative assets like developing-country debt. "Why build the euro area if only to get into such a situation?"
- No member has to repay its debt. It has only to stabilize it relative to GDP.
- OMT worked without being used because it was unlimited. The decrease in risk premia "remains fragile".

## Anti-patterns
- **Rules that assume a zero multiplier**: the Fiscal Pact and the redemption pact both ignore the output effect of generalized tightening.
- **Numerical thresholds (3%, 60%, 90%)**: "arbitrary". Ten of the original members breached 60% in 2013, and seven members were above 90% at end 2013.
- **Revising potential output after the fact**: the OECD measured a zero euro-area output gap for 2007 in June 2008, then a +3.3% gap in autumn 2012.
- **Criticizing deficits but not surpluses**: until November 2013 the Commission did not question German and Luxembourg surpluses, and then only "shyly".
- **Coexisting guaranteed and unguaranteed debt**: every two-tier scheme lets markets speculate on the unguaranteed part.

## Worked Example
The authors' structural-balance recalculation (Table 8.3, 2012):
1. Commission view: euro-area output gap -2.3%, potential growth 0.5% a year, structural balance -2.3% of GDP. All countries except Germany must tighten.
2. Authors' alternative: assume the crisis did not lower potential growth. The output gap is then about -11 points of GDP.
3. Target a structural primary balance of zero, which stabilizes debt if the interest rate does not exceed nominal growth.
4. Assume half of the lost output is recoverable. Recomputed structural primary balances: Germany 3.0, Italy 5.3, Greece 5.6, France 0.0, Spain -0.7, Ireland -1.2.
5. Conclusion: only Spain and Ireland need further effort. Germany, Italy and Greece run "excessive structural balance surpluses".

## Key Takeaways
1. Check the pre-crisis stability gap before calling a debt crisis fiscal in origin.
2. A debt path depends on the rate-growth gap more than on the primary balance. The periphery still paid rates well above nominal growth in 2013.
3. Output gap estimates drive every structural measure and are revised heavily. Test conclusions under a second potential-output assumption.
4. Any scheme that splits debt into guaranteed and unguaranteed parts keeps a speculative segment alive.
5. The legal position of OMT and the German court limit how far a guarantee can go.
6. The authors' bottom line is conditional: either open policy cooperation with a full guarantee, or the single currency fails.

# Chapter 13: European Banking Union as a Response to the Fragmentation of the Internal Market Resulting from the Financial and Sovereign Debt Crisis

**Contributors**: Dimitris Tsibanoulis with Gerry Kounadis (Tsibanoulis & Partners, Athens). Part II. A legal and institutional chapter. Written in 2014, after the SRM agreement of 20 March 2014 and before the ECB took up supervision.

## Core Idea
EU law integrated financial markets but left supervision, resolution and deposit protection national, and gave the euro no crisis tools. In the crisis that design fragmented the single market: funding costs came to depend on a firm's country, not its fundamentals. The Banking Union (single supervisor, single resolution mechanism and fund, common rules) partly fills the gaps and breaks two loops, sovereign-bank and deposit-bank. The authors say a third loop, between austerity and debt sustainability, remains unaddressed and needs growth tools and, in the end, Treaty change.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Minimum harmonization, single passport, home-country control**: the pre-crisis model. Directives harmonized only what was needed for (1) mutual recognition of authorization and supervision, (2) a single licence valid across the EU, (3) prudential supervision by the home member state. Market integration was "not followed by any considerable increase in supervisory integration".

- **Financial trilemma**: financial stability, cross-border financial integration and national authority over financial policy cannot all hold. Any two can be combined. Before the crisis Europe kept integration and national authority, and stability "slipped between the cracks". Corollary: the more cross-border business there is, the more authorities are forced towards supranational institutions.

- **Three negative loops**:
  1. Sovereign-bank: insolvent banks cause sovereign insolvency and the reverse. Banking crisis first in Ireland and Cyprus. Sovereign crisis first in Greece.
  2. Deposit-bank: redenomination risk from legal uncertainty about euro exit drives deposits from periphery to core.
  3. Austerity-debt sustainability: one-sided austerity in recession creates a deflationary spiral and shrinks GDP, raising debt ratios. Not yet addressed.

- **Fragmentation mechanism** (currency-risk uncertainty plus no common deposit guarantee):
  1. Deposits move from weak to strong members, raising recapitalization needs in the weak.
  2. Banks depend on state aid, and the real economy lacks finance.
  3. Banks do not liquidate collateral at depressed prices, which deepens recession.
  4. Austerity deepens the spiral given structural inefficiencies.
  5. The bank-sovereign-recession circle tightens.
  - Outcome: "country of origin" risk overrides the merits of the business. A firm pays a premium for its registered seat.

- **"Sovereign debt crisis paradox"**: creditor states' efforts to limit their own exposure to proposals such as the ESM produced the larger reforms. Direct ESM recapitalization of banks required a single supervisor first, which triggered the Banking Union.

- **"Community of interests"**: if the ESM invests in banks directly, strong states gain when the weak economy recovers. Lending to the sovereign for recapitalization does the opposite: it raises the recipient's public debt and gives lenders no stake in growth.

- **Bank safety net, five elements**: (1) single supervisory mechanism; (2) single resolution authority; (3) single resolution fund; (4) single deposit guarantee scheme, possibly merged with resolution in a European Deposit Insurance and Resolution Authority (EDIRA) modelled on the US FDIC; (5) a single rule book with full harmonization.

- **Resolution vs ordinary insolvency**: insolvency maximizes asset value for creditors and can be slow. Resolution aims at fast handling to preserve financial stability, protect critical functions (deposits, payments), limit taxpayer cost, and allocate losses to shareholders and creditors in a defined hierarchy, with results similar to insolvency for loss allocation.

## Key Concepts
- **SSM**: Single Supervisory Mechanism, the ECB plus national supervisors (Council Regulation (EU) No 1024/2013 of 15 October 2013).
- **SRM / SRB / SRF**: Single Resolution Mechanism, its Board, and its Fund.
- **BRRD**: Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive, agreed 11 December 2013, the common rule book for all 28 EU countries.
- **ESFS**: European System of Financial Supervision, formed after the de Larosiere Report (2009): the ESRB plus three authorities (EBA, ESMA, EIOPA), all based on secondary law.
- **European passport**: the single licence recognized across the EU.
- **Pringle judgment**: the ECJ ruling that the Treaties do not preclude the ESM Treaty. The ESM falls within economic policy.
- **Article 136(3) TFEU**: the one Treaty amendment made, providing the basis for the ESM.
- **Chinese walls**: the required separation of the ECB's supervisory and monetary functions (Article 27(2) of the SSM Regulation).
- **Too big to fail / too many to fail**: cases where a deposit guarantee scheme cannot pay from its own means.
- **Teleological interpretation**: purposive reading of the Treaties, used by the ECB and Commission to act.

## Mental Models
- "European regulation with national supervision" (Padoa-Schioppa) describes the pre-2014 system. Its lack of coordination was costly in the crisis.
- Home-country control "turned out to be a pitfall in times of crisis": national supervisors raised firewalls and encouraged banks to hold domestic debt.
- The crisis was "zero-sum": it damaged some members severely and benefited others massively, which strained solidarity.
- Price stability is not financial stability. The authors define financial stability as price stability plus full employment and argue the Treaties confine the ECB to the first, unlike the Federal Reserve.
- The Banking Union is "neutral" for convergence. Better supervision does not produce growth by itself.
- Risk was underpriced in the euro's first ten years and should not now be overpriced by tight one-sided conditions.

## Anti-patterns
- **Recapitalizing banks through loans to the sovereign**: raises public debt and provides no growth incentive for lenders.
- **Unilateral national fixes**: countries raised deposit guarantee ceilings on their own and ring-fenced liquidity.
- **Relying on national deposit guarantee schemes in a systemic failure**: Iceland is the example given.
- **Talking about euro exit without any legal framework for it**: deepened recession and caused deposit flight.
- **Internal devaluation in imbalanced economies**: the authors say it "failed because [it] shattered domestic demand" and cut tax income.
- **Ad hoc patching of Treaty gaps**: intergovernmental agreements and creative interpretation instead of durable rules.

SRM and resolution funding:

| Feature | Rule as stated |
|---|---|
| Scope | All euro-area banks and banks of other members that opt in |
| Decision chain | ECB notifies that a bank is failing. The Board adopts a resolution scheme. The Commission checks state aid rules and resolution objectives. The Council decides only if the Commission changes the fund amount or contests the public interest. National authorities implement |
| Legal basis | An SRM regulation plus an intergovernmental agreement on the fund |

## Worked Example
How direct recapitalization changes incentives, as the authors argue it:
1. Old route: EFSF and IMF lend to the member state. The state funds a national stability fund, which recapitalizes banks. Public debt rises. Austerity follows. Lenders have no interest in the borrower's growth.
2. June 2012 route: once a single supervisor exists, the ESM may invest in banks directly.
3. If the weak economy recovers, bank share values and the ESM's stake rise. Creditor-state taxpayers share the upside.
4. So it is "no longer in Europe's interest to pursue policies of depreciation in weak member states' economies".
5. What was actually agreed, as the authors record: Germany, the Netherlands and Finland insisted on private capital first, then national public capital, and the ESM only as a last resort. The final structure is centralized and intergovernmental, has minimal pooling, is built up over time, excludes smaller banks, "does not include direct bank recapitalization from the ESM", and has a large bail-in element. All key core-country demands were met.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat country-of-incorporation risk as a priced factor for euro-area banks and firms until resolution and deposit insurance are truly common.
2. The sovereign-bank link weakens only as far as loss absorption is mutualized. A EUR 55 bn fund built over eight years with national compartments is a partial answer.
3. Deposit guarantee remains national, so redenomination-driven deposit flight is only partly addressed.
4. Watch ECJ interpretation. The authors expect further "creative flexibility" in monetary and economic policy cases.
5. Reforms arrive when creditor states need protection for their own exposure.
6. The authors' closing condition: without growth and convergence tools beside the Banking Union, "the vicious circle of tight monetary and fiscal policy cannot be broken", leading "ultimately to the disintegration of the euro".

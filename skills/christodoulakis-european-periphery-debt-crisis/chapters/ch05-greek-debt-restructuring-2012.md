# Chapter 5: The 2012 Greek Debt Restructuring and its Aftermath

**Contributor**: Miranda Xafa (E.F. Consulting, Athens; former IMF board member). Part I. Written in 2014: official debt relief talks are "scheduled for the autumn of 2014".

## Core Idea
The March 2012 exchange was the largest sovereign restructuring in history and the first in the euro area. It was necessary and it delivered large debt relief in an orderly way. It did not deliver debt sustainability, because the adjustment programme derailed and GDP collapsed. Xafa's contested judgment: an earlier restructuring would probably not have restored sustainability either, and was not politically feasible. Uncertainty about how sustainability would be handled was the main source of contagion.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Trade-off: debt sustainability up front vs contagion, bank solvency and reform doubts**. The frame for when to restructure. The official side delayed because of (a) euro-area bank exposure, (b) contagion to other periphery sovereigns, (c) ECB refusal to take defaulted collateral, (d) doubts about Greek reform resolve.

- **PSI design (March 2012)**. A single-option offer with a 90% acceptance threshold:
  1. Eligible claims: EUR 205 bn (EUR 177 bn Greek law, EUR 28 bn foreign law).
  2. Per 100 of old face value: new discount bonds with face value 31.5, plus 15 in short-term EFSF notes (the "credit enhancement"), plus a detachable GDP-linked warrant. Face-value loss: 53.5%.
  3. New bonds: English law, maturities of 10 to 30 years (2023-2042), step-up coupon from 2% averaging 3.85%, maximum 4.3% in 2021. No cross-default clauses with the old foreign-law bonds.
  4. Retrofit collective action clauses (CACs) by act of parliament to Greek-law bonds. A supermajority above 66.7% binds all holders.

- **Local-law advantage**: debt in domestic currency under domestic law cannot be inflated away inside the euro, but the sovereign can change terms by statute. Greece chose retrofitted CACs over a direct statutory change, which would have looked like expropriation and been open to challenge.

- **Official sector involvement (OSI), November 2012**: defer EFSF interest by a decade; cut the Greek Loan Facility margin from 150 bp to 50 bp; cancel the EFSF guarantee fee; extend EFSF and GLF maturities to 30 years; pass ECB SMP income to Greece from 2013. Phased and conditional. Expected effect: EUR 8 bn of financing in 2013-16 and a debt stock 7% of GDP lower by 2020.

- **Debt buyback by reverse auction (December 2012)**: buy new GGBs at the secondary-market discount, paid in six-month EFSF notes.

- **Gross vs net debt relief**: subtract the recapitalization cost of domestic banks hit by the haircut from the face-value relief.

## Key Concepts
- **PSI**: private sector involvement in debt relief.
- **OSI**: official sector involvement, relief on official loans.
- **CAC**: collective action clause, letting a defined majority amend coupon, maturity or face value for all holders.
- **Holdouts**: holders of series where the CAC majority (typically 75% per series under foreign law) was not reached. Greece is repaying them in full to avoid cross-default.
- **SMP**: the ECB's Securities Market Programme, May 2010 to February 2012, about EUR 50 bn of GGBs bought, treated as senior and excluded from the PSI.
- **OMT**: Outright Monetary Transactions. Differs from SMP by conditionality and by having no seniority over private holders.
- **Restructuring Credit Event**: the ISDA ruling of 9 March 2012 that triggered CDS.
- **Debt re-profiling**: the IMF's 2014 proposal to condition support on a maturity extension when market access is lost and sustainability is uncertain.
- **Extend and pretend**: Xafa's label for the treatment of legacy official debt.

## Mental Models
- Delay moves the debt from private to official hands. Privately held bonds fell from EUR 268 bn (March 2010) to EUR 206 bn by the PSI, redeemed with official loans. Banks unloaded to the ECB and to speculative investors.
- After restructuring, the creditor mix decides where further relief can come from. At end 2013 only EUR 34 bn of EUR 321 bn was owed to private bondholders. Defaulting on the new bonds (11% of gross debt) would save less than 1% of GDP a year.
- A haircut on bonds held by domestic banks is partly circular. Greek banks held nearly a third of GGBs.
- Post-exchange bond prices reveal residual default risk. New Greek bonds traded far below par, pricing a second default with recovery near 25 cents, unlike most exchanges where new bonds trade near par.
- Announcing that restructuring is possible, without a firewall, widens spreads across peers (Deauville, October 2010).

## Anti-patterns
- **A haircut too small for the DSA**: the July 2011 deal (21% NPV haircut proposed by bondholders) could not restore sustainability and was never implemented.
- **Insisting on "voluntary" terms to avoid a CDS trigger**: CDS triggered anyway. Net outstanding contracts were reportedly EUR 2.5 bn, so the ECB's fear "proved exaggerated".
- **Unequal treatment within the official sector**: the ECB and euro-area central banks were exempt, yet the People's Bank of China took the private haircut.
- **Adjustment through taxes and investment cuts**: the programme relied on tax increases instead of public consumption cuts and cut public investment "to the bone".
- **No pre-built crisis institution**: the EFSF and ESM were created only after the first Greek package.

## Worked Example
Debt relief arithmetic.

Exchange
1. Tendered: 86% of Greek-law and 69% of foreign-law bonds, short of 90%.
2. CACs lift Greek-law participation to 100%. Accepted: EUR 177 bn Greek law + EUR 21 bn foreign law (75%) = EUR 198 bn. Holdouts: EUR 6.4 bn.
3. Gross relief: 198 x 53.5% = EUR 106 bn, 55% of 2012 GDP.
4. Greek bank losses: EUR 38 bn, recapitalized with official money.
5. Net relief: 106 - 38 = EUR 68 bn, 35% of GDP.

Buyback
1. EUR 62 bn of new bonds outstanding. EUR 32 bn tendered and accepted.
2. Cost: EUR 11 bn. Weighted average price 33.8 cents.
3. Relief: 32 - 11 = EUR 21 bn, 11% of GDP. Greek banks supplied about half of the bonds though they held about a quarter. Pension funds (EUR 7 bn) stayed out.

Total net relief: 35% + 11% = 46% of GDP. Outcome: the debt ratio at end 2013 was 175% of GDP, above end 2011, because GDP fell. Greece nonetheless reached a small primary surplus in 2013.

Counterfactual of restructuring in May 2010: an extra 53.5% x EUR 58 bn = EUR 31 bn, 17% of GDP. Xafa judges this would not have made the debt sustainable.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compute relief net of domestic bank recapitalization.
2. Check the creditor mix after any restructuring. When official holders dominate, further relief means maturity and rate changes unless official haircuts become possible. Here they "have been ruled out".
3. A restructuring cannot fix a ratio whose denominator keeps falling.
4. Local-law debt plus retrofitted CACs gives a sovereign strong leverage. Foreign-law series with per-series CACs produce holdouts.
5. The key to contagion control is a credible solution to sovereign distress plus a firewall, not a ban on restructuring.
6. Xafa's verdict in one line: "too little, too late", yet delay "beyond the spring of 2011 ... just added to Greece's debt burden".

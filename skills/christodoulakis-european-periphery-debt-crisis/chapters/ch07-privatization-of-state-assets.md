# Chapter 7: Privatization of State Assets in the Presence of Crisis

**Contributor**: George Christodoulakis (Greece's Secretary of State and Chief Executive for Asset Restructuring and Privatizations, late 2009 to early 2012; the volume's editor). Part I. Parts of the chapter are a first-person account ("In my view").

## Core Idea
Privatization is a reform tool, "not a panacea". Politics, not economic rationality, decides how privatization plans are built. For bailout countries it became a creditor condition. In Greece the troika raised the revenue target from EUR 3 bn to EUR 50 bn to plug the gap left by its own failed macro forecast, with a public timetable. The plan was unrealistic for a portfolio of infrastructure, monopoly stakes and real estate, and it underperformed massively.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Privatization as a "weighted introduction of private interests, capital and expertise"**. The public-private interest trade-off selects the mode:
  - full freehold sale (no residual state interest);
  - partial IPO or secondary public offering (SPO);
  - strategic investor with management rights (used for greenfield infrastructure);
  - concession agreement (state keeps long-term ownership, private party operates for a finite period, typically 20-30 years in Greece).

- **Five benefit channels of a well-structured plan**: (1) deficit and debt relief from selling loss-making or indebted state firms; (2) foreign capital and expertise, integration into value chains; (3) sovereign credibility signal, lower borrowing costs; (4) better private-sector funding conditions; (5) GDP growth with second-round effects on debt ratio and risk.

- **Three regimes of privatization policy in the euro area**, by indebtedness and financial sovereignty:
  1. purely domestic policy (Germany, France);
  2. firm advice from international organizations such as the IMF (Italy);
  3. bailout conditionality (Greece, Portugal, Ireland).

- **Troika negotiation strategy (author's observation from inside)**: (1) nothing is agreed until everything is agreed; (2) technical talks may run as long as the government wants; (3) positions on substantive targets never change; (4) only implementation fine-tuning is allowed; (5) substance is revised only against hard quantitative facts. Time works for the creditor: financing needs force the government to agree at the end of each review round. Reviews occur three to four times a year and gate each disbursement.

- **Why the EUR 50 bn plan was unrealistic**:
  1. The asset portfolio (mainly infrastructure and real estate) could not yield that revenue in five years.
  2. A public, restrictive timetable removes the seller's strategic freedom and hands it to buyers, especially for listed assets.
  3. Key assets need expert studies, DG Competition clearance, legislation, regulation and often public consultation before the transaction structure is known.
  - Result: implementation risk, mismanaged market expectations, lost credibility.

- **Agency model vs political reality**: moving ownership to a privatization fund (the Hellenic Republic Asset Development Fund, HRADF, modelled on Germany's Treuhandanstalt) does not remove politics when the assets shape market structure. The author calls the transfer "essentially redundant".

- **Privatization asset-class performance** (Christodoulakis et al. 2014): global portfolio of exchange-traded privatized assets, 1985-2012, split into strategic vs non-strategic and cyclical vs non-cyclical. Portfolios that dominate the market benchmark mostly overweight non-cyclical and non-strategic assets, with some rotation over the business cycle. The author notes this is partly in contrast with the expectations that guide policy advice.

## Key Concepts
- **Troika**: the official creditors, defined in note 1 as the EU, the ECB and the IMF.
- **ICARP / SSARP**: Interministerial Committee for Asset Restructuring and Privatizations and its executive Secretariat (law 3049/2002). SSARP structures and executes, ICARP decides.
- **HRADF**: the fund that took irreversible ownership of assets before sale. Established September 2011, operational February 2012.
- **Treuhandanstalt**: the agency that privatized 8,500 East German enterprises and about 2.4 million hectares of land. It closed in 1994 with a EUR 170 billion loss against an expected EUR 900 billion profit.
- **Concession agreement**: transfer of exploitation rights for a finite period.
- **Prior actions**: measures required before a disbursement.
- **Politically easy privatization revenues**: proceeds from sales that keep state control.

## Mental Models
- "Economic time was captured by political time." Delay in 2009-2010 cost Greece its few degrees of freedom with creditors.
- A privatization target can be a residual of a macro model. When the fiscal gap widens, the target is raised to close it on paper.
- A process can be technically efficient and still "totally moribund" without political will.
- Three reactions to the same conditionality: Portugal complied and over-performed, Ireland "is still making efforts", Greece went through a long, inconclusive internal political process.
- Demand for privatized assets is rational even if supply is political. Investors require risk premiums that vary with market development, legal system and government type.

## Anti-patterns
- **Publishing a tight sale timetable**: it shifts bargaining power to buyers.
- **Treating implementability as a technical issue**: troika teams asked whether infrastructure assets could be privatized within six months.
- **Accelerated mass sales**: the Treuhand precedent closed with a large loss.
- **Ad hoc transactions without a state asset management plan**: Greece in the early 1990s.
- **Assuming an independent fund depoliticizes sales**: the HRADF board changed with every government, and processes stopped under political uncertainty.

Stylized facts from the literature (sect. 4): politics determine plan structure; privatization improves company performance; it benefits the wider economy (market development, liquidity, FDI); investors require rational risk premiums (higher initial returns in less developed capital markets and regulated sectors; higher trade-sale returns under civil law and left-wing governments; higher returns on bailed-out assets); privatization portfolios tend to dominate market portfolios (Megginson et al. 2000, 33 countries).

## Worked Example
The Greek target escalation, as narrated:
1. October 2009: the new government inherits a portfolio where further sales mean handing over management control. It drafts Greece's first structured state asset plan, including real estate, aimed at sector restructuring instead of maximum fast revenue. Internal disagreement slows it.
2. May 2010: bailout signed. Target about EUR 3 bn, reflecting the troika's optimistic macro scenario.
3. February 2011: data show the economy is not responding and the political class is not delivering reforms. The troika unilaterally proposes EUR 50 bn. The government counters with EUR 15 bn based on asset facts. Weak political equilibria end the round at EUR 50 bn with a public timetable.
4. Mid-2011: agreement to create the HRADF. September 2011 established, February 2012 operational, SSARP dissolved.
5. Afterwards: targets are revised down repeatedly to a projected total below EUR 20 bn. The HRADF law is amended to return responsibility for sales to parliament.
6. Author's verdict: the evidence is not against the HRADF, "a professionally run organization", but against a policy mindset that ignored political and market realities.

## Key Takeaways
1. Discount privatization revenue assumptions in a programme when the asset mix is infrastructure, monopoly stakes and real estate.
2. Compare the target with the country's historical proceeds. Greece raised EUR 23 billion in about two decades and was asked for EUR 50 bn in five years.
3. Treat a sudden increase in a privatization target as a signal that the macro baseline has failed.
4. Expect privatization pipelines to stall around elections and government changes.
5. For investors, non-cyclical and non-strategic privatized assets carried the better risk-adjusted record in the cited study.
6. Creditor leverage rises with the debtor's financing calendar. Slow internal politics transfers bargaining power.

# Chapter 3: Ireland and Greece: A Tale of Two Fiscal Adjustments

**Contributors**: Jeffrey D. Anderson and Jessica Stallings (Institute of International Finance). Part I. Written about 2013: the text says "three years into Europe's crisis" and treats Ireland's programme exit in December 2013 as still ahead.

## Core Idea
Ireland and Greece differ less in deficits and debt accumulation than commonly thought. They differ in growth. Greece's much faster fiscal consolidation, hitting an economy with a fiscal multiplier at least twice Ireland's, shrank the denominator of the debt ratio faster than deficit cuts slowed the numerator. A slower, back-loaded adjustment (the Irish example) would cost less in the end.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Growth decomposition: exports, fiscal headwind, other** (Table 3.2).
  - How:
    1. Export effect = export volume growth x the share of domestic value-added in exports relative to GDP (OECD estimates: about 55% of GDP for Ireland, 15% for Greece, 2010-2012).
    2. Fiscal headwind = change in the cyclically adjusted primary balance (net of one-offs such as bank recapitalization, debt assumption, ESM contributions) x the aggregate fiscal multiplier.
    3. Other = residual.
  - When to use: attributing an output path under an adjustment programme, and comparing two programme countries.

- **Fiscal multiplier differential**. Studies cited suggest 1-1.5 for Greece and no more than 0.5 for Ireland. The authors compute with 1.0 and 0.4.
  - Why Greece's is larger: lower import share, so domestic spending changes fall more on domestic activity. More economic slack (larger negative output gap). The widening gap suggests the multiplier rose over time.
  - No monetary offset in either country: wider sovereign risk premia and extra charges on banks (Irish liability-guarantee fees, Greek ELA access and guarantee charges) more than offset ECB easing since 2009.

- **Denominator effect on the debt ratio**. Fiscal consolidation acts on debt/GDP through (a) a smaller deficit (numerator), (b) lower nominal GDP (denominator), (c) lower cyclical revenue and interest (numerator). In Greece the headwind added about 11 percentage points a year to debt/GDP in 2010-2012 (8.4 denominator, 2.6 numerator via revenue and interest), 10 points more than in Ireland.

- **The Irish example applied to Greece (policy proposal)**:
  1. Spread the remaining adjustment over a longer period and remove front-loading.
  2. Use the fiscal space, plus unused EU structural funds, for public investment. High multipliers work in favour here.
  3. Continue product-market liberalization and privatization.
  4. Finance the stretch with further cuts in official-credit interest rates, needed only until growth returns.
  5. Give less weight to end-of-decade debt/GDP projections. Let the ratio fall through the denominator.

## Key Concepts
- **Fiscal headwind**: annual change in the cyclically adjusted primary balance, the demand withdrawal from consolidation.
- **Cyclically adjusted primary balance**: primary balance net of cyclical effects and one-offs.
- **Domestic value-added in exports**: the export share that counts for GDP. It scales the export effect.
- **Relative unit labour costs**: the competitiveness measure used. Down 19% in Ireland and 20% in Greece from 2009 to 2012 Q3.
- **Negative buoyancy**: tax revenue lost because the new measures themselves depress activity (EUR 775 million for Ireland's 2012 package).
- **Carryover**: the next-year yield of a measure enacted this year. The appendices itemize it for every package.
- **ELA**: Exceptional Liquidity Assistance from the national central bank, as the text names it.

## Mental Models
- Similar competitiveness gains produce very different growth when export exposure differs. Economy-wide unit labour cost gains were nearly equal, yet Irish export volumes grew about 5% a year and Greek about 1%.
- Where the wage cut lands matters. Ireland cut private wages and left public wages little changed (pre-programme union agreements). Greece cut public wages as much as or more than private ones.
- Starting buffers buy a slower path. Ireland had smaller bond maturities to refinance, cash reserves and assets, so creditors accepted gradualism. Greece had no assets to draw on.
- Achievable targets preserve political support, and steady implementation revives bond demand. "These targets were achievable; but those set for Greece, in contrast, were not."

## Anti-patterns
- **Front-loaded consolidation in a high-multiplier economy**: partly counterproductive for the debt ratio.
- **Chasing headline deficit targets**: revenue weakness causes a miss, which triggers demands for more measures.
- **Assuming competitiveness gains turn into exports**: Greek exports were held back by lost tourism (demonstrations, travel disruption) and by loss of trade finance (bank liquidity stress, partners demanding cash).
- **Judging Ireland as far less indebted**: without drawing down assets for bank recapitalization, Ireland's ratio would have reached 130% of GDP, the level at which Greece began its programme in 2010.

Pre-crisis comparison (sect. 4): inward FDI 2000-2011 averaged 7.0% of GDP a year in Ireland and 0.8% in Greece. Growth averaged 6.6% a year in Ireland in the ten years before 2007 and 4.0% in Greece over 1997-2007. Employment grew 4.2% a year versus 1.5%. Labour productivity grew 2.3% versus 2.5%, TFP 1.7% versus 1.8%. Fixed capital formation 2000-2007 was 24% versus 23% of GDP.

Debt accumulation 2010-2012: Ireland +53 points of GDP (bank recapitalization 20% of GDP in 2010, another 12% covered by asset drawdown). Greece +27 points, which would have been +68 without the March 2012 exchange and December 2012 buyback. Greek bank recapitalization beyond the haircut losses was EUR 17 billion, 9% of GDP.

## Worked Example
The authors' arithmetic for 2010-2012, annual averages:

Ireland
1. Change in cyclically adjusted primary balance: 1.7% of GDP a year.
2. Multiplier 0.4, so fiscal drag is about -0.7% of GDP a year ("almost 0.75%").
3. Exports add 2.5% a year (5% volume growth x about 55% value-added share).
4. Residual other factors: -1.3. Total: +0.5% growth.

Greece
1. Change in cyclically adjusted primary balance: 5.6% of GDP a year, more than three times Ireland's.
2. Multiplier 1.0, so fiscal drag is about -5.6% a year.
3. Exports add 0.3% a year (about 1% volume growth x about 15% share).
4. Residual other factors: -0.8. Total: -6.1% growth.

Debt consequence for Greece: about 11 points a year added to debt/GDP from the headwind. Three years of contraction added more than 30 points to the ratio, of which at least six came from cyclical revenue loss and the rest from the falling denominator. Outlook as written: another 7.4% of GDP of measures in 2013-2014, real GDP down a further 4-5% in 2013, adding 9-10 points to the ratio.

The appendices list every Irish package from July 2008 to 2013 and every Greek package from 2010 to 2013 with euro amounts. Example contrasts: Ireland's 2011 package was EUR 6 billion, mostly spending. Greece's 2011 measures reached EUR 16.7 billion and 2012 measures EUR 13.2 billion, including a cut of the income tax-free threshold from EUR 12,000 to EUR 5,000 and a 20% nominal public wage cut.

## Key Takeaways
1. Decompose programme-country growth into export effect, fiscal headwind and residual before blaming structure.
2. Estimate the headwind as the change in cyclically adjusted primary balance times a country-specific multiplier. Raise the multiplier for closed economies with large output gaps.
3. In a high-debt country the denominator effect dominates. Check whether consolidation raises the ratio.
4. Export exposure, measured as domestic value-added in exports over GDP, decides how much an internal devaluation pays.
5. Influences other than exports explain two-thirds of the Ireland-Greece growth gap, and fiscal pace is the largest.
6. Credit supply is a separate brake: Greek bank credit contracted under deposit flight and tight ECB collateral rules, while Irish banks repaid the ECB.

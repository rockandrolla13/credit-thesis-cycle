# Chapter 11: Funding Risks for Corporates in the Periphery: Disintermediation to the Rescue for the Larger Ones, Challenges for the Others

**Contributor**: Blaise Ganguin (Head of Standard & Poor's Corporate and Infrastructure Ratings, EMEA). Part II. The views are stated to be the author's, not S&P's. Data to early 2014.

## Core Idea
Periphery corporates were over 90% bank-funded, like the rest of Europe. Bank deleveraging after 2007-2009 cut that supply. Large, rated firms escaped by issuing bonds and performed much like European peers. Small, bank-dependent firms had no alternative, and insolvencies multiplied. Ratings matter because they open non-bank funding. The SME funding gap is "the unanswered question". This is a short, descriptive chapter with most of its evidence in charts that did not survive extraction.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Two-universe reading of credit distress**: compare rated-company default rates with national insolvency statistics.
  - Rated firms (large national champions with capital-market access): defaults in Italy and Spain were higher than the rest of Europe by between 5 and 10% and followed the same time pattern (peak 2009, sharp decline 2010-2011, rise again 2012-2013).
  - All firms (national insolvency counts, mostly bank-funded): against 2006 levels, insolvencies rose 20-40% in the UK, France and Italy, declined in Germany, tripled in Greece, quadrupled in Portugal and rose eightfold in Spain.
  - When to use: to separate a funding-access problem from a general solvency problem. A wide gap between the two series points to a bank-dependence problem among smaller firms.

- **Why large firms cope better**: (1) less reliance on the home economy (exports or foreign operations act as a natural hedge); (2) diversified funding sources.

- **Corporate "sudden stop"**: a firm funded only by banks becomes insolvent almost at once if the bank calls its loan, whatever the reason. The same mechanism as for sovereigns.

- **Rated vs unrated funding terms** (Prof. Amrit Judge, Middlesex University; western European non-financial firms, 2000-2011; about 45,000 data points for unrated firms and about 3,000 for rated firms):
  1. Firms remain mainly bank-financed. UK, Swiss and Dutch firms are furthest along in disintermediation. Spanish and Italian firms lag.
  2. Rated firms have far more diversified funding (bonds and commercial paper). Few unrated firms issue bonds and almost none use commercial paper.
  3. In stress, rated firms substitute market funding for bank debt. In 2008-2009 bond issuance rose while net bank lending was negative.
  4. All firms cut capital spending in 2008-2011. Those with bond access cut less.
  5. Rated firms have longer maturities. Banks rarely lend beyond five years outside infrastructure. Ten years and more is common in capital markets.
  6. Bank loan costs rose less for rated firms in the crisis, because of bargaining power.
  - Note 6 says a control group shows the causality runs one way (from rating to access).

- **Disintermediation thesis**: Europe's high yield market should in time at least match the US market given the economies' sizes. Italian and Spanish issuers, and by extension Greek and Portuguese ones, are expected to lead, because of pressure on their banking systems.

- **SME funding disconnect**: investors want mid-market exposure, but family firms do not want market funding. Three reasons given: (1) the group is heterogeneous, from micro firms to BMW; (2) non-bank alternatives in Europe are in their infancy; (3) owners are wary of sharing information outside their bank relationship.

## Key Concepts
- **Disintermediation**: replacing bank loans with capital-market funding.
- **GIPS**: Greece, Italy, Portugal, Spain, the group used in the chapter's charts.
- **Non-investment grade / high yield / speculative grade**: the rating segment where almost all new European ratings appeared.
- **Information asymmetry**: the gap between what borrower and creditor know. The chapter treats ratings as the bridge.
- **Basel III / Solvency 2**: bank and insurer rules that raise the capital cost of long-term corporate lending.
- **Private placement**: non-public debt placement serving the mid-market.
- **SME loan securitization**: pooling SME loans, which was common in Italy, Spain and Portugal in the previous decade.
- **Corporate bond exchanges**: venues set up in the UK and Germany. The author knows of none in Greece, Portugal, Spain or Italy.

## Mental Models
- A concentrated banking system (Greece, Portugal) and a fragmented one (Spain, Italy) produced the same bank-heavy funding mix. Banking structure did not determine the model.
- Rising corporate cash balances signal doubt about future funding. Southern European treasurers accumulated cash while balances elsewhere stayed stable.
- A stable 20% periphery share of European bond issuance over a decade means substantial real growth, because the total grew.
- High yield markets are "notoriously fickle". They shut in 1997-1998, 2001-2002 and 2008, and reopened each time.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging a country's corporate health from rated-firm data**: rated firms are the least exposed to the domestic economy and to bank funding cuts.
- **Single-source bank funding**: no fallback when the bank deleverages.
- **Keeping affairs private at any cost**: some owners prefer secrecy "at the risk of running out of financing options".
- **Assuming investor appetite solves the SME gap**: supply of capital exists, but demand from family firms for market funding does not.

## Worked Example
Named cases the author uses:
1. Titan Cement Co. S.A. (Greece): cement sales fell at home and in south-eastern Europe. Its sizeable US operations offset the decline. Illustrates the natural hedge.
2. Frigoglass S.A.I.C. (Greece, beverage coolers, initially rated BB-): issued EUR 250 mn of unsecured notes in May 2013 and refinanced most of its bank debt.
3. Intralot S.A. (Greece, gambling, rated B+ after refinancing): issued EUR 325 million of unsecured notes in August 2013 to refinance bank and market debt.
4. Almirall S.A. (Spain, pharmaceuticals, initially rated BB-): intended to issue EUR 325 mn of unsecured notes in March 2014 to repay a bridge loan after a US acquisition.

Pattern: a mid-sized periphery firm obtains a speculative-grade rating, issues unsecured notes, and replaces bank debt.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compare rated-firm defaults with economy-wide insolvencies. The gap measures how much stress is concentrated in bank-dependent small firms.
2. Access to bond markets is a buffer in bank stress: less investment cut, longer maturities, cheaper bank loans.
3. Expect periphery issuers to take a growing share of European high yield as their banks stay constrained.
4. Treat high yield access as cyclical. It can close in a systemic shock.
5. The SME gap has no market solution yet. Securitization revival and bond exchanges are early-stage.
6. Corporate cash hoarding is an early indicator of perceived funding risk.

# Chapter 2: The Trade-Off between Fiscal and Competitiveness Adjustments

**Contributors**: Daniel Gros with Cinzia Alcidi (CEPS). Part I. An earlier version was prepared for the European Parliament's Economic Dialogue of 5 September 2013. Data "as of mid-2014".

## Core Idea
Peripheral countries are told to cut debt ratios and to restore competitiveness at once. Inside a monetary union, competitiveness needs an internal devaluation, which lowers nominal GDP and so raises the debt ratio. The EU's country-specific recommendations (CSRs) ignore this trade-off. Judge debt at the price level that would make the country competitive, not at today's prices.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Competitiveness-adjusted debt-to-GDP ratio** (stock view of debt deflation).
  - Formula (note 3, recovered from PDF p. 79; subscripts are small in the scan): the adjusted ratio of country i equals the headline ratio times the country's price level relative to the benchmark, `(D/Y)^a_i = (D_i / Y_i) x (P_i / P)`, where P_i is the country's GDP deflator index since the base year and P the euro-area benchmark deflator.
  - How:
    1. Choose a base year in which relative prices were at equilibrium. Gros and Alcidi use 1999: Germany then ran a small current account deficit and most of the periphery had small deficits or surpluses.
    2. Compute the change in each country's GDP deflator since the base year, relative to the benchmark (euro-area average). Use the GDP deflator, not unit labour costs, because government revenue scales with nominal GDP.
    3. Multiply the headline debt ratio by that adjustment factor.
  - When to use: any debt sustainability reading for a currency-union member that still has a price gap to close.
  - Sensitivity: base year 1995 (Sinn 2013, the year the commitment to the euro became irrevocable) and benchmark Germany instead of the area average both raise periphery ratios. The authors say the 1995 base overestimates the needed adjustment.

- **Relative debt deflation / debt inflation**: the gap between adjusted and headline ratios. Germany "enjoys debt inflation". As long as the euro area has no deflation on average, debt deflation is a relative concept inside the area, and it reverses the relative debt inflation the periphery enjoyed before 2008.

- **Flow view: growth-interest differential**. Compare nominal GDP growth with the interest rate actually paid. Applied to governments (ten-year yields, Table 2.4) and to non-financial corporations (new medium-term loans of 1-5 years, Table 2.5). A negative differential means interest accrues faster than income grows.

- **Fisher debt-deflation loop, SME version**: fiscal adjustment plus falling domestic prices -> lower revenue for firms selling at home (mostly small service firms) -> higher default probability -> bank losses -> higher loan rates and less credit to SMEs -> weaker domestic demand. The loop runs in reverse in Germany.

- **CSR critique (sect. 3)**: fiscal recommendations carry numeric targets. Structural ones are vague exhortations. Strong countries ignore them. Weak countries respond with many measures whose results cannot be judged. Open question the authors raise: judge compliance on outcomes (as in the SGP) or on effort?

## Key Concepts
- **European Semester**: annual cycle ending in early July with Council endorsement of the CSRs.
- **CSRs**: country-specific recommendations.
- **Internal devaluation**: a fall in domestic prices and wages relative to partners.
- **Debt deflation (Fisher 1933)**: falling prices raise the real debt burden, forcing distress selling and lower demand, which lowers prices further.
- **Ponzi units (Minsky)**: borrowers who need new credit to pay debt service.
- **1/20 rule**: the debt reduction target of one-twentieth per year of the excess of the debt ratio over 60% of GDP.
- **Excessive imbalances procedure (EIP)**: the macro-imbalance arm beside the SGP.

## Mental Models
- A successful internal devaluation makes the headline debt ratio look worse. Official creditors "will have to recognize" that the more Greece lowers its price level, the higher its debt/GDP ratio.
- Past competitiveness losers tend to be today's high-debt countries, so the adjustment widens debt disparities. The standard deviation of debt ratios is 30 for headline ratios and 35 for adjusted ratios (note 4).
- Read austerity through firm size: domestic-services SMEs carry the revenue loss.

## Anti-patterns
- **Headline debt/GDP as the sustainability test**: it is static and ignores the price adjustment still to come.
- **Recommending fiscal and competitiveness adjustment together without acknowledging the bank and SME consequences**.
- **Vague structural recommendations**: "What country would not benefit from 'better coordination of different levels of government'?"
- **Asymmetric coordination**: Germany's Grosse Koalition introduced a minimum wage (EUR 8.5 per hour, over 3 million workers affected), pension at 63 for early starters, and rent limits. The authors say no peripheral country could have done this without a reprimand.

## Worked Example
Greece, base 1999, benchmark euro-area average:
1. Headline debt ratio 2013: 177% of GDP.
2. Greek deflator has risen more than the average. Adjustment factor 1.07.
3. Adjusted ratio: 177 x 1.07 = 190%.
4. With Germany as benchmark the figure is 228%. With the 1995 base it is 205%.
5. Reading: both the headline and the adjusted ratio "denote an unsustainable situation". Germany on the same method drops from 80% to 72%.

Italy: 133 x 1.05 = 139 in the table. The authors call the Italian case "manageable".

## Key Takeaways
1. Multiply headline debt/GDP by the relative deflator gap before judging sustainability.
2. Expect a headline ratio to worsen as an internal devaluation succeeds.
3. Track growth minus interest for both the sovereign and the corporate sector. After 2008 it worsened by about one point for euro-area governments and over two points for corporations.
4. The corporate squeeze is country-specific: no change for Germany, a swing of over 7 points for Spain.
5. Small domestic service firms and their banks are where combined austerity and price adjustment show up first.
6. Discount structural-reform recommendations that have no measurable target. Italy's governance indicators worsened over a decade of declared effort.

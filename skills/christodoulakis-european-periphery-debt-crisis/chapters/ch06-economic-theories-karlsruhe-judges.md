# Chapter 6: Economic Theories that Influenced the Judges of Karlsruhe

**Contributor**: Paul De Grauwe (London School of Economics). Part I. Written after the German Constitutional Court's preliminary ruling of 14 January 2014 (2 BvR 2728/13, cited as "BvR 2728/13") and before any reply from the European Court of Justice.

## Core Idea
The Karlsruhe court held that the ECB's OMT bond-buying programme is illegal under EU law and referred the case to the European Court of Justice, asking for conditions that would, in De Grauwe's view, make OMT useless. He argues the ruling rests on two economic theories that should be rejected: the efficient market theory and the belief that a central bank needs positive equity. This is a short argumentative chapter with no data.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Multiple equilibria in a monetary union's sovereign bond market** (De Grauwe 2011).
  - Mechanism: a government in a monetary union issues debt in a currency it does not control, so it cannot guarantee cash at maturity. A stand-alone government can, because its central bank is the ultimate backstop.
  - Bad equilibrium: fear of payment difficulty -> yields rise -> sudden stop in liquidity -> liquidity crisis -> default unless outside support arrives.
  - Good equilibrium: no fear -> low yields -> liquidity available.
  - The result holds with rational agents (Calvo 1988; Gros 2012; Corsetti and Dedola 2011).
  - When to use: deciding whether a spread move reflects fundamentals or a self-fulfilling liquidity run, and whether a central bank backstop is justified.

- **Lender of last resort in government bond markets**: multiple equilibria are "the single most important justification". The central bank needs to act only in the secondary market. Primary-market purchases would be illegal under EU law.

- **Rebuttal 1, the efficient market argument**. The court's reasoning: yields express the market's view of default risk, so buying bonds to lower yields overrides the market and is economic policy, outside the ECB's monetary mandate.
  - De Grauwe's answer: this assumes markets price default risk correctly. If markets were efficient there would be no need for any lender of last resort, for banks or sovereigns. By the court's own logic the ECB's bank liquidity support in 2008 and the LTROs of 2011-12 were also illegal, yet the court did not challenge them.

- **Rebuttal 2, the central bank equity argument**. The court's reasoning (influenced by Bundesbank advice): if a government defaults, the ECB loses equity and taxpayers must recapitalize it, which is taxation without parliamentary consent.
  - De Grauwe's answer, step by step:
    1. A central bank with the monopoly of money issue cannot default. Banknotes are not a claim on its assets. It can operate with negative equity. Its only constraint is its price stability promise.
    2. When the ECB buys Italian bonds in the secondary market, the Italian treasury pays the same interest as before, now to the ECB.
    3. The ECB distributes that interest to national central banks by capital share. Germany has the largest share. Italy becomes a net payer. No taxpayer pays more.
    4. If Italy defaults, the interest flow from Italy to the others stops, and the ECB writes down the bonds.
    5. If governments recapitalize the ECB for reputational reasons, they hand it bonds in proportion to capital shares, pay interest on them, and get the same interest back by the same shares. It is "a pure bookkeeping operation".
  - Principle: "A government that can default cannot possibly be a fiscal backup of a central bank that cannot default."
  - Caveat in note 3: if the bonds carry different interest rates, recapitalization implies transfers from high-rate to low-rate countries (such as Germany). A juste retour rule could offset this.

- **Separation principle for moral hazard**: the central bank provides liquidity in a crisis. The European Commission contains moral hazard through the strengthened Stability and Growth Pact.

## Key Concepts
- **OMT**: Outright Monetary Transactions, the ECB's conditional secondary-market government bond purchase programme.
- **Efficient market theory**: prices reflect all available information and are the best assessment of default probability.
- **Bad equilibrium**: self-fulfilling expectations producing a liquidity crisis. Draghi used the term at the 6 September 2012 press conference.
- **Liquidity backstop**: the guarantee that cash will be available at maturity.
- **Capital shares (capital key)**: the proportions in which ECB income is distributed and capital contributed.
- **Fiat money system**: one in which money is not a claim on central bank assets.
- **LTRO**: the ECB's long-term refinancing operations for banks.

## Mental Models
- Treat a euro-area sovereign as lacking a backstop unless the central bank has credibly promised one. The promise itself selects the good equilibrium.
- Ask which model a legal or policy argument presupposes. "The willingness to accept this role of the central bank ultimately depends on the kind of model adhered to."
- Follow the cash, not the accounting. Central bank bond purchases redistribute interest among treasuries. They do not create a tax.
- Empirical basis cited: spreads in 2010-12 were to a significant extent unrelated to debt ratios, external debt or competitiveness (Beirne and Fratzscher 2012; De Grauwe and Ji 2013; Gartner 2013).

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading a yield as an efficient default probability**: wrong in a market open to self-fulfilling runs.
- **Treating central bank equity as real**: observers are "bogged down in bookkeeping conventions" (note 4).
- **Confusing the real risks with the alleged one**: OMT's real risks are inflation and moral hazard, not forced taxation.
- **Applying the mandate argument selectively**: to sovereign bond purchases but not to bank liquidity support.

## Worked Example
The Italian bond case, as De Grauwe walks it:
1. Italy issues bonds through its own democratic process. Private investors hold them. Italy pays them interest.
2. The ECB buys the bonds in the secondary market. Italy pays the same interest to the ECB.
3. The ECB passes the interest to national central banks by capital share, and they pass it to treasuries. German, French and Dutch treasuries gain. Italy gets back only a fraction.
4. Italy defaults. The transfers from Italy stop. The ECB writes down the bonds and its equity falls.
5. Option A: do nothing. The ECB functions with lower or negative equity.
6. Option B: recapitalize. Each government gives bonds by capital share, pays interest on them to the ECB, and receives the same interest back. Net tax cost: zero. "Taxpayers would not be asked to pay one additional eurocent."

## Key Takeaways
1. A central bank backstop in the secondary market is the instrument that removes the bad equilibrium. Conditions that weaken it bring back the risk of repeated bond-market crises.
2. Legal constraints on the ECB are a live risk factor. Their strength depends on which economic model judges accept.
3. Central bank losses on sovereign bonds are a redistribution of interest income among member treasuries, not a fiscal cost in the ordinary sense.
4. Price stability, not equity, is the binding constraint on a central bank.
5. Moral hazard from a backstop is real and belongs to fiscal surveillance, not to the central bank.

# Chapter 12: On Solving Europe's Financial Issues to Promote Sustainable Growth

**Contributors**: Adrian Blundell-Wignall and Caroline Roulet (OECD). Part II. Written in early-to-mid 2014 (credit "still falling in early 2014"; AQR manual released March 2014).

## Core Idea
Europe's problem is a set of conflicting policy objectives inside a currency union hit by asymmetric real shocks. Adjustment is forced through labour markets, fiscal consolidation and bank deleveraging at once, with deflation risk. The authors select a policy mix: ECB action to cap sovereign yields and prevent deflation, balanced consolidation, a real clean-up and recapitalization of banks judged by a leverage ratio, separation of securities business from deposit banking, more ESM firepower, and in the long run full fiscal union. Without full banking and fiscal union, parts of southern Europe face depopulation pressure.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Real shocks vs nominal shocks**: fixed rates or a currency union are optimal when monetary shocks dominate and real shocks are symmetric. With real shocks, floating is superior. The euro removed nominal shocks inside the area, but real shocks intensified. Emerging market economies grew to about half of the world economy by end 2013. Northern Europe became vertically integrated into their demand for investment goods. Southern Europe competed with them in manufacturing at a cost disadvantage.

- **Two properties lost with a flexible exchange rate**: (1) absorption of real shocks through fast competitive adjustment; (2) no default on own-currency debt, since monetary financing is available in the limit. Consequences in the euro area: competitiveness adjusts through nominal wage cuts and unemployment, and deficit financing requires fiscal retrenchment to reassure lenders.

- **Bank-economy two-way loop**: low bank capital and legacy assets -> deleveraging -> falling credit -> weak economy -> more non-performing loans -> claims on bank capital.

- **Leverage ratio over Basel Tier 1**: judge capital as core Tier 1 over total IFRS assets. The risk-weighted ratio is "a low hurdle" that banks control through internal models. OECD research finds the leverage ratio highly correlated with distance-to-default, and the Basel core Tier 1 ratio not correlated with it.

- **Distance-to-default (DTD)**: compare the market value of a bank's assets (derived with the Black-Scholes formula) with the book value of its liabilities excluding equity. Express the gap in standard deviations from the default point. Above 3 standard deviations has historically been a sufficient cushion in a crisis.
  - Drivers found by the OECD: heavy derivatives use and wholesale funding lower the DTD. Liquid trading securities raise it.

- **Sovereign carry trade and the SME credit squeeze**. A bank maximizing return on equity prefers government bonds to SME loans even at equal yield, because: (1) the ECB has given an assurance on sovereigns; (2) the Basel risk weight is zero for government bonds and high for SME loans; (3) the SME book holds legacy loans to "zombie" companies that are politically hard to call; (4) governments demand credit while private demand is weak.

- **Internal and external balance diagram** (real exchange rate against domestic absorption, Figure 12.9). Before the crisis the north sat near internal balance with large surpluses and the periphery was uncompetitive with deficits. Consolidation, bank stress and wage cuts push the south into the "more competitive, lower absorption" quadrant with soaring unemployment, heading towards unsustainable surpluses as absorption collapses. For a 2% area average, northern inflation must run above 2% while the south is below.

- **Debt dynamics** (note 16, recovered from PDF p. 262): `change in d_t = -pb_t + [(i_t - g_t) / (1 + g_t)] x d_(t-1)`, where d is public debt/GDP, pb the primary balance/GDP, i the effective interest rate, g nominal growth.
  - Illustration in the text: debt of 120% of GDP, borrowing at 5%, no growth, requires a primary surplus of 6% of GDP just to stabilize debt.

- **Bank separation**. The EU proposal following the Liikanen Report: ban proprietary trading in credit institutions; move market making and underwriting (derivatives and securitization in particular) into a separately capitalized and funded trading entity in which the deposit bank holds no voting shares. Scope thresholds as printed: EUR 30 bn, and trading assets of EUR 70 bn where these are 10% or more of total assets. The authors want separation once a bank's derivatives assets (IFRS basis) exceed 10% of its balance sheet, and a leverage ratio of at least 5% on unweighted assets.

- **ESM leverage through a public-private vehicle**: the ESM capitalizes a special purpose vehicle under the European Investment Bank. The vehicle buys warrants in poorly capitalized banks after the AQR with public and private money. After restructuring the warrants come into the money, "as in the USA", and the taxpayer bill could be zero.

## Key Concepts
- **GSIFI**: global systemically important financial institution.
- **Capital markets banking**: prime broking, market making, underwriting, origination and proprietary trading, all exposed to mark-to-market swings and margin calls.
- **AQR**: the ECB's Asset Quality Review of 2014, feeding a stress test on an AQR-adjusted CET1 ratio.
- **Legacy assets**: impaired assets still on bank balance sheets.
- **Forbearance**: non-recognition of losses, which made banks look safer in 2008 than they proved in 2012.
- **Bail-in**: converting unsecured bondholders into equity holders, in order of seniority, at a single point of entry before failure.
- **TBTF**: too big to fail, the source of cross-subsidy to high-risk activities.
- **Bad deleveraging**: simultaneous deleveraging by banks, firms, households and governments.
- **NOHC**: non-operating holding company, the OECD's ring-fencing structure.

## Mental Models
- "Europe is overbanked, particularly in the south." Bank lending falls partly because sovereign bonds are the rational asset under the rules.
- When governments must save more, another sector must save less. Corporate debt is very high in Ireland, Portugal, Spain, France and Italy, and households are highly levered in Ireland, Spain, Portugal and the UK, so no sector can offset.
- Leaving the euro "permits countries to convert credit risk into inflation risk". Note 14 adds that a bond can cheapen through a higher yield in the single currency or through a currency that falls to where it can be expected to rise again.
- Cross-border exposure maps contagion. Foreign bank exposure to Greek, Portuguese and Irish sovereigns is small. Exposure to Italy, France and Spain is large, which is why the ECB had to cap sovereign rates.
- Historical analogue: Britain and Ireland shared sterling from 1820 to 1920 without a transfer mechanism. The British population doubled while Ireland's fell by 40%.

## Anti-patterns
- **Stress tests without balance-sheet transparency**: the first two European tests "were not credible".
- **Relying on risk-weighted capital ratios**: regulatory arbitrage.
- **Watered-down separation**: French and German laws keep market making and underwriting inside the deposit bank. The US Volcker Rule does the same and cannot define proprietary trading cleanly.
- **Zero risk weights on sovereigns**: they crowd out SME lending and tie banks to their sovereign.
- **Expecting a currency union to produce convergence**: "Currency unions do not promote economic convergence, even if the single monetary policy is accompanied by fiscal prudence."

## Worked Example
Distance-to-default comparison for banks with over USD 50 bn of assets (Figure 12.5, from the prose):
1. Compute each bank's DTD for 2008 (worst crisis year), 2012 and 2013 H2.
2. US banks: all above the 3-standard-deviation line by 2013. Cause given: bad loans were dealt with and capital injected.
3. European banks: about half at or above the line in 2013, improving in the right order. About 20 large banks are still below.
4. In nine cases the market's perception in 2008 was better than the perception in 2013. The authors attribute this to forbearance and lack of transparency, and call it "the heart of the reason why European banks are not supporting the recovery".
5. Policy inference: use the AQR to clean balance sheets, then set capital plans on a leverage ratio.

## Key Takeaways
1. Measure bank strength by unweighted leverage and distance-to-default, not risk-weighted ratios.
2. Compare the bank loan rate with the government bond yield. While sovereign yields are close to or above loan rates, expect credit to SMEs to keep falling.
3. Apply the debt identity with nominal growth. Low inflation makes stabilization arithmetic much harder.
4. Bank and sovereign CDS move together because banks hold home sovereigns. A cap on sovereign yields is also bank support.
5. In a union, 2% average inflation requires above-2% inflation in the surplus countries.
6. Youth unemployment and emigration of the highly qualified are the early signs of the depopulation scenario.

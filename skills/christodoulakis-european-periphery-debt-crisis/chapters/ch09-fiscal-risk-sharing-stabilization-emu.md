# Chapter 9: Fiscal Risk Sharing and Stabilization in the EMU

**Contributors**: Kerstin Bernoth (Hertie School, DIW Berlin) and Philipp Engler (Freie Universitat Berlin). Part II. Data to 2013 Q2, downloaded January 2014.

## Core Idea
A monetary union removes national monetary and exchange rate policy, so asymmetric shocks hit harder and business cycles drift apart. A rule-based cyclical transfer system between member states can partly replace the lost stabilizers. The authors present it as a medium- to long-term stability device, "not an instrument to solve the current crisis". The price is some fiscal sovereignty, and the trade-off between transfer size and sovereignty is left to political debate.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Four-scenario comparison** (from the two-country DSGE model of Engler and Voigts 2013; small domestic country, large foreign country, moderate trade, temporary negative domestic demand shock):

  | Scenario | Regime | Domestic outcome | Foreign outcome | Cycles |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | Own monetary policy, flexible exchange rate | Rate cut and depreciation dampen the downturn | Appreciation cuts net exports, slight recession | Highly synchronized |
  | 2 | Monetary union, no transfers | Smaller rate cut, no depreciation, deeper downturn | No export loss, slightly lower rates, output rises | More volatile, asynchronous |
  | 3 | Union with deeper real integration (higher import share) | Shock spread over domestic and foreign goods | Shares the shock | More similar, but a long-term goal |
  | 4 | Union with compensatory payments | Transfer lifts demand, decline alleviated "considerably" | Stimulus curtailed by paying the transfer | Lower volatility, convergence |

- **Why cross-country transfers beat national fiscal stabilization** (Engler and Voigts 2013):
  1. Ricardian equivalence dampens a national tax or spending stimulus, because households expect future taxes. It does not apply to a transfer from abroad.
  2. A national deficit can raise the sovereign risk premium, which depresses the economy. A transfer adds no national debt.
  - Most relevant when national fiscal policy is constrained by high debt and high risk premia.

- **Design requirements for a cyclical transfer system**:
  1. Payments are quick and on time. Delays can destabilize.
  2. Rule-governed, automatic, transparent.
  3. Targeted at cyclical fluctuations, so each member is donor and recipient over a cycle.
  4. Accompanied by strong fiscal rules and a credible no-bailout clause.
  5. Participation conditional, for example on structural reforms, a constitutional debt brake or SGP adherence.

- **Balanced-each-period vs fund that may run deficits**:
  - A fund that can run surpluses and deficits stabilizes every member equally per capita, but needs a larger supranational budget.
  - A fund balanced each period gives larger countries smaller per-capita transfers. The authors treat this as an advantage, since small countries are more volatile and large stable countries face less risk of imported volatility. They study only this version as politically realistic.

- **Three implementation options (all balanced each period)**:
  1. Cyclical contributions, acyclical receipts: members pay in cyclically sensitive revenue such as VAT. The fund redistributes in proportion to per-capita potential output.
  2. Acyclical contributions, cyclical receipts: fixed per-capita contribution. Receipts are proportional to the deviation of the national per-capita output gap from the euro-area average. Below average means net recipient.
  3. European unemployment insurance alongside national systems: a fixed payment per worker into a European fund, redistributed according to the deviation of cyclical unemployment from the EMU average. Households, not governments, receive the money.

- **Measuring the trigger**: output gaps and NAIRU-based unemployment gaps are inaccurate and revised. The authors suggest short-term unemployment (under one year) as the proxy for cyclical unemployment, because it is available fast and leaves less room for political discretion. Use cyclical, not total, unemployment so transfers do not flow permanently one way.

## Key Concepts
- **Asymmetric shock**: a shock hitting one member and not the others.
- **Compensatory payment / transfer**: used interchangeably (note 1).
- **Cyclical transfer mechanism**: insurance against asymmetric cyclical income fluctuations, not income equalization.
- **Ricardian equivalence**: households offset debt-financed stimulus by saving for future taxes. Note 17 adds that empirically only part of the private sector behaves this way.
- **Output gap**: percent deviation of actual from potential GDP.
- **NAIRU**: non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment.
- **German bloc / French bloc**: the two groups of output-gap co-movement found in the data.
- **I.i.d. shocks assumption**: country shocks are independent, identically distributed, with zero mean, so payments balance over time (note 7).

## Mental Models
- A single interest rate is set for the area average. It is too tight for the member in a slump and too loose for the rest, so the union itself increases divergence.
- Transfers are zero when cycles are perfectly correlated and grow as correlation falls. Correlation is the sizing variable.
- Small members gain most, for two reasons: specialized production makes sector shocks macro shocks, and the ECB's reaction gives them little weight.
- A transfer system aligns the amplitude and frequency of national cycles. It does not dampen the aggregate euro-area cycle. Union-wide counter-cyclical policy is still needed.

## Anti-patterns
- **Confusing cyclical insurance with income equalization**: permanent donors and recipients destroy reform incentives. The existing EU budget (about 1% of GDP, around 35% of it in structural funds) does the latter.
- **Triggers that need negotiation**: revised output gaps invite delay and abuse.
- **Assuming everyone gains**: a stable country can see its volatility rise (note 15: when cycles are synchronized but differ in amplitude, the calmer economy's boom gets larger).
- **Using transfers as a substitute for sound policy**: national governments may reduce their own stabilization effort (Persson and Tabellini 1996; Migue 1993).

## Worked Example
Who would have paid and received (sect. 2.5), using output gaps against the euro-area weighted average:
1. Data: Commission annual potential output (autumn 2013), interpolated to quarterly, and real quarterly GDP.
2. Blocs: Netherlands, Austria and Finland correlate with Germany at 0.78 to 0.91. Italy and Spain correlate with France at 0.97 and 0.95.
3. Germany and France correlate only modestly (0.57 in the text, 0.58 in the table). On four occasions one country's boom coincided with the other's recession.
4. Early 1999 to early 2008: Germany would have been a net receiver and France a net contributor.
5. Since early 2010: the German bloc would have been a net contributor. France would have received roughly zero, because its cycle matched the average.
6. Volatility against size: leaving out Malta and Cyprus, smaller EMU countries have higher output-gap volatility (in line with Alouini and Hubert 2012).
7. Conclusion: both groups could have gained in output-gap stabilization since the euro began.

## Key Takeaways
1. Measure pairwise output-gap correlation with the core to size a member's exposure to a one-size monetary policy.
2. Expect two blocs in the euro area, not a core and a scattered periphery: a German bloc and a French bloc that includes Italy and Spain.
3. A cross-border transfer is more powerful than the same national stimulus when households are Ricardian or the sovereign pays a risk premium.
4. A balanced-each-period scheme tilts stabilization towards small members.
5. Short-term unemployment is the practical trigger variable.
6. Transfers need fiscal rules and a credible no-bailout clause beside them.

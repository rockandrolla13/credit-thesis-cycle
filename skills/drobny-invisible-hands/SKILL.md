## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. The author's thesis: three macro principles for real money (Ch 1, Conclusion)
1. Replace return targets with risk-adjusted return targets.
2. Look forward, not backward. Consider the macro environment first.
3. Rethink liquidity. "Time horizons are much shorter than generally believed."
Four flaws of the Endowment Model: equity-heavy diversification is not diversification. Valuation matters. Fund commitments are leverage. Horizons are short when cash is owed each year. Losses are non-linear: there is a "kink" after which they force the institution to change behaviour.

### 2. Risk as an input (the Pensioner, Ch 14)
- A 60-40 portfolio is about 90-10 in risk terms. Equal risk in stocks and bonds is about 20-80 in dollars.
- Build the Maximum Sharpe Ratio portfolio by equal-weighting risk across many premia. Then lever it to the risk level you accept.
- Four types of leverage. Good: to hedge liabilities, and to lever low-volatility assets for diversification. Bad: to lever risky positions, and hidden off-balance-sheet leverage such as private equity.
- "Leverage is not a true measure of risk." A ban on accounting leverage drives risk into economic leverage.
- Most "alpha" before 2008 was equity premium, illiquidity premium, short volatility, or credit. All four are correlated.
- 2008 showed non-constant volatility in liquid markets, not exceptional fat tails.

### 3. Illiquidity (Ch 3, Ch 5, Ch 12, Ch 14)
- "Illiquidity is essentially a short put option on opportunity cost" (Pensioner). It earns steadily and gives it back when volatility spikes.
- It is a negative externality inside a fund. One desk earns the premium and the whole plan pays. Set a central liquidity limit.
- Cash has option value: "Holding cash when markets are cheap is expensive, and holding cash when markets are expensive is cheap" (Leitner).
- "You cannot hedge illiquidity in any way other than staying liquid" (A. Drobny). "You can never hedge a liquidity crisis" (Predator).
- Hard-to-access assets are cheap only until money piles in (Philosopher's "beta-plus", Predator on Harvard).

### 4. Price, not risk, sets expected return (the House, Ch 4)
"The expected return is a function of the risk premium of the asset at a given price." He saw the credit bubble "just from looking at the price of credit products": compare the spread with cycle-average default cost and with what risk-free liquidity provision pays. Weak borrowers and easy terms were "just backup signs".

### 5. Cheap insurance before the event (Ch 3, Ch 4, Ch 13)
- Leitner, summer 2007: investment grade index spread at 35 bp, risk about 10 bp, reached almost 300 bp.
- The House, 2005 to 2007: protection on index tranches (losses between 6% and 9%), payout up to 500 times. Entered two years early. Exited early for counterparty risk.
- Plasticine, 2006 to 2007: 1% of assets a year in index puts.
- The Philosopher dissents: "I buy options when they are cheap, not for insurance."

### 6. Hypothesis testing in place of stops (the Philosopher, Ch 5)
A market is "not a truth mechanism". Trade the gap between priced probabilities and the range of reasonable opinions. Decide in advance what would falsify the trade, size from that, and exit when it happens, by price or by fundamentals. Pain-threshold stops give "the illusion of discipline". Three return streams: beta, beta-plus, alpha.

### 7. Catastrophe-first sizing (the Professor, Ch 7. Leitner, Ch 3. the Predator, Ch 12)
- Titanic funnel: fix the fund's loss in a cataclysm (20%), run each trade through disaster scenarios, size back from that.
- Deconstruct each trade to its core bet and its hidden short put.
- Zero test: "value every asset in your fund at an absurdly low price and make sure that you can still survive."
- Leitner: no more than 10% in a month or 20% in a year, then go to cash.

### 8. Hard-coded limits (Ch 10, Ch 11)
- Commodity Hedger: 2% stop per trade, delever a theme at -5%, delever the portfolio at -10%, zero-cost risk collars on every full position.
- Equity Trader: 7% stop per portfolio manager, then the book is shut. Trade the P&L when fundamentals stop working.

### 9. Crowding and actors (Ch 1, Ch 4, Ch 11, Ch 12)
- "Success brings emulation, emulation brings leverage, and leverage eventually brings disaster" (Predator).
- Know who else holds the trade and when each type lets go. "If you are the buyer of last resort, then there is no liquidity" (Predator).
- Crowded breaks are "sharper, faster and bigger than expected" (Equity Trader).
- Bucket by risk factor, not by investment type (Equity Trader).

### 10. Gross exposure and correlation (Ch 5, Ch 9, Ch 13)
- Halving gross is not a risk cut if volatility quadrupled (Philosopher).
- When correlations rise, cut gross. Adding hedges raises gross and basis risk (Commodity Investor).
- If both legs of a hedge make money, it is one position (Plasticine).
- "If in doubt, buy a third or sell a third" (Plasticine).

### 11. Macro reasoning rules (Ch 2, Ch 7, Ch 13)
- Private debt was the problem, not public debt. Watch its direction, not only its level (A. Drobny).
- A bond collapse needs rising nominal yields with flat or lower real yields, which means inflation (A. Drobny).
- Investment booms: judge by the growth bought per unit of investment. The Professor cites the claim that sustained investment above 50% of GDP has always ended in a bust.
- Creditor nations that block currency appreciation push liquidity into asset prices: US 1920s, Japan 1980s, China 2000s (Plasticine).
- Sources of alpha: noneconomic actors, market change, structural shifts, policy error (Professor).

### 12. Commodities (Ch 8, Ch 9, Ch 10)
Supply trends and demand swings. Put inventory in a demand context and look for stocks-to-use thresholds. Trade the back of the curve. Passive front-month indices are poor inflation hedges in contango.

## Use for Credit and Macro Questions

- **Credit spreads**: useful, as practitioner rules and one dated case. A price test for a credit bubble (House, Ch 4), tight spreads read as cheap equity puts (Leitner, Ch 3), credit as "a slice of the equity risk premium" (Pensioner, Ch 14), and the sequence of buying credit before equity in late 2008 (Predator, Ch 12). No spread model and no data series.
- **Debt-financed investment booms**: partial. The investment-to-GDP rule and the marginal-return test (Professor, Ch 7), the copying cycle from success to leverage to disaster (Predator, Ch 12), the creditor-surplus pattern and China's 2009 capacity build (Plasticine, Ch 13), and the private-debt stock-versus-flow rule (A. Drobny, Ch 2). All are opinions with few numbers.
- **Inflation and Fed regimes**: dated views only. The bond-crisis test (Ch 2), inflation by way of deflation (Ch 13), the "paradox of perfection" (Ch 4), and the Philosopher's reading of the Fed, ECB and Bank of England in 2008 (Ch 5). The managers split on inflation versus deflation in 2009. Outcomes are not in the book.
- **Issuer screening**: nothing systematic. Only tells: a levered firm raising leverage and buying back stock into stress (Predator on Lehman, Ch 12), and reading positioning from reaction to news (Equity Trader, Ch 11).
- **Positioning and liquidity risk**: the strongest use. Crowding, funding, counterparty and exit rules appear in almost every chapter.

## Method or Case

The book is interviews, so most chapters mix a person's standing process with an account of past trades. The split below is by what dominates.

- **METHOD**: ch01 (the author's framing and three principles), ch14 (the Pensioner's portfolio construction method, plus the Conclusion).
- **CASE**: ch02 (A. Drobny on the crash and policy response), ch06 (the Bond Trader on 1987, 1994, 1998, 2008), ch12 (the Predator's 2007 to 2009 sequence).
- **MIXED, method sections usable on their own**: ch03 (Leitner), ch04 (the House), ch05 (the Philosopher), ch07 (the Professor), ch08 (Commodity Trader), ch09 (Commodity Investor), ch10 (Commodity Hedger), ch11 (Equity Trader), ch13 (Plasticine Macro Trader). Each chapter file opens with a "Type" line and separates process from trades.
- Every "Views stated in 2009" section and every worked trade is CASE. Treat them as dated testimony. Do not load them into fresh reasoning as rules.
- Selection warning: Drobny chose managers who made money or held capital in 2008. This is a sample of survivors.

# Chapter 7: The Professor (anonymous, "real macro" manager, ex bank prop trader)

Type: MIXED. The Titanic funnel, the screening process and the investment-boom rule are METHOD. The 1997, 1998, 1999 and 2008 trades are CASE.

## Core Idea
Start from an economic prior, find where market pricing departs from it, and deconstruct each trade down to its core bet. Then size it by how much the whole fund loses if the world ends, not by a trade-level stop.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Titanic scenario and the Titanic funnel**:
  1. Set a hard cash number the fund may lose in a cataclysm. Tie it to the return promised to investors. His current limit: maximum loss 20 percent in a "once-in-a-lifetime" event. He would not risk that for a 5 percent expected return.
  2. Run every trade through several negative scenarios: a repeat of the Asian crisis, the 1994 bond sell-off, the dot-com bust, and from now on 2008. Add hypothetical ones.
  3. A trade whose scenario loss is ten times the fund limit does not enter, however good it looks.
  4. A trade with good expected return that *makes* money in the bad scenarios enters and is sized up for its non-correlation.
  5. If a wanted position is implicitly selling insurance, add "some uncorrelated, antirisk trade" or drop it.
  - Purpose: "generate option-like payoff profiles in the portfolio" without buying options. He hardly uses options now.
- **Two things that matter to the organization**: (1) research and expected return per trade. (2) How the fund performs in Armageddon.
- **Trade deconstruction**: "After making all the necessary adjustments, what are you actually betting on?" Even after stripping extraneous risks, a risky asset may be "selling some kind of put on an unfavorable state of the world".
- **Sources of alpha** (his "overarching thesis"): three things create recurring good risk-versus-reward trades:
  1. *Noneconomic actors.* Governments with generational horizons, corporate hedgers who care about the quarterly report, reserve managers. They "dwarf the leveraged footprint of hedge funds".
  2. *Market change.*
  3. *Structural shifts.*
  - Add *policy error*: volatility in times of flux raises the chance of one, "one of the greatest sources of alpha for macro managers".
- **Basic Principles**: his list of macroeconomic priors and known patterns. Examples he names:
  - behaviour and persistence of inflation in convergence economies
  - the trend for exchange rates in small open economies
  - the link between P/E multiples and exchange rate valuation in economies with large trade surpluses
  - the central bank credibility premium after an inflationary bust
  - "Financial markets invariably lag structural developments in the real economy."
  - Tools turn the principles into fair value estimates. A large gap to forward market pricing flags something to research.
- **Three cautions on models**: (1) models are unstable, so treat them as tracking devices and use the model that fits the paradigm. (2) The sample period matters because of institutional and policy shifts. (3) Forecast combinations can beat one "best" model.
- **Research order of priority**: own research. Then researchers and specialists. Then other portfolio managers. Then the public sector (central banks, finance ministries). Then locals. He ignores sell-side research.
- **Attractiveness and shrinkage**: attractiveness is his fair value estimate minus market pricing. He adjusts the raw expected return for the uncertainty of the trade type (equity, rates, directional currency differ). He shrinks expected returns "towards zero", a Bayesian approach, then feeds the result to the funnel. He also adjusts for transaction cost and volatility.
- **The investment-boom rule**:
  - Cited statistic (he attributes it to Austrian-school sceptics): "every country in history with sustained investment-to-GDP ratios of more than 50 percent has eventually blown up", meaning a big investment bust.
  - His test: the *marginal return of investment in terms of growth*.
    - Very high marginal return: get involved through equities.
    - Very low marginal return with near-euphoric sentiment: still get involved, but through the currency.
  - Currencies of investment-boom countries "typically do well, during both boom and bust". Foreign direct investment supports them in the boom. A deflationary bust gives supportive real rates, more so if the currency started undervalued.
  - India against China: India invests about 25 to just under 30 percent of GDP, half China's ratio, for growth about 1 percentage point lower. He reads that as a high return on investment.
- **The nominal anchor**: his ideal macro trade has one. The 1998 ECU basket had it in rates and in currencies. The 2008 opportunities had none, because spreads reflected real doubt about bank solvency.
- **Crowding read two ways**: a crowded, unanchored trade held for macro reasons is probably near fair value, so expected return is low. A trade crowded by people looking "through non-macro goggles" can be far from fair value, and is a candidate to fade.

## Key Concepts
- **Real macro**: structural, country-specific bets on policy and fundamentals that take time. Minimum horizon about three months. A trade should start to perform within 12 months.
- **Vintage macro**: trade-by-trade stop losses anchored on a year-to-date stop, with discretionary sizing and timing. He keeps the scaling-in and drops the stop fixation.
- **LIBOR-OIS spread**: LIBOR minus the overnight indexed swap rate, a measure of bank funding stress.
- **Budgetization of monetary policy**: his phrase for quantitative easing.
- **IMF Article VIII, HDI, Corruption Perceptions Index**: his country screens for long-run real asset exposure.

## Mental Models
- "Roast the current generation or the future generation?" Policy chose to hold nominal asset values and protect the current one. The cost is high debt and low potential growth.
- After an asset bubble "the labor market flips from a lagging indicator to a leading indicator".
- A liquidity-driven rally with "no second-order multiplier into the real economy" is not durable in his view.
- Know who is on the other side. In the ECU trade "you knew the loser": forced deleveraging after LTCM and Russia.
- Use anecdotes only where the sample can be representative. Iceland has about 300,000 people, so 100 anecdotes say something. In China they are "absolutely useless".
- Travel to sharpen a forecast formed in advance, not to find inside information.
- Imagine the impossible on purpose. His example is aliens landing in Geneva.

## Anti-patterns
- **Tight stops on volatile assets**: buying a stock with 30 percent annual volatility, risking 1 percent to make 10 percent in a week "is madness". It is almost certain to be stopped out. Overusing stops leaves "pennies on the table".
- **Going straight to target risk from historical volatility and correlation**, with no regard for entry and timing. Such funds "come close to failing once every decade or so".
- **Sizing a peg trade as low volatility**: his 1997 renminbi forward position.
- **Hedging with an instrument that has a credit element**: his LIBOR futures in 2008.
- **Selling the tail without a hedge**: those who know and still do it are "gaming an annual compensation system or worse, they are fooling themselves".
- **Story-fitting**: instinctive trading "that fits a story around what is really momentum or chart-based technical trading".

## Worked Examples
- **Renminbi forwards, 1997 (his first setback)**: he faded 1 to 2 percent of annualized risk premium on the thesis that China would hold its peg. When the Thai baht devalued, the forward points jumped on devaluation talk. The thesis was sound. The size, set for a low-volatility trade, made it "very painful".
- **ECU basket, fourth quarter 1998 ("the best trade in the history of macro")**: under three months before the euro launch the basket blew out, offering 3 to 4 percent (he also says 3 to 5 percent) in weeks with no capacity limit. The small risk that monetary union would fail could be hedged almost for free.
- **Turkish T-bills, 1999**: on-the-ground research showed the conditions for an IMF standby program would be met easily. Local political and corporate consensus was "orthogonal" to London and New York. One-year yields were above 100 percent. The domestic T-bill market was about $40 billion, so size was possible. After the IMF package yields fell to 30 percent at one point. Deconstructed, the trade held rate risk, currency risk and default risk. Hedging the currency removes much of the yield.
- **2008**: "given our exposures, our organization should have failed, but we were still up small." Risky trades fell more than they should have from illiquidity. The LIBOR futures hedge failed when the LIBOR-OIS basis blew out. Long rates in peripheral economies with steep curves saved the year. Lesson: next time use exchange-traded futures on central bank rates. Add scenarios where nothing blows up but there is no liquidity to exit. "There was no stop in 2008 as markets gapped."

## Views stated in 2009 (dated)
- Medium-term theme: "very high nominal growth and very low real growth", with wider dispersion between regions. Bearish, with the bearishness "concentrated in G3". Favourable for select emerging markets.
- He agrees with the consensus that policy makers in the most damaged countries "are effectively targeting 4 to 8 percent inflation". Whether they can reach it is open.
- Central banks may move from inflation targeting to targeting inflation and credit growth. He argues inflation targeting worked and also led to 2008, because belief in permanent price stability invited leverage.
- Bubble candidates: emerging markets later, commodities and alternative energy around peak oil and "the end of fiat money".
- The dollar keeps reserve status partly through power.
- China: he leans to "maybe this time it is different, whilst being mindful of the downsides".
- Lottery winner portfolio: 70 percent global inflation-linked bonds unhedged (Norway, Sweden, Euroland, US, UK). 15 percent BRIC inflation-linked bonds. 15 percent BRIC equities.
- CalPERS at 8 percent real: "challenging but achievable" through unhedged emerging and frontier equity. He admits this raises the odds of underfunding.

## Key Takeaways
1. Fix the catastrophe loss in cash first. Size every trade back from it.
2. Name the core bet and the hidden put in every position.
3. Keep an explicit prior and look for forward pricing that departs from it.
4. In an investment boom, compare the growth each unit of investment buys, and choose equity or currency on that basis.
5. Prefer trades with a nominal anchor and a known forced seller.
6. Test hedges for basis risk in a banking crisis.

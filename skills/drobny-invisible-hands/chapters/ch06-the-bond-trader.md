# Chapter 6: The Bond Trader (anonymous, fixed income specialist, no losing year in over two decades)

Type: CASE. A short, guarded interview about how he traded 1987, 1994, 1998 and 2008. He refuses fixed rules, so this chapter is thin on method. His stock answer is "It depends."

## Core Idea
There is no formula. Find the next driver of the economic cycle, compare your view with what is priced, stay in simple liquid instruments, and cut gross exposure when the financial system itself is in doubt.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three reasons investors lost in 2008** (his list):
  1. Their structure or style did not let them act quickly enough.
  2. A persistent "buy the dips" mentality.
  3. Illiquid positions that were expensive to exit.
  - Funds without these three problems started 2009 with "a clean slate" and fewer competitors.
- **Driver of the cycle**: "I stay ahead of the market simply by correctly interpreting the next driver of the economic cycle." Form a view of what causes the current situation and what will change it. Focus on those drivers "and disregard the rest".
  - Leading indicators rotate. "You cannot use the same economic data as your leading indicator, year in and year out."
  - Government action makes this harder now, because policy has a larger effect on the path.
- **Priced versus expected**: focus "on what is already priced into the market" and weigh it against your own view.
- **Liquidity measured as exit cost**: "how much we think it will cost to get out during a certain period of time". It changes while you are getting out.
- **Liquidity matching**: "The liquidity of our portfolio matches the liquidity we offer our investors." Work out the unencumbered cash needed under worst-case scenarios.
- **Sizing**: look at the instrument's daily volume and volatility, estimate how far the position can move in a day or a week, and decide how much to lose on the bet. Check the fit with the other trades in the book.
- **Crowding read from the tape**: you cannot know positions for sure. Price action and liquidity levels show "something brewing". Other bank desks being told to cut is also information.

## Key Concepts
- **Gross derivative book**: the notional book of derivatives with bank counterparties. He cut it for counterparty risk, separately from his government bond leverage.
- **LIBOR basis and ABX**: the instruments he saw "getting out of control" in 2007.
- **Gates and side pockets**: limits on redemption imposed by funds in late 2008. He says their use "is not right". "Changing liquidity is not an excuse for putting up a gate."
- **Reaction function**: central bankers are very smart, but "their reaction functions are different than ours".
- **Unencumbered cash**: cash not pledged as collateral. He lists it with collateral, counterparties and credit as business risks a fund must manage.

## Mental Models
- A view shift is the most interesting signal from traders, academics and researchers. Ask why they changed.
- A crowded core position "might worry me, but it might also be confirmation". It depends on the structure and on why everyone holds it.
- Lower liquidity and higher volatility "roughly cancel each other out". After 2008 he makes the same return with a fraction of the position size.
- Big long-run questions (reserve status, China, oil) are interesting but he would not buy on them.
- The market's own time horizon changes. Before Lehman it looked far ahead. Afterwards it "does not care about something six months from now". Decide whether you want to trade against that.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reaching for illiquidity to smooth returns**: wide bid-offer spreads or few transactions make the profit and loss look less volatile.
- **Judging a manager by track record alone.** If volatility is "too low", ask what instruments produce it.
- **Blind copying of a hot trader's positions.** Know the trader's history and weaknesses first.
- **Funds knocked out by business issues, not market issues**: "There is no excuse for that."
- **Putting all your own money in the fund**: it "could change your behavior for the worse".
- **Lack of risk control**: "the most common reason for failure" among traders he hired.

## Worked Example: deleveraging through 2008
1. *2007.* He sees LIBOR basis blowing out and ABX and other credit spreads widening sharply. He had already doubted the large gross derivative book for about a year.
2. *Decision.* Cut leverage drastically.
3. *March 2008.* Bear Stearns is sold. The unwinding of positions creates high trading volume. He uses that liquidity to exit derivatives faster than planned, "at minimal costs". Half is done in the first few weeks after Bear.
4. *Rest of 2008.* The remainder is done over nine months to limit bid-offer cost. It "still cost a lot of money".
5. *Government bond leverage.* Cut slightly after Bear, further in the weeks after Lehman, and again for the last quarter. His reason: if things worsened, "even cash markets could, in theory, suffer mark-to-market problems".
6. *End state.* Low gross leverage. Remaining positions mainly in liquid G3 bonds.
7. *Junior traders.* He closed down all traders hired after 2003 within a week of Lehman.
8. *His caveat.* Cutting size is not always right. In another environment the right step may be adding hedges.

## Other episodes he reports
| Year | What he did | Lesson he states |
|---|---|---|
| 1987 | Not long bonds before the crash. Got long just after | Bid-offer spreads on bonds blew out. He found 1987 more useful than 1998 for 2008 |
| 1994 | The speed of the early sell-off told him it could get much worse. He flipped long to short | "Market prices can go to silly levels" |
| Early summer 1998 | Unwound most positions and cut those that relied on liquidity or were common to big players, before LTCM | Deal sizes and liquidity offered were extremely high against prior years. Also: crises can normalize quickly |
| 1999 to 2000 | Flat in 1999. Made all of 2000's profit in December | His only flat year |
| Summer 2008 | Lost money when the ECB and Bank of England voiced inflation fears as the economy rolled over | His worst recent trade |

## Views stated in 2009 (dated)
- Inflation or deflation: nobody knows. He does not expect inflation and hopes policy makers can create it, because "deflation is a very bad outcome for a world awash with debt".
- One 10-year holding: cash, probably euros, for capital preservation. To maximize wealth he calls cash "a very bad bet".
- A European Union break-up would "extremely surprise" him. The second ten years will be harder than the first.
- Markets are neither zero-sum nor efficient.

## Key Takeaways
1. Cut gross exposure into liquidity, over time, before you are forced.
2. Separate counterparty risk on derivatives from mark-to-market risk on cash bonds.
3. Match the fund's assets to the liquidity terms it sold.
4. Change your leading indicators as the cycle changes.
5. Treat business risk (cash, collateral, counterparties) as seriously as market risk.

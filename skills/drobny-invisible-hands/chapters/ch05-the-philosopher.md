# Chapter 5: The Philosopher (anonymous, multibillion dollar macro fund, ex money markets trader)

Type: MIXED. The hypothesis-testing process is METHOD, and it is the most transferable method in the book. His 1994, 2006, 2007 and 2008 trades and his reading of Lehman are CASE.

## Core Idea
A market is "not a truth mechanism". Prices reflect the probability people attach to outcomes at that moment. Do not ask which outcome is true. Ask where the market misprices the skew of probabilities, state your trade as a hypothesis, and cut it when the hypothesis is falsified.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Hypothesis testing, not "vision macro"** (he credits Popper, and Soros's *Alchemy of Finance*):
  1. Form a probabilistic set of hypotheses about how the world could look.
  2. Work out the market impact in every scenario.
  3. Compare with market prices and look for differences.
  4. Ask what could drive the difference to widen or converge.
  5. Take good risk-versus-reward trades around the hypotheses.
- **His rate-trade template**: (a) economic drivers set the frame: growth, inflation, employment. (b) Model the outcomes and weight them by probability. (c) Work out the central bank's reaction in each. (d) Compare with market pricing.
- **Range of reasonable opinions**: if two schools of thought both exist and both are reasonable, sentiment can move from one to the other and price will follow. He needs the believers to exist, not to be right.
  - If the market prices inflation "to the moon", he argues unemployment and falling wages. If it prices "deflation forever", he argues the quantity theory.
- **The 60/40 poll effect**: if outcome A has a 60 percent probability, everyone polled says A. Move it to 40 percent and everyone says B. A 20-point change in probability flips the reported consensus from 100 percent A to 100 percent B. He looks for "cases where a small change in fundamentals could cause a large change in how people perceive the fundamentals".
  - Humans treat low-probability events as "either inevitable or impossible".
- **"Hypothesis falsified", not "stop loss"**:
  - Decide in advance what it would take to believe you are wrong. Work backwards to conviction, to the loss you will accept, and so to position size.
  - Evidence can be price (the market crosses a level it should not) or fundamentals (the reasons for the trade no longer hold).
  - "You do not have to wait until a trade has lost money before cutting it."
  - Exit trades that make money "for all the wrong reasons".
- **Portfolio drawdown with no falsified theme**: obey preset drawdown triggers and cut risk overall. Two likely errors: (1) sizing, meaning the volatility input was wrong. (2) Correlation between themes was higher than assumed.
- **Two sources of trade ideas**:
  - *Fundamentals to market*: bottom-up economics compared with what is priced.
  - *Markets to fundamentals*: start from a market move or someone else's idea. First ask what follows *if* the hypothesis is true. Only then ask in what circumstances it could be true. "By the time something is clearly true, it is already largely priced in."
  - Also invert bad ideas. A pitch to buy credit for a ridiculous reason can signal the opposite trade.
- **Three return streams**:
  1. *Beta*: the S&P 500 or equivalent.
  2. *Beta-plus* ("hard-to-access beta"): private equity, distressed real estate, other illiquids. Cheap only while hard to access. Excess return disappears once money piles in.
  3. *Alpha*, which includes macro. Not tactical beta trading. Example: which country hikes first.
  - "Deciding when to be involved in the beta-plus assets is a macro call."
- **Playing a bubble**: do not fade it, because it can last longer than you think. Do not buy assets that are already too expensive and could become illiquid. Use the most liquid products that correlate with the bubble.

## Key Concepts
- **Liquidity as a common risk factor**: "a perpetual common risk factor across a broad variety of markets". Invisible in normal times, so models assume it away.
- **Negative skew risk**: trades that can turn illiquid quickly. He refuses them.
- **Greater fool theory**: if you will not hold a credit product to maturity, you rely on selling it to someone else. Collectively that is "logically impossible".
- **Keynes's beauty contest**: predicting what average opinion expects average opinion to be. His extension: animal spirits are not irrational, so they can be anticipated.
- **True believer**: someone for whom "price loses its importance". "None of the survivors become true believers."
- **Black swan (Popper's sense)**: an observation that falsifies a hypothesis. Not an unforeseeable event.
- **Rehypothecation**: a broker pledging customers' collateral for its own borrowing. He says this, not CDS counterparties, was the real Lehman problem.
- **Illiquid versus insolvent**: a lender of last resort can fix the first. The second needs the taxpayer.

## Mental Models
- "The biggest macro question is always: 'What type of market are we in?'" Then use the trading style that suits it.
- Most market explanations are "rationalizations", simple stories for complex situations.
- A hit ratio near 50 percent with an average payoff of three to one makes a lot of money. Being wrong half the time makes stopping out easier.
- Golf: you control the process, never the result.
- "Illiquid markets put a lot of pressure on you to be right." He stays liquid "to reserve the right to be wrong".
- Central bankers "have influence but not control". People swing from thinking them omniscient to thinking them impotent.
- Global macro "is agnostic to market direction; you just want things to happen". Many other strategies are bull market strategies.

## Anti-patterns
- **Pain-threshold stops**: the trader stops out at 1 percent, never believes he was wrong, and re-enters the same trade the next day. "The illusion of discipline."
- **Buying options as insurance at any price**: "I buy options when they are cheap, not for insurance."
- **Counting gross exposure as risk**: cutting gross from two times to one time is not a reduction if volatility went from 20 percent to 80 percent and correlations are uncertain. Risk could have doubled or risen tenfold.
- **Calling 2008 a black swan** to avoid responsibility. "The other hypotheses always existed."
- **Benchmark incentives**: if underperformance gets you fired, managers take excess liquidity risk until an absolute drawdown.
- **Countertrend trades before the trend is exhausted**: his 2006 long volatility.
- **Diversification as drawdown control**: it reduces volatility, not drawdowns. Liquidity risk cannot be diversified.
- **Treating policy makers as in control**: his first-week ERM call in 1992 was wrong within three hours.

## Worked Examples
- **Deflation priced forever (end 2008)**: US government bonds priced deflation forever. His only question was whether people *might* come to think inflation possible. Quantitative easing answered yes, because many monetarists exist. So the pricing was "completely lopsided".
- **UK rates (second quarter 2008)**: hypothesis: the UK economy is deteriorating fast, so rates will be cut. The market traded on inflation instead, with commodity prices rising. He exited "well before it hit a stop loss" because the hypothesis was falsified by price action and inflation data.
- **Fed funds (presented 23 October 2008)**: Fed funds would trade near zero before year-end because the Fed could not mop up excess liquidity at positive rates. People told him he was wrong and none could say why. The edge came from knowing the plumbing.
- **LIBOR spike (August 2007)**: it showed a systemic banking crisis. The right response was to sell anything illiquid or able to become illiquid. Most losses came in the second half of 2008, "a full year after the bubble had clearly popped". In big bubbles you can be months late and still survive.
- **Commodity currencies (2007)**: he rode the bubble through liquid currencies until August, then "flipped my book in a day".
- **Lehman (September 2008)**: Paulson said clearly he would not bail it out. The fund cut leverage the week before and had no open deals with Lehman. His error was to underestimate the disorder that followed.
- **Misses**: 1999 and 2006, when he was stuck on fundamental value. In 2006 he also expected the Fed to keep hiking. It accommodated the bubble.

## Views stated in 2009 (dated)
- The financial crisis and deleveraging problem are over because governments backstopped the banks.
- Most likely: a Japan-style malaise. Alternative: overstimulation and a 1970s outcome. The US more likely ends in inflation, Europe stays deflationary.
- Policy divergence between central banks will raise volatility. The Fed may target growth while the ECB targets inflation.
- One 10-year trade: inflation-linked debt, because he can predict nothing over 10 years.
- All beta and beta-plus assets "equal nominal GDP in the long term". Without skill, nominal GDP is a fair benchmark.

## Key Takeaways
1. Write the falsifier before the trade. Size from it.
2. Trade the gap between priced probabilities and the range of reasonable opinions.
3. Treat liquidity as a risk factor in every market.
4. Adjust positions for volatility and correlation before saying risk is cut.
5. Hold illiquid assets only if you can hold to maturity with secured funding and do not care about drawdowns.
6. Learn the funding plumbing. In 2008 it decided the outcome.

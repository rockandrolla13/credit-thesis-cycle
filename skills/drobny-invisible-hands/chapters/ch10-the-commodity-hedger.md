# Chapter 10: The Commodity Hedger (anonymous, ex Cargill, runs a global macro fund)

Type: MIXED. The hard-coded risk rules and the risk collar are METHOD. The trades of 1991, 1993, 1994, 1999, 2001, 2007 and 2008 are CASE. The source refers to this manager as "she".

## Core Idea
Look at every market through real assets first. Enter only at a value price that passes two hurdles, cap each loss with fixed rules, and put a near-zero-cost option collar on every full position so that a shock hedges you without forcing you out.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Hard-coded risk discipline** (her exact rules):
  1. A hard stop of 2 percent of portfolio assets per trade.
  2. Delever a theme when it is down 5 percent.
  3. Delever the whole portfolio at down 10 percent, and review the analysis.
  4. Risk collars on all full positions.
  - She manages on "absolute dollar stops". VaR and quantitative analysis are monitoring tools only.
- **Risk collar**: for a long, sell out-of-the-money calls to buy out-of-the-money puts. Inverse for a short.
  - Strikes come from her price model's two-week overvaluation and undervaluation zones. Options run two weeks to two months.
  - Cost is close to zero. Most expire worthless, "which is exactly what I would like them to do".
  - Why it beats a stop: a stop removes the position. The collar hedges it and keeps it, so the position returns if the market rebounds. She calls this "positive convexity".
  - She says the collars dampen volatility and do not truncate returns, because she uses them dynamically.
  - Made mandatory after 11 September 2001.
- **Two valuation hurdles before entry**:
  1. *Fundamental economic value.* Supply and demand, crunched over several months. "At core, I am a value trader, not a momentum trader."
  2. *Price analysis model.* A proprietary technical model that finds overvalued and undervalued zones, "bull traps, bear traps, and zones of value". One-year horizon for trades.
  - Entry is scaled across "very wide zones".
- **Reward-to-risk minimum**: about three times the risk. On a negative carry trade the target is often five times.
- **Exit on invalidation**: if the underlying hypothesis does not materialize she exits, "even if the trade is making money". "Making money for the wrong reasons means you are rolling the dice, not trading."
- **The extremes scan**: read the statistics pages of *The Economist* for extreme values. The highest and lowest nominal interest rates, the strongest and weakest currencies, the highest and lowest stock markets and commodities. For commodities compare price with cost of production. Then ask whether each extreme is justified.
- **Theme first, then trades**: themes are world trends (remittances, climate volatility, commodity inelasticity), not asset classes or regions. Work top-down to every country and market affected, then bottom-up to instruments. Start small and add as digging confirms.
- **The real asset lens**: start with supply and demand for goods, services and commodities, then infer rates and currencies. She believes commodities and real assets "drive movements in other markets more so than vice versa".
- **Liquidity is priced inversely to valuation**: "The need for liquidity is often inversely related to valuation." In 2009 she says liquidity is "overvalued" and illiquid assets are cheap for those with cash and horizon. For illiquids, study "the supply and demand of the supply and demand": who else holds it and how crowded it is.

## Key Concepts
- **Notional leverage**: she quotes leverage on notional value. Futures and currency trades need 3 to 5 percent cash margin. She never borrows to lever and never goes negative cash.
- **Zones of value**: the price bands from her model, used for entries, targets and collar strikes.
- **Housekeeping trading roles**: rolling currency positions, repo and reverse repo, stock borrow and lend, futures rolls. She calls them "integral to the process of learning the pulse of any market".
- **Gold as a funding currency**: borrowing gold at under 1 percent to fund higher-yielding assets.
- **Intracommodity decoupling**: her expectation that food and agricultural prices part from energy and base metals.

## Mental Models
- "The science of trading ponders the past, the art of trading focuses on the future." Most funds overweight past correlations.
- Everything quantitative and widely available "is all in the rear-view mirror". Look for the piece of information others consider insignificant.
- The same input gives a different output in a different environment. Ask what characterizes today and how it differs from 1929, 1973 or 1998.
- "Always ask." A railroad cut its rate from $3.05 to $1.05 per hundredweight because she asked and offered guaranteed volume.
- Do not chase trades. Stay informed and "wait for trades to come to me".
- For allocators: "manager, manager, manager". Judge the ability to make money and preserve capital, not the asset class.

## Anti-patterns
- **Stops without collars in a shock**: on 11 September 2001 she stopped out at 2 percent per trade, took a 10 percent portfolio hit, and watched several trades bounce back.
- **Shorting on historical valuation alone**: her worst trade, short the S&P at just over 400 in late 1991.
- **Passive commodity indices**: because of how they roll, they "practically guarantee that you overpay for exposure". Weights follow past correlations and flows. Real money said it would hold through a 30 percent fall, and then "everyone sold".
- **Diversifying to avoid blame**: "Diversification, diworsification." She prefers "rational and purposeful concentration".
- **Policy allocations fixed forever**: she rejects the consultant view that timing does not work, especially for equities.
- **Trades that do not match fund liquidity**: in 2008 she passed on good opportunities for this reason.

## Worked Examples
- **Gold carry, 1993 (her best year)**:
  1. Scan: gold had the lowest nominal interest rate in the world and traded 30 to 40 percent above the highest global cost of production. Canadian provincial bonds had some of the highest rates in the developed world.
  2. Borrow gold at $410 as funding, at under 1 percent.
  3. Buy one-year Saskatchewan, Alberta and British Columbia bonds at 9 to 12 percent, with some leverage.
  4. Hedge the gold short with low-cost small-cap gold miners (cost below $250 an ounce).
  5. When gold reached her interim target of $350, buy Australian small-cap miners that had fallen over 50 percent.
  6. All three legs worked. She did several weeks of checking before entry.
- **Philippine peso, 1994**: the market was short the peso on inflation fears, with rates at 14 to 15 percent. She saw steady remittances from overseas workers that nobody counted. USD/PHP had peaked at 30 in late 1993. She went long the peso at 27.50 and bought local paper at 13 to 14 percent.
- **Yield curve inversion (Greenspan conundrum era)**: real asset inflation lifts the short end. Productivity and a core CPI that excludes food and energy hold the long end. She bought long bonds and curve inversion trades.
- **August 2007**: markets fell through the protective strikes on most of her 35 positions. Exposure dropped from a few billion dollars to minimal with no trading and no slippage. Within two weeks the positions were back above the collars. The fourth quarter made the year.
- **2008**: gross leverage reached almost four times and core positions moved 30 to 60 percent against her in two months. The fund closed the year flat. She honoured all redemptions with no gate or side pocket.
- **Oil, 2008**: in August, with oil at $115, she said publicly it would go to $80 and privately thought $50. She shorted only in small size near $140 because a geopolitical spike skewed the risk. She did not foresee the wider commodity fall, and underestimated how fast index money would leave agricultural markets.
- **Hang Seng, 1998 to 1999**: premise: lack of transparency would stop flows into Asia. The short made money but flows did not stop, so she exited.
- **Gold, 1999**: bought at $250 with a multi-year target of $500 to $600. Took profit at the one-year model target of $320.

## Views stated in 2009 (dated)
- A lower-return world, with growth of 0 to 3 percent and long rates converging to low levels.
- Inflation in real assets, not in consumer prices. Large overcapacity.
- Developed-country equities are "the most overinvested, overvalued, mismanaged asset class in the world".
- Bullish agricultural commodities and farmland on a five-year view. Bearish base metals and energy. Oil in a $50 to $80 range or lower. She does not believe in peak oil.
- Latin America to outperform Asia on demographics and resources.
- The dollar to fall 30 to 50 percent over 5 to 10 years.
- US unfunded liabilities of about $125 trillion against world GDP near $60 trillion. "The more debt we issue, the safer we are."
- Endowment mix today: about a third farmland, 20 to 30 percent selective high-yield fixed income (for example Brazilian sovereigns), no equity indices, 20 to 30 percent with good risk managers.

## Key Takeaways
1. Write loss limits as numbers at trade, theme and portfolio level.
2. Use a collar where a stop would throw you out of a good position.
3. Demand two independent valuation tests before entry.
4. Exit when the reason fails, whatever the profit.
5. Match portfolio liquidity to the liquidity promised to investors.

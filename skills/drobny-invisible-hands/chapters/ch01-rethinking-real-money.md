# Chapter 1: Rethinking Real Money

Type: METHOD (the author's framing essay), with the history of US pensions and endowments as CASE material. Includes points from the Foreword (Jared Diamond) and the Preface.

## Core Idea
Real money funds (unlevered pensions, endowments, foundations, family offices, sovereign funds) lost 20 to 40 percent in 2008 because they built portfolios on return targets, history and illiquidity premia. Drobny says they should copy the risk-first habits of the global macro managers who made money or kept capital that year.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three macro principles for real money** (Section III). Real money managers should:
  1. *Replace return targets with risk-adjusted return targets.* Big drawdowns and volatility matter. Account for extreme worst cases with overlays, tail hedges, or a less aggressive allocation with truly diversifying exposures.
  2. *Look forward, not backward.* History of an asset class or fund is not a good guide. Consider the macro environment "first and foremost".
  3. *Rethink liquidity.* Do not undervalue liquidity when the world looks benign. Do not overvalue the return from illiquidity. "Time horizons are much shorter than generally believed."
- **Four flaws of the Endowment Model exposed in 2008** (Section II, "The Crash of '08"):
  1. Diversification with a high equity orientation is not diversification. International equity, long-biased equity hedge funds, private equity and venture capital are one bet. Real assets are equity-like because they depend on nominal growth.
  2. Valuation matters, for equities, real estate and liquidity alike. Early entrants bought illiquids cheap. Followers drove the price up.
  3. Limited-partnership commitments are a form of leverage. They give the portfolio "a short option-like profile".
  4. Time horizons are shorter than assumed for investors with annual cash needs.
- **The macro manager's risk questions** (Section III): What does this trade add to total portfolio risk? What are the true risks of each position? In a worst case, how much can the portfolio or the position lose? The manager asks these "regardless of valuation metrics".
- **The peasant rule** (Foreword, Diamond): do not maximize long-run average yield. Maximize yield only above the minimum that avoids ruin in any one year, then add a small safety margin.

## Key Concepts
- **Real money**: a fully funded, long-only, unlevered traditional asset manager. Leverage, where used, is non-recourse (for example as a limited partner in a levered fund).
- **Endowment Model (Yale Model)**: a broadly diversified portfolio with a heavy equity orientation that seeks a premium for illiquidity (Swensen, *Pioneering Portfolio Management*, 2000).
- **60-40 policy portfolio**: 60 percent stocks and 40 percent bonds, the standard benchmark of the 1980s and 1990s.
- **Over-commitment**: committing up to two times the target allocation to private funds, relying on distributions from old funds to pay new capital calls.
- **Denominator effect**: an illiquid allocation rises as a share of the fund when the rest of the fund falls.
- **Siegel's Paradox** (the author's label): losses and gains are not symmetric. A 50 percent loss needs a 100 percent gain.
- **The kink**: "the pain of investment losses is not linear". Past a point, losses force the institution to change behaviour.
- **Pension funding level**: both liabilities and asset returns are estimates. A small change in the discount rate moves the reported funding level a lot. "It's a big guessing game."

## Mental Models
- Think of each real money regime as a reaction to the last loss. Bonds until 1970s inflation. Then 60-40 through the Great Moderation. Then the Endowment Model after the 2000 to 2003 equity crash. Then 2008.
- Treat crowding as a risk in its own right. Real money managers "often share the same consultants and occasionally the same board members", so portfolios that each look diversified become correlated with each other.
- Ask why the Endowment Model worked in 2000 to 2003 and failed in 2008. The author's answer: crowding, and the sheer size of assets in illiquids and alternatives, often through leverage.

## Anti-patterns
- **Hiding behind benchmarks and peers**: "Losing less than peers or benchmarks does not provide the annual cash flow needs of pensioners, universities, and charities" (Preface).
- **Treating 2008 as a one-in-a-hundred-year storm that has passed**: the Preface calls this "quite simply poor risk management".
- **Sticking to one style regardless of valuation or environment.**
- **Claiming equities win in the long run without stating the risk taken.**
- **Fast, large reallocations on consultant advice**: see the worked example below.

## Worked Example: "Public Pension Goes Endowment" (boxed text)
1. November 2006: a ballot vote lets a state pension fund invest outside the US for the first time. Until then it held half US listed equities and half US government bonds.
2. The treasurer hires a CIO who had run the Endowment Model elsewhere.
3. Consultants plan to turn a two-slice pie into dozens of slices, with portable alpha (leverage) and an over-commitment to private equity. They execute fast through swaps, special purpose vehicles and block trades.
4. The plan starts in 2007. In 2008 total assets fall by a third and much of the rest is illiquid.
5. The fund still wins an industry award for reform.
6. The author's counterfactual: with the old portfolio, assets would be about 20 percent higher, "a few billion dollars".

## Key Takeaways
1. Anchor portfolio construction in the worst case first and expected return second.
2. Count committed but uncalled capital as exposure.
3. Test a "long-term" claim against the annual cash the institution must pay.
4. Check what share of the portfolio depends on equity and nominal growth, whatever the labels say.
5. The author quotes CalPERS CIO Joseph Dear: the standard allocation method rests on normal returns and historical correlations, which are "empirically false".

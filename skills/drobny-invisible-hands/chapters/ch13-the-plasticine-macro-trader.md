# Chapter 13: The Plasticine Macro Trader (anonymous, equity specialist turned macro, ex long-only manager)

Type: MIXED, mostly CASE. The risk rules (gross exposure, thirds, trend confirmation) are METHOD. The debt deflation thesis, the China view and the 2008 trades are CASE and dated 2009.

## Core Idea
The last 30 years were a historical anomaly for equities, so reputations and benchmarks built on them mislead. He holds a contrarian "macro prejudice", waits for a price trend to confirm it, expresses it through hundreds of small parts, and manages risk by moving gross exposure up and down.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The anomalous 30 years**: stock data go back to 1720 and it is "almost impossible to find a period comparable to the last 30 years". Inflation, taxes and interest rates collapsed, so returns on capital exploded. Long-only stars "dominated the zeitgeist of the age". Returns were measured "off an absolute low point".
  - Counter-evidence he cites: without dividends, no real appreciation in the Dow from 1906 to 1974. The US market took 25 years in real terms to pass its 1966 high.
- **Debt deflation thesis** (his central view): when society "develops revulsion for debt", asset prices take the brunt and it takes 30 to 40 years to correct the excess. His question to long-only managers: "What happens if we exhaust the private sector's willingness to take on more leverage?"
- **Risk defined properly**: the industry defines risk "as being a function of market deviations rather than the likelihood of losing clients' money". He approaches risk "by considering the worst possible outcome".
- **The cockroach mandate**: "being a survivor no matter what". In 2006 and 2007, when option volatility was cheap, his long-only portfolios spent 1 percent of assets on out-of-the-money index puts, rolled quarterly.
- **Trend-following contrarian**: "Even a true contrarian is only really contrarian about 20 percent of the time." The rest is trend following.
  - Rule: form the contrarian view, then wait for "the legitimacy of a positive price trend". "You won't find me buying things in a downtrend."
  - "I am always late, I will never be early. But I never lose much money when I am wrong."
  - Origin: his worst trade, Reader's Digest, was "a potent idea that wasn't subject to the scrutiny of the chart".
- **Volume control on pundits**: when an extreme view (US hyperinflation) is voiced with confidence and not challenged, it has "become an acceptable currency". That usually means most investors already lean that way. He turns that voice down.
- **Gross exposure is the risk tool**: "risk management is all about manipulation of gross exposure." "If you're fearful, you could buy some put options, but the best strategy is to take the blasted exposure down."
  - Options are for when timing is off and he wants to keep an idea "without killing ourselves".
- **The rule of thirds**: "if in doubt, buy a third or sell a third." Put on one third of the normal size. If right, you have a position. If wrong, it was only a third.
- **The centipede portfolio**: hundreds of positions express one prejudice. He can "lose a few legs but keep moving". A focused fund of best ideas is the opposite, because "best" is emotionally charged and giving it up means admitting error.
- **Inflation by way of deflation**: for a commodity super cycle to run, a deflationary shock must come first. Central banks then cut rates to zero and hold them. So his rule for 2006 to 2008 was: "if you think the future is inflation, buy government bonds." He made money on rate contracts and government bonds in 2008.
- **The creditor-surplus pattern**: a creditor nation runs a persistent trade surplus and stops its currency from rising. The liquidity goes into asset prices. He names three cases: the US in the 1920s, Japan in the 1980s, China in the 2000s. Each left a boom and bust. He calls this "the real quantitative easing".
- **Fear as an allocation test**: the positions that yield big returns "always involve fear and a sick feeling" of being wrong alone. If an allocation feels good and nobody objects, step back. A $10 billion hedge fund is "the pinnacle of consensus".

## Key Concepts
- **Macro prejudice**: his own standing bias, stated openly so it can be controlled.
- **Franchise risk**: long-only firms fear losing more than peers, not losing money. It is "okay to lose money as long as everyone else is losing money".
- **A-B-D ("Anything but the dollar")**: the 2009 consensus stance toward inflation bets.
- **Minsky moment**: the point at which debt-fuelled speculation unravels and no bid can be found (boxed text).
- **Binary world**: markets swing between inflation and deflation with everyone "all in or all out". He sees no real diversification left.
- **Economic tourist**: he travels to get away from the screen, and suspects travel hurts the trade.

## Mental Models
- "Oil found in hell" (Buffett's story): the tycoon starts the rumour and then follows the crowd himself. He applies it to the 2009 rush back into equities and commodities.
- Don Coxe's saying: the best opportunities are "where the people who know it best love it least".
- "Invest first, and investigate later" (Soros). Livermore bought before he shorted, to feel the position.
- "Markets are irrational but they are right at every moment. They are right until they are wrong."
- Fundamentals "just reinforce the trend" (he credits Robert Prechter).
- Losing 30 percent leaves 70. Getting back needs 43 percent.
- A manager who is uncorrelated and contentious is the only diversifier left. "Why sacrifice precious liquidity for correlated return streams?"
- "How do smart people keep fucking it up?" His answer: non-linear events catch orthodox intelligence "time and time again".

## Anti-patterns
- **Benchmark thinking**: "arrogant people who think it's okay to lose 30 percent of their clients' money so long as the peer group loses 31".
- **Ignoring macro as a stock picker**: managers who boast they do not know where oil, rates or government are going.
- **A false barbell hedge**: see the worked example.
- **Letting fundamentals pull you into the trend at the extreme**: in summer 2008 he replaced oil futures with calls when he "should have been buying out-of-the-money puts".
- **Buying what is already popular**: gold in 2009, large hedge funds, inflation bets.
- **Paying active fees for passive execution** in a long deflation. Own cash, bonds and an ETF, and pay only for a truly active manager.
- **The Church of England pension fund**: went 100 percent into equities in 1999. He cites assets of £461 million against liabilities of £813 million.

## Worked Example: the barbell that was one position (first half of 2008)
1. *Positions.* Long commodities as the dominant trend. Long front-end rates because he feared a deflationary shock.
2. *December 2007 to February 2008.* He made "a huge amount of money on both views".
3. *What that meant.* "There was no hedge, after all." Both positions pointed one way.
4. *March 2008.* Both reversed. It was his largest monthly drawdown since launch.
5. *Lesson.* Profits on both sides of a hedge are the warning. Cut gross on both sides.

## Worked Example: Potash, summer 2008
He praised Potash Corporation on television, noticed that everyone agreed, and sold. From July to October 2008 it fell from $260 to $90 and moved in line with other risk assets. He would still make the fundamental case. "Fundamentals are on one side and an emotional landscape of human psychology and behavior is on another."

## Worked Example: the worst case for a real money fund (his test)
1. Take the Harvard fiscal 2010 policy portfolio (table below).
2. Almost half is in emerging equities, commodities, real estate, private equity and junk bonds. "This portfolio will work if the dollar keeps falling or the world has inflation."
3. Worst case: a repeat of the fourth quarter of 2008, with 30-year Treasuries at 2.5 percent.
4. Liabilities then need 20 percent or more a year from the risky assets, exactly when their returns look worst. "Insolvency would beckon."

## Views stated in 2009 (dated)
- Debt deflation is more likely than inflation. Bank loans are still contracting. If risk rolls over while everyone holds inflation bets, "bonds could go parabolic".
- The Fed is "tackling a Minsky moment through the lens of Milton Friedman" and may decide too early that the worst is over. Later generations may say central banks should have done more.
- Volcker parallel: a hawk produced a melt-up in gold in 1979. A dove may produce one in long bonds.
- China is "a very deep out-of-the-money call option on the U.S. economy". Decoupling "is a joke". The 2009 fiscal surge added capacity and left China "very vulnerable" for the first time in two decades. Infrastructure is mismatched with income of about $3,000 a head.
- US GDP may be no more than $20 trillion in 10 years.
- A flat renminbi since mid-2008 is his indicator. A rising one would make him drop the bond view.
- Japan: after another risk-off move with dollar/yen near 80, he fears Japan debauches the currency and the Nikkei returns to 40,000. Not yet.
- Agriculture is the one equity area he might buy.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask whether a track record is skill or a 30-year tailwind.
2. Hold the contrarian view and wait for the trend before paying for it.
3. Cut gross first. Buy options second.
4. If both legs of a hedge make money, it is not a hedge.
5. When in doubt, do a third.
6. Test a real money portfolio against low long yields and falling risk assets at the same time.

# Chapter 9: The Commodity Investor (anonymous, trades commodities and commodity equities at a large firm)

Type: MIXED. Triangulated conviction, the threshold method and the factor risk system are METHOD. The 2007 corn trade, the 2008 oil short, the 2009 restocking trade and the China and inflation views are CASE and dated 2009.

## Core Idea
Combine the macro context with micro supply-chain detail, and take size only when both agree across asset classes. Look for the few markets close to an inventory threshold, because that is where prices move non-linearly.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Triangulated conviction**: check conviction across macro factors, micro factors, and the asset classes (commodities, equities, currencies, bonds). Play "in real size" only when all agree. Hold no risk when they do not. He rejects the idea that you must always be invested to keep a feel for the market.
- **The threshold method (supply in the context of demand)**:
  1. Never read inventory alone. 100 million barrels means nothing without the demand growth rate.
  2. Use stocks-to-use curves to find the threshold level for each product.
  3. Inside the balanced range there is "no convenience yield of any consequence". Past the threshold "a nonlinear fundamental event will lead to a corresponding nonlinear price event": a squeeze, or an oversupply that collapses the term structure.
  4. Of about 80 liquid products, roughly 5 are near a threshold at any time, bullish or bearish.
  5. Filter: Does it fit the macro view? Is there a stocks-to-use constraint? Does qualitative information support it? Is it already discounted in the forward market?
- **Macro plus micro, never one alone**:
  - Macro-only failure: macro funds bought front-month corn on the ethanol story. A bumper crop came that year. The structural change took one to two more years to affect balances.
  - Micro-only failure: copper looked cheap on stocks and use a year before the interview. The macro signal was a credit collapse, which caused "epic destocking".
- **Cyclical versus secular**: "Cyclical analysis is all about microeconomic imbalances, whereas secular is all about macroeconomic imbalances." First find the prevailing market bias. Then ask whether macro and micro are "in concert or in conflict". In conflict, keep risk low. At extremes people mistake one for the other.
- **Four judgments for every idea**:
  1. *Direction*: long or short.
  2. *Duration*: commodities versus equities. Commodities tend to be slightly longer duration.
  3. *Diffusion*: how far a fundamental event spreads across the supply chain.
  4. *Expected value*: own estimates against intrinsic value and consensus.
- **Risk system in two parts**:
  - *Defensive*: measure the portfolio daily against macro factors over several time frames. Calculate the dollar beta to each. Flag unintended exposures.
  - *Offensive*: watch rates of change in core factors to see whether the macro matrix is starting to cross-correlate. Cross-correlation means higher volatility ahead. Then cut gross and net exposure.
  - Then check diversification, then the correlation matrix of the top 10 strategies by exposure.
- **Risk units**: size each position by how much daily swing the portfolio can tolerate, so trades are comparable. Equal dollar positions in natural gas and gold "would be completely inappropriate".
- **Liquidity measure**: volumes on 30-day and 60-day bases against 180-day and 360-day figures. The rate of change is "the most important element".
- **Verification chain for a theme**: Is there truly no inventory? Where is it? Do regional arbitrages or physical differentials show distortion? Is the expression in the commodity or in equities? If equities, is it upstream, midstream or downstream?

## Key Concepts
- **Convenience yield**: the premium a buyer pays for prompt physical delivery. It moves inversely to inventory.
- **Single point volatility**: his term for commodity markets gapping faster since speculative flows grew.
- **Resource nationalism**: "if you have it, you are going to keep it". Export tariffs and keeping value-added processing at home.
- **Tradable float**: the commodity supply available to the market. He expects it to shrink as China buys resources.
- **Export land model** (he credits an oil consultant): producers consume more of their own output. Since 2000 Middle East oil production rose 11 percent, net exports 5 percent, domestic demand 40 percent.
- **Analytical Sharpe versus trading Sharpe**: the gap between the quality of the ideas and the profit captured.
- **Time on the water**: experience that builds "psychological capital".
- **NAIRU, dependency ratio, SDR**: defined in boxed text.

## Mental Models
- "If I am 60 percent long commodities, I am really just a currency fund in drag." With commodities highly correlated to the dollar, that is about a 50 percent dollar short.
- Adding hedges when volatility rises raises gross exposure and adds basis risk "precisely when overall portfolio risk should be reduced". Cut positions instead.
- Just-in-time supply chains are "perfectly calibrated to a world where nothing goes wrong" (from Barry Lynn's *The End of the Line*). A squeeze can occur in a low-growth world because the chain cannot adapt fast enough.
- At extremes, good macro investors "interpolate based on small data points". Committees "extrapolate based on recent past experience".
- "Fear blinds market participants to the self-correcting mechanisms at the extremes."
- Equity tails differ from commodity tails because new equity supply can be issued. In commodities the only short-run fix is demand destruction at higher prices.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading supply and demand as separate numbers.**
- **A risk system that does not adapt position size to the volatility regime.**
- **Establishing a full position at once**: his August 2007 grain trade.
- **Letting fear or denial override the process.**
- **Risk management under the CIO alone**: it must be shared or independent so it is enforced.
- **Committees chasing what did well recently**: "survivorship bias".

## Worked Examples
- **Oil, 2008**: his research showed that the "secular" rise in the cost of oil production was cyclical, because much of that cost depends on the oil price itself. Worried about demand destruction, he shorted commodities while peers stayed long.
- **Restocking, early 2009**: his standing theme was that just-in-time inventory would destabilize commodity markets. A metals CEO told him that in a delevered world firms compete on volume, not price, so once credit returns there is a rush to restock. The CEO already saw it in his order book. Producers had run inventory down for cash while "the system was sized for Armageddon".
- **Brazil crop tour, fall 2008**: his agronomist learned that agricultural companies were cutting $40 to $50 billion of fertilizer and crop financing to farmers. He put lower acreage and yield into the models.
- **Corn, August 2007**: he built a large grain position early. The USDA raised yield expectations against his research. Grains had their largest one-day fall since 1994, costing almost 3 percent of the fund. Six weeks later the USDA revised down and prices soared. Lesson: scale into core positions. Official data and field information can stay apart for a while.
- **2008 sizing**: with the VIX in the 70s his stock positions were about a quarter of normal.
- **Counterparty risk, 2008**: cutting exposure for counterparty risk "superseded the goal of maximizing profits". He calls it fiduciary duty.

## Views stated in 2009 (dated)
- Stagflation is "in the making": commodity inflation at growth too low to create jobs.
- Hyperinflation is unlikely because weak unions and capital-for-labour substitution block a wage-price spiral. The central risk is that Bernanke avoids the 1930s and gets the 1970s.
- A developed market sovereign debt crisis "looms", in a G3 nation.
- China: money and credit growing together is possible only in a semi-command economy. Risks are inflation, excess production because output is not driven only by profit, and the transition to a convertible currency.
- Several reasons point to higher forward interest rates.
- Ten-year trades: deferred oil through 10-year futures (his favourite), or deferred gold.
- For a pension fund: low developed-market bonds and equities, a majority in commodities and producing assets, both an index and an active sleeve, and quarterly tactical adjustment. "Liquidity is all that matters when it ceases to exist."

## Key Takeaways
1. Put every supply number in a demand context.
2. Hunt for thresholds. Leave balanced markets alone.
3. Take no risk without agreement across macro, micro and asset classes.
4. Measure hidden factor exposure daily, especially the dollar.
5. When correlations rise, cut gross. Do not add hedges.
6. Scale into core positions.

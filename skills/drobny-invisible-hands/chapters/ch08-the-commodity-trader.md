# Chapter 8: The Commodity Trader (anonymous, runs a large commodity hedge fund, ex energy derivatives trader)

Type: MIXED. Trade structuring and risk limits are METHOD. The super cycle thesis, the 1998, 1999 and 2008 trades, and the China data are CASE and dated 2009.

## Core Idea
Get the fundamentals right on supply and demand, then structure each trade so the payoff is asymmetric. With a positive reward-to-risk skew and a diverse book, you make money without needing to be right most of the time.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Supply trends, demand swings**: "with most commodities the supply side of the equation tends to trend, especially in energy and metals". Supply up to 18 months forward is "relatively predictable". Demand has been the volatile side for 20 years, so the macro outlook drives the demand assumption. Inputs: global industrial production, global GDP, and the US dollar.
- **Asymmetric trade structure**: a positive reward-versus-risk skew plus "a minimum 30 to 40 percent target rate of winning trades" gives profit over time.
  - Buy options when volatility is cheap. Options give "longevity" because the path from A to B and its timing are unknown.
  - Limit: "if you are not right about the fundamentals a reasonable amount of the time, option price decay will bleed you to death."
  - Stated weakness: if the hit rate falls to about 25 percent and markets do not move enough to give 5-to-1 winners, the fund is "lucky to be flat".
- **Portfolio limits he names**: VaR, margin-to-equity, portfolio P&L volatility, sector risk, individual position risk, vega, theta, and premium spent. Delever when losing money.
- **Equal-risk positions**: normally over 20 positions (he also says 20 to 30), none much larger than another "on a daily volatility and liquidity-adjusted basis". The exception is an option that runs deep into the money.
- **Target a fraction of the drawdown limit**: never work to the maximum drawdown. Target a much smaller level and measure positions and risk factors against a fraction of the limit.
- **Trade the back of the curve**: front months are driven by short-term physical factors. Physical storers and shippers know stocks, locations and quality, so they have the edge there. He structures trades six months out and beyond.
- **Ways for real money to hold commodities** (his ranking for an inflation hedge):
  1. Passive front-contract index: weakest. When markets are well supplied it is "very negative yielding". Owning a commodity costs storage, insurance and shipping.
  2. Enhanced index that optimizes roll yield: better.
  3. Active long-biased managers who manage downside: "the most attractive option", but manager selection is key. A manager who turns bearish in a bull cycle hurts twice.
  4. His advice for a pure inflation hedge: a mix of active management and enhanced indexing.
  - Commodity equities are "not a clean inflation hedge". A mine with supply or management problems can fall while the commodity rises.

## Key Concepts
- **Commodity super cycle**: a long demand upswing from emerging market population and income growth against finite resources. The boxed text cites Jim Rogers's three earlier bull markets (1906 to 1923, 1933 to 1955, 1968 to 1982) and a start in 1999.
- **Roll yield**: return from rolling futures in a backwardated market.
- **Backwardation**: spot above futures. **Contango**: futures above spot.
- **Headline versus core inflation**: core excludes volatile items, usually food and energy.
- **Crack spread**: a derivative commodity product he uses in spread trades (named, not defined).
- **Commodity squeeze**: control of deliverable stock to move front spreads. He says regulation has all but ended it.

## Mental Models
- Commodity curves swing harder than yield curves. Small changes in daily supply move exchange stocks, then front spreads, then the curve. The effect fades two or three years out.
- Any sudden drop in demand that causes "a sharp rise in storage levels" crashes prices, even inside a long upswing.
- "Speculators or investors can drive prices for very short periods of time, but fundamentals always win out in the long run." His evidence: coal, steel and minor metals, which outsiders could not buy, rallied at least as much up to mid-2008.
- A fund manager works against the high-water mark and competes with the yields on other assets. A prop trader restarts every 12 months.
- Speak in percentages, never in cash amounts.
- "It's a lot easier to analyze and trade soybeans after having traded energy" than the reverse.

## Anti-patterns
- **Oversizing against the life of the theme**: a good view, a big position, a tight stop, and an immediate stop-out. "Excitement and thoughtlessness kill 80 percent of would-be traders."
- **Marginal trades**: "Why would you ever put a marginal trade in your portfolio?"
- **Shorting a skyrocketing market**: flows can keep it irrational on momentum alone.
- **Overconfidence after a run**: "When the day comes in your career where you think, 'I'm really good at this,' you are clearly in trouble." Think twice before investing with someone who never had a significant drawdown.
- **Fading a rebound too early**: his 1999 macro shorts.

## Worked Example: his 1998 and 1999
1. *1998.* He plays for a large macro downturn across fixed income, currencies and commodities. He shorts oil from $23 to $11. LTCM and Russia fail. He makes over 100 percent.
2. *Early 1999.* He covers, thinking prices have fallen far enough.
3. *1999.* He watches the relief rally, then puts the bearish macro bets back on. Too early.
4. *Second half of 1999.* He shorts the Nasdaq, having drifted from commodities into financial futures. He stops out when AOL and Time Warner merge, thinking the index could reach 7,000 or 10,000. That deal was near the top.
5. *Result.* He loses about a third of his capital, his only large annual loss. He cuts risk and is flat the next year.
6. *His reading.* He had compounded at 50 percent a year and grown overconfident.

## Views stated in 2009 (dated)
- Core inflation is contained in the medium term by the output gap. The long-term picture is inflationary through commodities and emerging market demand. Mature economies with trade deficits and weak currencies face stagflation.
- US gasoline demand has already peaked.
- Ten-year longs: crude oil, soybeans, the Russian ruble. He names no commodity he would short long-term.
- Bearish sterling and cautious on UK commercial property.
- China restocks when prices are low and releases supply or cancels cargoes when prices are frothy. The 2008 price collapse was a "get out of jail free card" for it.
- Commodity markets traded in an orderly way through the 2008 fall, "the ultimate stress test".
- For a pension fund he would hold heavy weights in commodities, emerging markets and property, because "inflation is a pension fund's real risk".

## Key Takeaways
1. Model supply as a trend and spend the effort on demand.
2. Pay for optionality only when volatility is cheap and the fundamental view is strong.
3. Size by volatility and liquidity so positions carry equal risk.
4. Stay out of the part of the curve where someone else has better information.
5. A passive front-month index is a poor inflation hedge when the curve is in contango.

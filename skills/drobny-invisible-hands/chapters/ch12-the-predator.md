# Chapter 12: The Predator (anonymous, equity-focused fund with a macro overlay)

Type: CASE, with a few reusable rules. The interview is mostly his account of 2007 to 2009: when he turned bearish, when he went to cash, and why he went long in March 2009.

## Core Idea
A stock picker without a macro overlay becomes the prey. In a liquidity crisis valuation stops mattering and only cash or government bonds protect you. Half the money is made from other participants' mistakes, so study the actors as well as the asset.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two ways investors got 2008 wrong**:
  1. *Value managers who bought the dips.* They bought Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, AIG and Lehman because the models said cheap. They had played 1980 to 2008 that way. They did not see "the fracture", which came from "the break in liquidity".
  2. *Bears whose hedges failed.* Credit and liquidity problems made normal crash hedges "null and void".
- **Liquidity, defined**: "the number of days it takes to sell out of a position or the whole portfolio", and how fast he can cut leverage on no notice. It also depends on who else holds the name. "If you are the buyer of last resort, then there is no liquidity."
- **The zero test**: "A bear market is one in which everything tests zero then bounces back." To prepare, "value every asset in your fund at an absurdly low price and make sure that you can still survive" without forced selling.
- **Focus on the actors**: for each situation ask: Is long-only buying the dip? Is levered money involved? What are insiders doing? Is retail involved? Find the point at which each group lets go. "Half the money we make is at the expense of other market participants."
- **Consistency and trajectory over magnitude**: in June 2008 Lehman, Citigroup, Merrill and others reported bad results together. "The magnitude of the bad results was not as informative as their consistency and trajectory."
- **Become a different manager every six months**: his sequence from 2008 to 2009 was short seller, cash manager, corporate bond buyer, deep value stock buyer, then "a long-only index fund". Next he expects to be a dividend-yield buyer with low gross exposure.
- **Stock picking plus timing**: longs make the real money. Shorts and the macro overlay protect it. Over time each contributes about half. In 2008 timing did the work. In 2009 stock picking did.
- **Four risks of a hedge fund** (his list of what saved him):
  1. Counterparty risk.
  2. Liquidity risk.
  3. Leverage risk: prime brokers can pull leverage abruptly or reprice it. He had seen it in 1998.
  4. Client risk: all clients leaving at once because they fear the others will. He visited clients and showed them the portfolio.
- **The copying cycle**: "Success brings emulation, emulation brings leverage, and leverage eventually brings disaster." The disaster comes "not because the idea is wrong, but rather because there are too many people in it".
  - His rule for a model that has worked for 10 years: take 50 percent and do something different, even something basic such as Treasuries or an index fund with puts.
- **Idea plus trigger**: of ten things that could move a price, the skill is naming the one that will. "Identifying the trigger is as difficult as having the idea."

## Key Concepts
- **The fracture**: his word for the market break of fall 2008.
- **Wealth transfer**: capital moving from the participants who get it wrong to those who use the liquidity they provide.
- **Dimensions of thinking**: basic value funds are "one-dimensional": they only know how to buy more as it gets cheaper. The best have about five, and hold a hedge when they buy.
- **Second derivative**: the slowing of the rate of deterioration. He says he did not know the term at the time.
- **Macro overlay**: index shorts, puts and cash decisions laid over a stock portfolio.
- **Moderating versus decision-making boards**: endowments have the first, pensions the second.

## Mental Models
- "There is a big difference between being bearish and being right. There is always a lag." A short can take up to three years, until management has run out of ways to dress up the company.
- "You can never hedge a liquidity crisis." Only cash or government bonds work.
- "Stocks can look cheap, but they can continue getting cheaper until you are bust."
- "There is nothing I sell that I cannot buy back the next day." If you do not understand what is happening, get out. The re-entry level is irrelevant.
- A cheap small or illiquid asset is cheap because people like him will not buy it. Once it outperforms, everyone piles in, and it goes "from cheap to expensive and then crash".
- Who tells a long-term investor he is wrong? His clients tell him by leaving. An endowment has nobody.
- Committees "settle to the lowest common denominator". "By the time a committee decides to buy tech, it is already March 2000."
- "Luck is a term that people attribute to other people's talents."

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying all the way down**: his own worst trade, the 1987 crash. "Well, it's cheap."
- **Hedging single stocks with index futures sporadically**: the "great stock pickers" were whipsawed on the hedge, then sold at the bottom in early 2009 and went short.
- **Raising leverage into stress**: Lehman increased leverage in 2007 and in March 2008 and spent a billion dollars on buybacks in January 2008.
- **Being tactical after the event**: after 2008 "everyone wants to be tactical, and it is too late".
- **Hubris after a long run**: Harvard "sinned because of arrogance". The illiquid share was too large "exactly because these assets had done so well".
- **Running money through consultants or committees**: "If you have a committee, you should buy an index fund and stop trying."

## Worked Example: 2007 to 2009, step by step
1. *Summer 2007.* He turns bearish. Too early: equities peak in October 2007. He shorts Countrywide while a mutual fund buys millions of shares.
2. *Spring 2008.* He says at a conference the market may "go to hell" to pay for "an orgy of credit".
3. *June 2008.* Bank results are bad together and worsening. He "retrenched the fund violently", cutting leverage and raising cash months before others. He exits all financials and buys puts on the rest.
4. *September 2008.* Lehman fails. He goes fully to cash.
5. *End of November 2008.* He starts buying corporate and investment grade bonds at "absurdly cheap prices". By March 2009 each is up 10 to 15 points.
6. *The March 2009 signals*:
   - Early March: JPMorgan, Citigroup and others say they are making money again.
   - Economic data show the deterioration slowing.
   - He estimates global GDP contracted 6 percent while "something like 20 percent" in stimulus was being applied, much of it not yet deployed.
   - The interbank rate fell from a high of 5 percent in November 2008 to 2 percent. CDS levels were improving.
   - Some stocks traded below book value.
   - Investors visited him in panic and said he should be in cash.
7. *Execution.* He covers S&P shorts at 670, shorts again at 730, and worries at 820. The market opens down to 780 on news that GM may go bankrupt. Two days before quarter-end he closes every short and goes fully long.
8. *His admission.* The second half of March 2009 was his "worst two weeks ever".

## Worked Example: Softbank, 2005 (his best trade)
The stock fell for 18 months and traded below book value. He built the position while losing money, holding half a billion dollars of convertibles. In summer 2005 it turned and rose more than fourfold in six months. The convertibles doubled. He then shorted over a billion dollars of stock, and it fell 30 to 40 percent in December 2005. The trade took two years. "People have to be shorting for you to get a sizable position on and they have to be buying for you to get out."

## Views stated in late 2009 (dated)
- No inflation for now. Government money is "a fraction of the shrinkage of bank balance sheets and personal balance sheets". Inflation needs capital expenditure, lending and speculation. He allows it could come later.
- The big fear is behind us. He expects a slow recovery over three years, with more regulation, overcapitalized companies and higher taxes.
- He names no 10-year trade. "Nothing remains good for 10 years."
- At new highs he would go to cash and build shorts or puts.

## Key Takeaways
1. Mark the whole book at absurd prices and check you survive without selling.
2. Know who else is in the trade and when each type gives up.
3. In a liquidity break, drop valuation and cut gross.
4. Buy credit first when good companies' bonds reach distressed prices. He did so four months before he bought equities.
5. Watch interbank rates and CDS for the return of liquidity.
6. After long success, move half into something different.

# Chapter 2: The Researcher (Dr. Andres Drobny, Drobny Global Advisors)

Type: CASE. A named interview from late 2009 about the 2008 crash and the policy response. The macro reasoning rules inside it are his own and are dated to that moment.

## Core Idea
Private sector debt, not government debt, was the problem that had to "end in tears". Since 2008 the stock of that debt is falling, so the adjustment is under way. Whether it ends in deflation, muddle-through or inflation depends mostly on how long fiscal stimulus is kept in place.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Great Macro Experiment**: the attempt to use aggressive reflation to overcome debt deflation after a bubble bursts.
  - Phase 1: Greenspan's preemptive rate cuts of 2001 to 2003. From 2003 to 2007 it "appeared to have worked" because there was "another asset to inflate: property".
  - Phase 2, the "hyper-experiment": quantitative easing and bailouts after 2008.
  - Its double aim: avoid a 1931 to 1932 style second downturn and produce an FDR-style recovery. "It is not an easy task to do both."
- **Private debt versus public debt**: "The government can almost always fund its debt if it decides to print money; the private sector cannot." Stated exceptions: a gold standard, a fixed exchange rate, or an inflationary scenario.
- **Stock versus flow of debt**: the stock stays very high but it is falling, because households repay or default. He treats the direction of change as the investment signal. It is one reason he stopped being bearish on equities.
- **The bond-crisis test**: a true bond collapse needs rising nominal yields *and* steady or lower real yields. That means rising inflation.
  - Without inflation, higher nominal yields raise real yields. Bonds get cheaper, buyers return, or borrowing and activity slow. "Without inflation, it is hard to get a bond rout."
  - With inflation, bonds "don't actually get cheaper as prices fall". That is the disaster case.
  - Stated hedge for the disaster case: buy break-even inflation (long TIPS against nominal bonds).
- **Path to hyperinflation**: it usually starts when a fiscal deficit is monetized. The path runs through a period of healthy-looking recovery first. "You do not want to skip any steps here." The process takes longer than people assume.
- **Fiscal policy is underrated**: tax cuts aimed at "the investing classes" added a leg to the asset bubble in both 1925 and 2003. Tightening fiscal and monetary policy together in 1931 to 1932 caused the second leg down. Remove fiscal stimulus only "after it starts to work and the multipliers are taking over".
- **The corner solution**: standard theory optimizes subject to a budget constraint only. 2008 showed that an entity needs a second constraint: avoid losses large enough to imperil its existence or force severe spending cuts. The result is a second-best solution in which risk-adjusted return and volatility count.
- **The Bacow rule** (Larry Bacow, president of Tufts, as reported): "the greater the dependence of the operating budget on investment returns, the lower the risk profile should be, not the other way around."

## Key Concepts
- **Favorite trade**: the Drobny Global research format. Each participant brings one best idea and defends market, instrument and time horizon to peers. The expression "needs to value downside risk above all else".
- **Bond vigilantes**: bond market participants who push yields up in protest at inflationary policy. Example given: 10-year Treasury yields rose from 5.2 percent to over 8.0 percent between fall 1993 and fall 1994.
- **Break-even inflation**: nominal yield minus the real yield on an inflation-linked bond of similar maturity and credit quality.
- **TIPS**: Treasury inflation-indexed bonds. He stresses the principal guarantee in deflation.
- **Decoupling**: creditor countries raise domestic demand while debtor countries save. He had doubted it and says 2008 to 2009 proved him wrong.

## Mental Models
- "The pessimists are actually now the optimists" (from his 2005 interview, reprinted). If assets fall without rates rising, low rates cushion the fall. The worst outcome is the optimistic one, where recovery forces rates up more than markets price.
- Read a savings glut as a pair. More saving in the US must be matched by more spending and a narrower surplus abroad.
- Ask *why* the dollar is rallying before judging the effect. A rally from falling growth and asset deflation fits a double dip. A rally from fiscal-led growth and the end of easy Fed policy does not.
- Treat being wrong as data: "Being proven wrong provides great information content."

## Anti-patterns
- **Balancing the budget while the private sector retrenches**: "precisely the wrong thing to do". It is what Hoover did in 1931 to 1932.
- **Age-based bond weighting**: a 60-year-old who cut equities and raised bonds at the start of 2009 "virtually committed suicide".
- **Hedging illiquid assets with correlated liquid ones**: "You cannot hedge illiquidity in any way other than staying liquid." The hedge adds new risks.
- **Saying never**: his 2005 statement that he could hardly imagine being bullish on the dollar.
- **Holding a view against changed facts**: "a recipe for failure and constant distress".

## Worked Example: his own 2008 turn
1. *Prior view.* Bearish equities, and bearish emerging market equities since 2004. He says that view "had been very painful".
2. *Late 2008.* He moves from bearish to neutral, which means buying. He buys Asian equities.
3. *Stated reasons.* (a) Prices fell hard while stimulus had already started. (b) Asian currencies were cheap and got cheaper. (c) He had to admit he had been wrong on decoupling.
4. *Supporting observation.* Some investors were selling liquid equities to hedge illiquid holdings, so equities "were probably overshooting to the downside".
5. *Liquidity rule he took from it.* An illiquid position "has to be exceptionally small and nonmaterial" to enter his portfolio. He does not know how to price the premium.

## Views stated in late 2009 (dated, not verified in the book)
- One trade for ten years: TIPS, for the "safety margin". A floor in deflation and protection in an inflation-driven bond rout.
- The probability of inflation "has increased" relative to two years earlier. Deflation remains "very possible" if fiscal stimulus is withdrawn.
- A middle outcome is possible: 4 to 5 percent US nominal growth for a prolonged period.
- Dollar: officially neutral with a bullish lean. The US trade deficit fell far more than expected in 2008. A change in rate expectations could bring "a very powerful move up".
- Real estate is "a spread trade": repossessed property near 2000 prices against oceanfront at three to five times 2000 levels. He says the trade cannot be put on.

## Key Takeaways
1. Watch the direction of private debt, not only its level.
2. A bond-bear thesis must include an inflation view. Supply alone raises real yields, and higher real yields draw buyers.
3. Fiscal stance decides whether reflation holds. Watch political tolerance for it.
4. Liquidity lets you avoid forced decisions and gives you cash at extreme prices.
5. "Views sometimes count very little, whereas good risk management always counts a lot."

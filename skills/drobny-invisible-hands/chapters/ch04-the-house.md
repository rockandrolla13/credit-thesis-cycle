# Chapter 4: The House (anonymous, Stockholm, fixed income specialist)

Type: MIXED. His portfolio process and his prescription for a pension fund are METHOD. The credit bubble trades of 2005 to 2008 and the arbitrage trades of late 2008 are CASE.

## Core Idea
Be the casino, not the gambler: many small, reasonably independent bets with a slight edge, sized so that none can force a decision. Always pay for insurance and keep dry powder, because survivors of a crisis are "granted a license to print money for a while".

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The house model**: on average more than 100 positions and often more than 500 instruments. Each bet is small and reasonably independent, with a slight average edge. The aim is "to accumulate a number of small profits slowly".
- **Expected return is a function of price, not of risk**: "The expected return is a function of the risk premium of the asset at a given price." Saying return follows risk is wrong unless all markets are always priced correctly.
  - "A risky asset priced with a small risk premium is just a bad investment."
  - His two examples: Nasdaq equities in 1999 and risky bonds in 2007. Both had high risk and were "priced for a low future return".
- **How he recognized the credit bubble** (in order of weight):
  1. *The price of credit products.* "The bubble was easy to see just from looking at the price." This was the main signal.
  2. Interest rates "too low for much too long" after the dot-com crash.
  3. Credit expanding too fast to be sustainable from a macro view.
  4. Weak borrowers and credit too freely available. He calls these "just backup signs".
  - Method: look at market pricing, calculate "the price of likely defaults over cycles", and compare the risk premium. A back-of-the-envelope test, as with Nasdaq at 5,000.
- **Mistakes versus losses**: "losing money and making mistakes are not the same thing". He judges a period by whether he read the market, sized and hedged well, not by the result.
- **Running a pension fund (his prescription)**:
  1. List natural advantages and disadvantages. Advantages: balance sheet, creditworthiness, long horizon, liquidity. Disadvantages: constraints from regulators, benchmarks and boards, slow governance, weaker talent.
  2. Do not try to outguess the market in the short or medium term. Outsource that.
  3. Use the balance sheet to earn "risk-free liquidity" income: repo special securities, lend equities in demand, with the right counterparties and haircuts.
  4. Over multi-year horizons be overweight cheap asset classes and underweight expensive ones.
  5. Buy low-risk illiquidity only: government-guaranteed bank bonds, government bonds trading above LIBOR. Illiquid *risky* assets are "a totally different ballgame".
- **Risk level first, then gearing**: set an acceptable risk level from the mandate, in volatility, value-at-risk and maximum drawdown ("which may in fact be a better measure of risk"). Build the efficient mix. Then scale it. "I do not believe that the mix of the underlying assets has to add up to 100 percent."
- **Always-on insurance**: hold trades "that you can be fairly sure will benefit from increased turmoil". If you will not give up some upside for insurance, "you are not doing your job as a fiduciary".
- **Stress testing**: run the book over historical episodes (September 11, LTCM) and hypothetical scenarios, with "an emphasis on qualitative". The numbers should match logic and gut feeling. If they do not, look for a calculation error, a bad assumption, or something truly different.
- **Paradox of perfection** (boxed, from the 2006 book): as central bankers perfected their game, that perfection encouraged excess risk taking. His 2009 version: "central banks lowered risk premia enough to spark a bubble in credit markets."

## Key Concepts
- **Liquidity provider** (he credits Myron Scholes): an investor with long-term money who is paid for pockets of illiquidity. Hard for a levered fund with redemptions.
- **Dry powder**: "always, always, always keep dry powder". The cost is option premium or an allocation to low-yielding cash.
- **Reflexivity** (Soros): a feedback loop between participants' thinking and the situation. If everyone seeks the same diversification with the same instruments, it fails when needed.
- **Risk reversal**: write calls to buy puts for zero premium. Always available, "not always smart". Calls are often cheaper than puts "for good reasons".
- **Index tranche protection**: paying a small annual premium to be compensated for index losses between two attachment points.
- **Leverage ratio**: total assets to equity. Many banks were near 30 and were about 20 at the time of the interview.
- **Alpha**: zero-sum before costs by his definition, but alpha *seeking* is positive-sum for society through price discovery.

## Mental Models
- "Never play for so much that you might not be allowed to play again."
- "You never want someone else to decide your fate", whether the market, a regulator or a counterparty.
- CDS and tranches "behave like options". When spreads are very tight the downside is small, so you can hold.
- In turbulent markets assume correlations and volatilities are unknown. Market technicals can override logical correlations.
- Banks were the casino and then became "the big player at the table". That burned them.

## Anti-patterns
- **Equating risk with volatility.**
- **"Equities always generate good long-term returns, regardless of the price"**: "total baloney".
- **Levering a mispriced premium**: taking a risk premium that is "much too low" and gearing it into an illiquid product.
- **Selling catastrophe options cheaply**: "Risking survival for a few pennies is incredibly bad risk management."
- **Trusting ratings and consultants**: agencies rated a leveraged CDO and Iceland triple-A.
- **History as model input**: a pension fund locked in a 2 percent real yield for 30 years on inflation-linked bonds and assumed 4 percent in its model because that was the historical return.
- **80 to 90 percent in equities**, or diversifying heavily into private equity and real estate. He doubts the illiquidity premium is paid.
- **Falling in love with a trade** and hoping instead of testing the downside.

## Worked Example: gearing an efficient mix (his illustration)
1. Optimal mix on your assumptions: 35% equities, 15% corporate bonds, 10% real estate, 15% alternatives, 25% government bonds.
2. Its risk is only 80% of the risk level set for the fund.
3. Keep the mix and scale every position by 25%. Total underlying assets are 125%.
4. Get the exposure with swaps or futures, or with ETFs that have embedded leverage, or high-beta versions of instruments.
5. The reverse case: if the best value is in risky assets, hold a large passive cash or T-bill position and keep active risk assets well under 100%.

## Worked Example: the credit bubble trade, 2005 to 2008
1. *Entry.* Second half of 2005. "Much too early." It cost money through 2006.
2. *Structure.* Protection on tranches of credit indices. Example: a small annual premium against all losses between 6% and 9% of an index. The premium is the maximum loss. The maximum payout was "in some cases 500 times your money".
3. *Reasoning.* Losses of 6 to 9 percent on the index mean something near the Great Depression. Even at a once-in-500-years probability the seller only breaks even. At once in 50 or 100 years the seller is ruined.
4. *Pricing evidence.* Some CDOs, with leverage stripped out, offered about 10 basis points of yield pick-up after fees. He says risk-free liquidity provision paid more.
5. *Adding.* More in the first half of 2007, mainly the second quarter. Protection got cheaper while conditions worsened.
6. *Payoff.* The first installment came two years after entry. The option-like structure gave the staying power.
7. *Exit.* Early, because of counterparty risk against major banks. He doubted he could find buyers to take that risk later. He moved to long front-end rates (two-year Treasuries, German Schatz) and long-dated long volatility.
8. *Admission.* He did not know how large the bubble was, especially subprime. He never traded subprime.

## Worked Example: arbitrage in late 2008
- Three- to five-year US TIPS traded flat to LIBOR when he could fund them at LIBOR minus 50. After entry the spread went to plus 20, 40, 60, 80 and briefly LIBOR plus 100. He lost "a lot of money".
- He held, reasoning that LIBOR plus 100 priced a risk of US default on dollar debt, and "they can print the dollars needed".
- Other steps: cut every macro-view position except protective ones. Keep the portfolio easy to understand. Size each position so none can cause trouble. Be ready to hold three to five years to maturity.
- Balance sheet at year-end 2008: about three times equity, almost all government bonds.
- Long-dated fixed income volatility fell further after Lehman while every other implied volatility rose, because levered holders were forced out. He added.
- He put on more arbitrage trades at the start of 2009.
- Surprise of 2008: not the equity fall or the spread widening, but the number of "outright risk-free arbitrages".

## Views stated in 2009 (dated)
- Two forces fight: deflation from the economy and financial system, reflation from stimulus. He has no strong view and runs low macro risk.
- Stimulus is more likely to be withdrawn too late than too early, because of political pressure.
- Large deficits and excess reserves do not automatically bring inflation (Japan). An "inflation paranoia" is possible. So is a psychological bubble in gold.
- Protectionism hurts all risky assets. His hedge: own the short end of most sovereign bond markets.
- The weak link is rates staying "too low for too long".
- One 10-year trade: cheap US residential real estate (Florida, California, not the fancy addresses), or farmland, for utility value.

## Key Takeaways
1. Judge a credit market by its price against cycle-average default cost.
2. Structure a bubble short with limited, option-like downside so you can wait two years.
3. Plan the exit of a hedge around who can pay you.
4. Size so that no single position can force you out.
5. Balance sheet and funding are the scarce resource in a crisis. Bank repo capacity is no longer free.

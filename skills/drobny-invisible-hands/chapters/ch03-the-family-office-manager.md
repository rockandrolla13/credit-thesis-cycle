# Chapter 3: The Family Office Manager (Jim Leitner, Falcon Management)

Type: MIXED. The portfolio rules are METHOD. The 2007 to 2009 trades and the back tests are CASE. Interview dated 15 October 2009. Leitner sat on the Yale Endowment Investment Committee from 2004.

## Core Idea
Cash and liquid government bonds are assets with option value, not "trash". Overvaluation is a risk factor that portfolio construction must address directly. Truncate the left tail with a hard loss limit and with insurance bought when the market sells it cheaply.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The dynamic value of cash**: cash is "bounded on the lower side by its actual return". The upper side adds the return from being able to buy when others cannot.
  - Rule: "Holding cash when markets are cheap is expensive, and holding cash when markets are expensive is cheap."
  - His record: average cash balance 25 percent since 1997, with a high of about 90 percent.
  - Liquid government bonds count as cash-like. He is "loath to just call it cash".
- **Valuation-driven exposure**: as equity and equity-like assets get more expensive, hold more cash and cash-like assets.
  - Stated yardsticks: price to earnings, price to book, Tobin's Q (Smithers), cyclically adjusted price to earnings. He says he also asks many people for their sense of fair value.
  - *The weather analogy*: on 15 October you cannot say which January day will be coldest, but you can say January will be colder. Forecast the 7 to 10 year return, not next week.
  - "Overvaluation becomes a risk factor that must be addressed directly in portfolio construction."
- **Tail correlation rule**: models that use long-run correlations of 0.3 or 0.5 tell you to take too much risk if the tail correlation is one. Running the efficient frontier with correlations of one "would bring the efficient frontier in".
- **Diversification needs valuation dispersion**: it "only works when you have assets that are valued differently". If everything is expensive, everything falls. "The key is finding cheap things that will go up in scenarios where everything else is going down." Historically he finds only government fixed income did that.
- **Leverage to do something different**: leverage defined as "borrowing more to do the same thing" blows up. Leverage used to buy assets with a historically negative correlation can cut tail risk.
  - *Levered bond overlay*: bonds can be repo'd at the cash rate and carry a risk premium over cash. With an upward-sloping curve on average, the overlay is "insurance that, on average, makes you money".
  - Stated limit: in a run on the country, bonds fall with stocks. His answer to explore: lever a *foreign* bond portfolio on a US-centric equity portfolio.
- **Personal loss limits**: no more than 10 percent in any one month and 20 percent in any year. After a 10 percent loss he is "95 percent in cash" so he "cannot blow up two months in a row".
- **Risk premia hunting**: "Investing is the art and science of extracting risk premia from financial markets over time." Look across the whole universe for cheap premia to own and expensive premia to use as insurance against the portfolio.
- **Commitments scaled to investor size**: private equity commitments are "an implicit call on the future resources of the investor". He proposes commitments that shrink in proportion as the investor's asset base shrinks.
- **Equity index calls as inflation options**: equity indices are priced in nominal terms. A long-dated out-of-the-money index call "implicitly includes an option on future inflation".

## Key Concepts
- **Endowment Model view of cash**: any cash balance is wasted capital, because cash has the lowest backward-looking return.
- **Efficient frontier**: the set of portfolios with the highest return for each level of risk.
- **Repo**: selling a security with an agreement to buy it back. The price difference is the interest.
- **Negative compounding**: a fund down 40 percent needs 67 percent to get back to flat. Annual cash outflows make it longer.
- **Career risk**: large losses or lagging on the upside get managers fired. He cites Jeremy Grantham in the late 1990s as someone who took it.
- **Tactical asset allocation models**: his are price-based and momentum-driven, running since about 2005. He does not yet use valuation-driven models because reversion timing is hard. He calls that "a flaw in our system".

## Mental Models
- Treat a tight credit spread as a cheap out-of-the-money put on equities. In 2007 he reasoned that spreads would blow out only if equities fell hard.
- Compare every hedge on carry. In 2007 credit wideners, S&P puts and put spreads, and forward-starting volatility all had negative carry. Long government bonds paid.
- The volatility "has to flow into the investment account, not the budget". A budget cannot take 30 percent less cash.
- Ask why there are no very wealthy Roman families. Total destruction of capital is not unimaginable.

## Anti-patterns
- **Valuing illiquidity without its opportunity cost.** In 2008 few real money funds could buy assets that had fallen 70 percent.
- **"Valuation doesn't matter because my horizon is long"**: he calls it "irresponsible". With a 300-year horizon even a 1 percent premium works.
- **Hiding behind the efficient market hypothesis**: "simply just avoiding the hard work".
- **Diversification as the only risk tool.**
- **Tactical reweighting at CalPERS size**: slippage would be "enormous". Large funds need better construction and protection instead.

## Worked Examples (the author's numbers)

### Cheap insurance before the event
| When | Instrument | Cost or entry | Outcome he reports |
|---|---|---|---|
| Summer 2007 | Investment grade credit index (IG8) spread wideners | Spread 35 bp. Risk about 10 bp of further tightening | Spread reached almost 300 bp in 2008. Market "entirely liquid" at size |
| "Not too many years ago" | 10-year CDS on European sovereigns | 5 bp a year | Many moved above 80 bp in 2008 |
| October 2009 | Swedish receiver swaptions on 1-year rates, struck 1.8% | Spot 1-year 0.87%. 1y1y forward 2.52%. 2y1y forward 3.50% | Would rise six times in value if 1-year rates stay unchanged |
| October 2009 | 10-year S&P 500 calls struck at 10,000 (index near 1,000) | 14 bp total, 1.4 bp a year | Hyperinflation protection. Analogy: Turkey's ISE 100 went from 1 in 1986 to 55,000, flat in real terms since 1988 |
| October 2009 | Euro Stoxx 50 dividends for 2018 | 110 euros against about 115 for 2009 | Cheap inflation hedge if any inflation occurs. Worst case assumed: Japan-style deflation of about minus 40 bp a year |
| October 2009 | Japanese inflation-linked bonds | Imply 100 bp a year of deflation for 10 years | Japan had no such 10-year period since 1990 |

### Deploying cash in 2008
Ghanaian Eurobonds traded at 59 cents on the dollar. He saw no reason to doubt payment. He reports a 72 percent return on invested capital in about 10 months.

### Levered bond back test (Table 3.1, source Falcon)
- A 5 percent levered allocation to long US government bonds improved absolute return, return to worst drawdown, and worst drawdown for an Endowment-style portfolio with over 90 percent of risk in equity-like assets.
- On a 100 percent US equity portfolio, 1992 to 2009: adding 100 percent leverage in Treasuries raised annual return by almost 5 percent and cut the worst drawdown by 10 percent.
- Tests on 1940 to 1980 were "less conclusive".
- Half US and half Japanese bonds beat US bonds alone in 1992 to 1999, but not in 2000 to 2009.
- He concedes: "We very well might be fighting the last battle."

### His models in 2008
The long-only long-term model was down about 8 percent by fall 2008, switched heavily into US fixed income and broke even for the year. The short-term long-short model made money.

## Key Takeaways
1. Price cash by what it lets you buy in a crisis.
2. Raise cash-like holdings as valuations rise, and name the valuation yardstick.
3. Stress the frontier with correlations of one.
4. Look for insurance when spreads and option prices show complacency, "before the horse has left the barn".
5. Fix an absolute loss limit and cut to cash when it is hit.
6. In the short term he says equities and inflation are uncorrelated and equities may fall first. Over the long term they are correlated.

# Chapter 11: The Equity Trader (anonymous, runs a multi-manager equity fund of a few billion dollars)

Type: MIXED. The drawdown rules and the risk-bucket critique are METHOD. The 2008 trading, the missed subprime short and the Yahoo trade are CASE.

## Core Idea
Prices move "because people buy and sell". A fundamental data point matters only through how it makes holders change positions. Run many uncorrelated books under hard stop limits, and change the style of trading when the profit and loss says the current one has stopped working.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The second derivative of a data point**: getting the fundamental right is "only an input". The trading question is how the data "is going to cause people to change their positions and how aggressively".
  - Ways to read positioning: several upgrades before earnings mean high expectations. Compile "whisper numbers". Watch reaction to news: a stock that rises on bad news is probably heavily shorted with low expectations.
  - Check your difference against both the sell side and the buy side. Sell-side consensus can be stale, and the buy side may already be in.
- **Hard stop per decision maker**: each portfolio manager, including himself, has a stop loss of 7 percent, "at which point we shut down the book". Risk is cut on the way down so a gap does not blow through the stop. "If we lose enough, we take the portfolio to cash."
  - Layered below it: VaR limits, stress test limits, net exposure limits, concentration limits, liquidity limits.
- **Match research to holding period**: a Buffett-style process needs capital that can hold 5 to 15 years through any volatility. Without that capital, focus on catalysts over months or quarters. "As things get cheaper, people tend to want to buy more of them", fitting a long-term judgment onto a short-term mandate.
- **Define the job as profit, not as an exposure range**: managers who say "I keep an exposure between 20 and 80 percent long" trap themselves. "For an investor, blowing up once every 5 or 10 years is more than enough."
- **Regime decides method**:
  - Range-bound markets (2006, first half of 2007): fundamentals make the money and trading gets chopped up.
  - Crisis markets (fall 2008): correlations go to one and companies have no visibility. Fundamentals cannot separate two stocks. Trade price action and flow with holding periods of a few days.
- **Build positions by confirmation**: start small on initial research. Add as more research and price action confirm. The best trades grow five- or tenfold from the first size. "Waiting for over-confirmation on a trade is a sure way to miss a big part of the move."
- **Always hold a toehold**: keep a small short or a small put on a crowded theme so you keep watching it. When it turns, get aggressive and stay with the trend. Hold a core through the second and third legs.
- **Asymmetry targets**: risk 5 percent to make 10 or 15 percent, or risk 25 percent to make 50 to 100 percent. Scale out into strength, so winners are always sold "too soon".
- **Portfolio-relative bar**: with six long ideas in one sector, the bar for a seventh long is very high and the bar for a related short is low.
- **Bucket by risk, not by type** (his main point for real money): institutions start by deciding how much capital goes into the private equity bucket, the real estate bucket, the hedge fund bucket. They should start with "how much overall risk should be taken", which means "how much you can stand to lose if everything goes against you".
- **Pure alpha or efficient beta**: "You cannot have it both ways." Either take an absolute return approach whatever the market does, or buy cheap beta and accept the outcome. For a cheap asset class, buy an index or ETF with a predetermined stop. Do not pay hedge fund fees for it.

## Key Concepts
- **Trading the P&L**: cutting risk because the book is losing, whatever the thesis.
- **Bank shot trade**: using information on one instrument to trade another that it will affect. A strength, and a source of needless complexity.
- **Whisper numbers**: what analysts really expect beyond published estimates.
- **13-F filings**: the public holdings reports that showed hedge funds of every label owned the same stocks.
- **Disguised beta**: funds sold as absolute return that were highly correlated with stock markets.
- **Negative selection bias**: talented risk takers will not work for large bureaucratic institutions, so in-house hedge fund platforms hire the rest.

## Mental Models
- The Yahoo day: short into good news, the stock up 20, 30, 40, 50 points. He scaled out as others added. When it held at +50 he went long. It closed up 100. "Stocks are driven by supply and demand, and can go absolutely anywhere regardless of what you think the business might be worth."
- "Often a stock or sector will change character prior to a fundamental shift—especially at major turning points."
- Long-term stories "do not really change that much". The price path in between can ruin you. Crude went up several times, down 80 percent and back up 50 percent on the same story.
- Crowding means that when a break comes it is "much sharper, much faster, and much bigger than expected".
- Emotion scales with the loss relative to capital and to the usual daily swing, not with the cash amount.
- Investors amplify the manager's mood. Inflows during good times are "a state of mind that usually precedes a big drawdown".
- When a decision moves "more towards gambling than investing or trading", cut risk and react afterwards.

## Anti-patterns
- **Chasing momentum when frustrated**, with no method.
- **Skipping a clear asymmetric trade because it is outside your expertise**: the subprime short. The index fell from 99 to 5. Lesson: put it on small if the downside is known. "You learn a lot about a position just by having it on."
- **Covering crowded shorts too early**: his biggest 2008 mistake. Shorts down 30 to 50 percent in summer ended down 80 or 90 percent.
- **Betting ahead of a policy decision with unquantifiable risk.**
- **Betting against a crowded trend on valuation with no timing.**
- **Allocators who bucket managers by gross and net exposure ranges.** It stops the best managers from changing. His example is John Paulson, who began in risk arbitrage.
- **A book of levered buyouts, long-only equity, and "portable alpha" in long-biased funds**, all called diversified.
- **Complacency in easy periods**: counterparty risk and portfolio liquidity must be fixed "when things are good".

## Worked Example: the TARP vote, September 2008
1. Lehman had failed. The first TARP vote was due.
2. He could not judge the outcome. He expected a very large move either way.
3. He also did not know whether counterparties would exist in a month.
4. Action: the fund went "virtually flat" before the vote.
5. Rule he states: "We have had better luck reacting to policy changes than predicting them." Being small and nimble makes that possible.

## Worked Example: a 15 percent loss limit for a pension fund (his answer)
1. Hold things that benefit from risk-off markets. A global macro allocation has a better chance of making money in a down market than long equity or long credit. No guarantee.
2. Choose managers who have shown they change exposure and make money in down markets.
3. Use buying power to set up customized accounts. He would run a $5 billion long-term account with a 15 percent stop loss, and thinks most managers would.
4. If managers or rules do not allow that, buy long volatility regularly, or hold a sizable cash or government bond allocation.
5. Accept the cost: if global stocks rally 50 percent, the fund will not match it.

## Views stated in 2009 (dated)
- A liquidity-driven rally with little differentiation between stocks.
- Unsustainable trends: a growing government share of spending, very large on- and off-balance-sheet government debt, and ever larger "one time stimulus". If not reversed, "massive inflation or government defaults down the road".
- He blames the subprime failure partly on the political mandate behind Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. This is his opinion.
- The hedge fund industry will split into niche specialists and large institutional firms. The world splits "between pure alpha and efficient beta".

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask who holds the position and what will make them act.
2. Put a loss limit on every decision maker and enforce it.
3. Let the P&L tell you when the method does not fit the regime.
4. Hold a small position in themes you may want to press later.
5. Set total risk first. Fill buckets second.

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. The headline (ch01, ch06)
"Managing debt crises is all about spreading out the pain of the bad debts, and this can almost always be done well if one's debts are in one's own currency." The biggest risks are not the debts. They are policy makers who lack the knowledge or the authority to act, and the politics of adjustments that hurt some to help others.

### 2. Classify first: deflationary or inflationary (ch01, ch05)
- **Deflationary depression**: debt mostly domestic, in local currency. Rates hit about 0 percent. Defaults and austerity dominate until money is printed. No currency crisis.
- **Inflationary depression**: reliance on foreign capital and debt in a foreign currency that cannot be monetized. Capital leaves, the currency falls, inflation rises while credit contracts.
- Treat a gold peg as akin to foreign-currency debt (ch08).
- Basis: 48 cases with a real GDP fall of more than 3 percent, 21 deflationary and 27 inflationary.

### 3. The four levers (ch01, ch03)
Use these to bring debt and debt service down relative to income:
1. Austerity (deflationary)
2. Debt defaults/restructurings (deflationary)
3. The central bank "printing money" and buying assets or giving guarantees (inflationary)
4. Transfers from those who have more to those who have less

Policy makers typically start with too much of 1 and 2. "In the end, policy makers always print."

### 4. Seven stages of the deflationary cycle (ch02, ch03, ch04)
1) Early part ("Goldilocks") → 2) Bubble → 3) Top → 4) "Depression" → 5) "Beautiful deleveraging" → 6) "Pushing on a string" → 7) Normalization.
- Bubble: debt rises faster than income, typically 20 to 25 percent of GDP over about three years.
- Top: most often triggered by central bank tightening. The more leverage and the higher the prices, the less tightening it takes.
- Depression: rate cuts are exhausted. Equities typically fall around 50 percent.
- Normalization: 5 to 10 years for activity to regain its peak.

### 5. Bubble checklist (ch02)
Ask of each big market:
1. Are prices high relative to traditional measures?
2. Are prices discounting future rapid price appreciation?
3. Is there broad bullish sentiment?
4. Are purchases financed by high leverage?
5. Have buyers made extended forward purchases?
6. Have new buyers entered the market?
7. Does stimulative policy threaten to inflate it more, and tight policy to pop it?

No single metric predicts a crisis. Look at the debt-service ability of specific entities, not averages. The worst bubbles show up as debt-financed asset inflation, not CPI inflation.

### 6. Beautiful deleveraging (ch04)
A deleveraging is beautiful when stimulation offsets the deflationary forces and brings **nominal growth above the nominal interest rate**, without so much printing that there is a run on debt assets. Printing is not inflationary when it offsets the credit contraction: a dollar of spending paid for with money has the same price effect as one paid for with credit. Typical turn: money creation near 4 percent of GDP a year, a currency fall against gold of about 44 to 50 percent, deficits near 6 percent of GDP.

### 7. Monetary Policy 1, 2, 3 (ch04)
- **MP1**: interest rates. Broadest effect. Ends near 0 percent.
- **MP2**: QE. Works through investors and savers. Strongest when risk and liquidity premiums are wide, fades as they compress, widens the wealth gap.
- **MP3**: money put more directly in the hands of spenders, with incentives to spend. Runs from debt-financed fiscal spending backed by the central bank to "helicopter money" and debt write-downs with money creation. Best with fiscal and monetary coordination.

### 8. Solvency versus cash flow, and the rescue sequence (ch03, ch09)
A solvency problem needs equity capital or changed accounting rules. A cash-flow problem needs cash or guarantees. For systemically important institutions: guarantee liabilities, provide liquidity, support solvency, then recapitalize or nationalize. Err on the side of too much. Let non-systemic institutions fail in order.

### 9. Inflationary cycle and currency defense (ch05)
Vulnerability rises when a country: lacks a reserve currency, has low reserves, has large foreign debt, runs large and rising budget and/or current account deficits, has negative real rates, and has a history of inflation and negative currency returns.
- Defenses with reserves and rate rises rarely work. An expected 5 percent fall in a month needs about 80 percent annualized interest.
- Prefer a large, surprising, one-off devaluation to a level with a two-way market, then policy tight enough that holding the currency pays.
- Capital controls seldom work.

### 10. Spiral to hyperinflation (ch06, ch07)
It happens when policy makers do not close the gap between external income, external spending and debt service, and keep printing to fund it. Each round sends more money into real and foreign assets. Currency weakness leads inflation, which leads money growth. Exit: debt relief, a new hard-backed currency, limits on issuance, no monetization, a closed deficit, tight credit, rebuilt reserves.

### 11. "D" versus "R" and the stress test (ch09)
A "D" is a deleveraging, a different process from a recession. Find it early by pro forma cash-flow projections for key sectors and institutions, and by marking lenders' illiquid assets to liquid proxies. Leverage predicts future volatility better than VAR.

### 12. War and debt (ch06)
Internal conflict between rich and poor plus rivalry between comparable powers raises the probability of war. The worst outcome is heavy debt and a lost war.

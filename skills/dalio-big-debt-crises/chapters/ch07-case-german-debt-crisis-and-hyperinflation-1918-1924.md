# Chapter 7: Case Study: German Debt Crisis and Hyperinflation (1918–1924)

*Covers Part 2, first case study. Memory class: case (a historical episode, read against the inflationary template in Ch 5 and Ch 6).*

## Core Idea
Weimar Germany is the book's iconic inflationary depression. War debts in local currency were inflated away. Reparations were a hard-currency debt that could not be printed, defaulted on, taxed for or borrowed against, so policy makers relied only on printing. Capital flight drove the currency, the currency drove inflation, and inflation drove money printing. The hyperinflation ended only when the external burden was cut and a new, hard-backed, strictly limited currency replaced the mark.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Gold-peg dilemma**: when (a) money in circulation far exceeds the gold backing it at the conversion price and (b) investors rush to convert, the central bank must either shrink the money supply or end convertibility and print. "Central banks almost always choose suspending convertibility and printing more money", because it is less painful.
- **Monetization spiral in war finance**: debt monetization raises inflation, which lowers real interest rates, which discourages lending to the government, which forces more monetization.
- **Four levers for a balance of payments crisis**: to make an external payment a country must 1) spend less, 2) earn more, 3) finance it by borrowing and/or drawing on savings, or 4) default or get relief. The case shows each one blocked:
  - Spending cuts: about 50 percent of government revenue would go to reparations, and the rest funded essential social spending. Cuts were "politically impossible".
  - Taxes: the 1919 Erzberger reforms had already raised burdens. More tax would speed capital flight.
  - Savings and borrowing: Versailles had seized or frozen foreign holdings. Gold reserves did not cover the first interest payment. The Bank of England "politely refused" a 500 million gold mark loan.
  - Default: impossible under threat of invasion.
  - What was left: let the currency fall and print.
- **Causality rule for inflationary depressions**: "money printing was not the source of the currency weakness so much as currency weakness was the cause of money printing". In the 1922 chart described in the text, currency weakness leads inflation, which leads money supply growth.
- **Debtor/creditor brinkmanship**: debtors demand maximum relief, creditors try to recover as much as possible without making the debtor insolvent. J.P. Morgan, Jr.'s framing: the Allies had to choose between a weak Germany that could not pay and a strong Germany that could, and that would be strong militarily too.
- **The stabilization package.** The text announces "five crucial steps" and then details seven numbered measures:
  1. Restructuring the reparations debt.
  2. Creating a new currency with hard backing.
  3. Imposing limits on money printing.
  4. Ending monetization.
  5. Closing the deficit.
  6. Tightening credit.
  7. Accumulating foreign exchange reserves.
  - Why it works: each step makes the next credible. A new currency fails if it can be used to monetize debt. A central bank can only refuse to monetize if the government can pay its bills. None of it works while an unpayable external liability remains.
  - Failure mode shown earlier in the case: one-off measures (a short-lived peg, capital controls) did not hold.

## Key Concepts
- **Gold mark**: an artificial unit measuring the paper mark against gold. In 1914 one gold mark equaled one paper mark.
- **Hard currency debt**: foreign-currency or gold-denominated debt. It "cannot be printed away".
- **Floating debt**: the short-term government debt used to finance the deficit.
- **Hot money**: speculative investment that can be pulled at a moment's notice.
- **"Flight from the mark to the machine"**: firms pushing borrowed money into capital goods for their "intrinsic value", not their "use value".
- **Notgeld**: "emergency money" that large depositors, local governments, trade associations and companies printed.
- **Rentenmark**: the new currency of November 1923, pegged at one to one trillion paper marks and 4.2 to the dollar.
- **Reichsmark**: the gold-convertible currency of October 1924.
- **Dawes Plan**: the 1924 rescheduling that cut reparations service to about 1 percent of GNP and added a foreign exchange loan.
- **Debt revaluation**: forcing debtors to pay creditors more than face value. It tightens credit, supports the currency and lowers inflation.

## Mental Models
- **Borrowing in foreign currency is a bad sign.** Germany began doing so in the last two years of the war because lenders would not take promises in marks.
- **A buying boom can be flight, not prosperity.** Bare shelves, record auto sales, full order books and a tripling stock market in late 1921 were a rush out of money into "stuff".
- **Printing is the least bad option inside a spiral.** "If it were that easy, hyperinflations would almost never occur." Stopping the press would have stopped wages, factories, railways and government within weeks.
- **Inflation or deflation is a choice between creditors and debtors.** Keynes, as quoted: inflation is unjust and deflation is inexpedient, and deflation is perhaps worse because it provokes unemployment.
- **Capital inflows make a central bank's job easy, and their dependence makes the recovery fragile.**

## Anti-patterns
- **Relying exclusively on printing.** The author's judgment: German policy makers "made a mistake in not trying to achieve a better balance between deflationary forces and inflationary ones".
- **A payment schedule that rises with the debtor's recovery**: 70 billion gold marks of reparations were to start whenever the Allies judged Germany able. That unnerved investors.
- **Defending a peg with borrowed hard currency.** The 1923 dollar peg lasted six months, cost about 400 million gold marks, and left extra hard-currency debt.
- **Capital controls.** The October 1922 limits on buying foreign exchange did not stop the flight.
- **Symbolic rate rises.** The Reichsbank raised rates to 18 percent with inflation near 10,000 percent.
- **Buying the "cheap" currency of a debtor with an unpayable external burden**, as foreign speculators did in 1920 to 1921. Keynes called the inflow bubble "the greatest ever known".

## Worked Example

**The 1923 stabilization, step by step.**

1. *External relief first.* Ruhr industrialists began cooperating in September 1923. By mid-October the government ended its funding of passive resistance. The Dawes committee suspended reparations while it worked. In August 1924 the plan cut debt service by over 90 percent versus 1923.
2. *Informal currencies.* From late 1922 industry priced in foreign currencies. By fall 1923 nearly 2,000 types of emergency money circulated. The August 1923 dollar-indexed "Gold Loan" bills held their value and were hoarded.
3. *Rentenmark.* Announced 15 October 1923, in circulation 15 November. Because it exchanged against paper marks at a fixed rate, the hard backing covered the whole existing money stock. By December both currencies traded at par abroad.
4. *Why the backing was credible.* Flight from the mark had shrunk the real money stock so far that the monetary base in dollars equaled Germany's gold reserves by 1923. Little debt was denominated in the new currency.
5. *No monetization.* On 18 August 1923 the Reichsbank told the government it would discount no more government debt from 1924. The Rentenbank refused the government's December request for another 400 million.
6. *Budget.* Taxes were indexed to inflation by October. Lower inflation raised the real yield of existing taxes, which built confidence: a virtuous cycle.
7. *Credit.* On 7 April 1924 the Reichsbank capped total private credit. Inflation turned slightly negative in May 1924.
8. *Reserves.* Hoarded foreign currency flowed back, and the Dawes loan added a buffer against speculative attack. The reichsmark followed on 11 October 1924.

## Key Takeaways
1. War debts and wartime inflation did not cause the hyperinflation by themselves. Most combatants had similar wartime inflation. The undefaultable hard-currency burden did.
2. In this kind of crisis, follow capital flows and the currency first, then inflation, then the money supply.
3. A rally in local stocks or a consumer boom can signal flight from money.
4. Each round of printing sends more money out of the currency. Psychology shifts from "semi-managed" to "out of control".
5. News on debt negotiations can drive 10 to 20 percent currency swings.
6. Ending a hyperinflation needs debt relief, a new hard-backed currency, a cap on issuance, no monetization, a balanced budget, tight credit and reserves, together.
7. The text links the episode's trauma to the later politics of the 1930s.

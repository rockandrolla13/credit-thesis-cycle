# Chapter 10: Compendium of 48 Case Studies: Method, Glossary, and the 21 Primarily Domestic Currency Debt Crises

*Covers Part 3: the introduction "48 Debt Crises", the "Glossary of Key Economic Terms", and the first 21 case auto-summaries. Memory class: the template and glossary are method. The 21 rows are case (historical episodes).*

## Core Idea
Part 3 runs every one of the 48 cases through the same simplified lens. Each case has a computer-generated "auto-text" summary and a chart deck. The value is the common template: a bubble (or war) phase, a depression phase, and a reflation phase, each described by the same few statistics. The 21 domestic-currency cases are all labeled deflationary.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Case selection screen**: the list was generated by "systematically screening for periods of deleveraging across major countries over the last century", focusing on cases with a real GDP decline of more than 3 percent, then triangulating against the IMF and prominent academics.
  - Limits stated: it is a sample, not all debt crises. Post-WWII Germany, Italy and Japan were excluded for data inadequacy. Blank charts mean data could not readily be found.
- **The auto-summary template for a deflationary case.** Each summary fills the same slots:
  1. *Label*: "classic deflationary deleveraging cycle" (or "classic wartime deflationary") and the years covered.
  2. *Bubble phase*: what drove it (rising debt, equity returns, housing returns, growth), the pre-crisis peak of debt as a percent of GDP, whether debt was in domestic currency and domestically owned, growth, the peak GDP gap, annualized equity returns, and how much policy makers tightened (short rates in basis points).
  3. *Depression phase* ("ugly deleveraging"): years, the shock that triggered it, falls in GDP, stock prices and home prices, the rise in unemployment, pressure on financial institutions, and the change in debt as a percent of GDP with its drivers (falling real incomes, deflation, interest payments financed with new debt).
  4. *Reflation phase* ("beautiful"): the year it began, the peg break or devaluation against gold, the rise in M0 as a percent of GDP, where rates ended, average real FX, how many of "9 classic policy levers" were pulled for financial institutions and bad debts, nominal growth against sovereign long rates, the fall in unemployment and in debt as a percent of GDP with its drivers, years for real GDP to regain its peak, and years for equities to recover in USD.
  5. *Politics*: a closing line where a populist leader came to power.
  - When to use: as a schema for recording any new episode so it can be compared with the 48.
- **Wartime variant**: a "War Phase" replaces the bubble. Debts rise through war borrowing and "the economic stats are not reflective of typical economic linkages". Winners get "a brief postwar recession" and "a more orderly deleveraging".
- **The gauges**: two gauges head each chart deck. One measures bubble/depression conditions and one measures tightness/easiness of money and credit. "The difference from zero conveys the extent of the bubble while the crossing above/below zero represents the shifting into or out of the bubble." They are "meant to be rough measures".
- **Deleveraging attribution**: Bridgewater's breakdown of what moved debt as a percent of GDP. A dot shows the annualized change. Bars above zero raised the debt burden and bars below lowered it. Factors act on GDP (inflation, real growth) or on debt (borrowing to cover interest, other new borrowing). The method differs by country with data availability.
- **Chart deck sections**: Indebtedness, Monetary and Fiscal Policy, Economic Conditions, Markets, External Position, and for wartime cases Government and Military.

## Key Concepts
Definitions as given in the Part 3 glossary, shortened:
- **Balance of payments**: the balance of all transactions between a country/currency and the rest of the world.
- **Balance-of-payments crisis**: the country's entities lack buying power in world markets. It "has essentially run out of cash and credit".
- **Current account balance**: exports minus imports plus net income receipts. "Think of it as essentially being net income."
- **Depression**: a severe downturn in a debt crisis, classically when central banks are limited in their ability to ease.
- **GDP gap**: an imprecise measure of production against "potential". Positive means little slack.
- **Money 0 (M0)**: money printed in a currency, usually physical currency in circulation plus reserves at the central bank.
- **Real FX**: an imprecise measure of whether a currency is cheap or expensive against its own past, usually trade-weighted. Positive means more expensive.
- **Foreign FX returns**: the return to a foreign investor from the exchange rate change plus the interest differential.
- **Reflation**: easy, stimulative monetary policy that helps produce a recovery.
- **Yield curve**: the difference between short and long rates. Inverted means short rates are priced to fall.

## Mental Models
- **Simple lens, then detail.** The author sets these summaries against the "blizzard of details" in Part 2. Viewed simply, "the important things pop".
- **Read each case as three questions.** How big was the build-up and in whose currency? How deep was the ugly phase and did debt/GDP rise during it? When and how did nominal growth get above nominal rates?
- **Debt/GDP usually rises in the depression.** The summaries repeat the phrase "even though [the country] needed a deleveraging, its debt as a % GDP went up".

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating the auto-text as analysis**: the author calls it "a very simplified version of our algorithmic analysis".
- **Reading wartime statistics as normal ones.**
- **Treating the list as complete.**

## Worked Example

**The template filled in: United States 1926–1936.**

| Slot | Value in the auto-summary |
|---|---|
| Label | Classic deflationary deleveraging, 1926 to 1936 |
| Bubble | 1926 to 1929. Debt peaked at 125% of GDP, domestic currency, mostly domestically owned. Growth 3%. GDP gap peaked at 13%. Equities returned 31% a year. Short rates rose about 250 bps |
| Shock | The 1929 stock market crash |
| Depression | 1929 to 1933. GDP -26%. Stocks -84%. Home prices -24%. Unemployment +23%. Debt/GDP up 98% (26% annualized), from falling real incomes, deflation and interest financed with new debt |
| Reflation | From 1933. Peg to gold broken. M0 up 6% of GDP. Rates to 0%. Real FX averaged -5%. 8 of 9 levers pulled. Nominal growth averaged 8% against long rates falling to 3% |
| Result | Unemployment -14%. Debt/GDP down 70% (21% annualized), mostly from rising real incomes, less from inflation. Real GDP regained its peak in 7 years. Equities in USD recovered within 25 years |
| Politics | Set the stage for FDR, "whom many people consider a populist leader" |

**A wartime winner: United Kingdom 1941–1967.** Postwar recession 1943 to 1947, GDP -15%. Debt/GDP rose 102% with a peak fiscal deficit of 31% of GDP. Reflation from 1947: currency devalued 30% against gold, M0 fell by 8% of GDP, rates to 1%, inflation averaged 4%, nominal growth 7% against long rates of 2%. Debt/GDP fell 139% (7% annualized), "driven primarily by higher inflation".

## Key Takeaways
1. Record any new episode in the same slots so it compares with the 48.
2. The peak debt levels in the modern cases (roughly 200 to 440 percent of GDP in the table) are far above the 1920s cases. Depth of the fall does not track the debt level alone.
3. Reflation dates cluster where the peg broke or QE began: 1931 for the UK and Japan, 1933 for the US, 2009 for the US, UK, Germany and Austria.
4. Hungary, Ireland, Portugal, Spain, the Netherlands and Italy show later reflation dates (2013 to 2015) than the 2009 group, and mostly longer recoveries (6 to 9 years against 3 to 5).
5. In the UK 1941–1967 case, inflation and nominal growth above long rates did the deleveraging, not money creation.
6. The count of levers pulled is the book's rough index of how aggressively financial institutions and bad debts were handled.

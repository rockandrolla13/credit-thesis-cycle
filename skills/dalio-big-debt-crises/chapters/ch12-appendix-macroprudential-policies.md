# Chapter 12: Appendix: Macroprudential Policies

*Covers the Appendix at the end of Part 3. Memory class: method (tools and questions), illustrated with a dated history of US measures.*

## Core Idea
Macroprudential policies direct credit toward or away from particular areas through the central bank's regulatory powers. They exist because credit growth is uneven: one area can be in a bubble while another is starved. They let policy makers tighten a frothy sector without tightening the whole economy, or ease a starved one when rates are at zero.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Definition**: "macroprudential policies are tools for directing credit one way or another through the central bank's regulatory authorities". The classic countercyclical use: make it easier to buy a house in a housing bust (lower credit standards or down payments) and harder in a housing bubble.
- **When the typical US case occurred**:
  1. Interest rate changes were losing effectiveness: either stimulation was needed with rates at zero, or tightening was needed in one area but not for the whole economy.
  2. It was desirable to send credit to starved sectors and away from frothy assets or lending.
  3. Several types of policy were used at once.
  4. The Fed, Congress, the executive branch and regulators coordinated.
  5. Results were mixed, and the best cases involved experimentation and flexibility.
  6. The toolset changed as financial innovation made old tools easy to avoid.
- **Demand-side tools** (changing demand for credit):
  - Required loan-to-value ratios.
  - Required debt service-to-income ratios.
  - Requirements around loan maturities.
  - Margin requirements for buying financial assets.
  - The cost of loans through interest rate subsidies, tax policy or other regulations.
- **Supply-side tools** (changing supply of credit):
  - Capital/reserve requirements for certain types of lending.
  - The portfolio of assets financial institutions may hold.
  - Accounting rules on different assets.
  - Supervisory pressure for certain lending behaviors.
  - Interest/lending rate ceilings and other limitations.
- **The six questions for a macroprudential decision**:
  1. Where are bubbles emerging, and how confident are you that they are bubbles?
  2. Which sectors are starved for credit? Is differentiated credit policy needed?
  3. How comfortable are you picking winners and losers? What are the political costs?
  4. Which tools will you use, and will you target demand, supply or both?
  5. How will you measure effectiveness, and how will you handle second- and third-order consequences, which often come years later?
  6. How will you coordinate legislators, the executive branch and the central bank, and empower regulators?
- **The 1950s four tests for selective credit policy**:
  1. How effective is general monetary policy in balancing the provision of credit to the economy?
  2. How potentially destabilizing is credit growth in the specific sector?
  3. How important is credit to the growth of that sector?
  4. How effective would selective credit controls be to administer?

## Key Concepts
- **Margin requirement**: the collateral, often cash, an investor must provide to buy an investment on credit.
- **Regulation T / Regulation U**: margin requirements for brokers and for banks.
- **Regulation W**: tighter underwriting on consumer installment loans from 1941.
- **Regulation X**: 1950 loan-to-value and maturity caps on residential real estate loans, stricter as loan size grew.
- **Regulation Q**: ceilings on deposit interest rates, from 1933.
- **Credit Control Act (1969)**: let the Fed set terms for credit to targeted sectors. Not used until the Volcker era and terminated at the end of 1980.
- **"Direct pressure"**: supervisors pushing banks toward or away from kinds of lending.
- **"Mopping up" approach**: not pricking bubbles and dealing with the effects after they pop.
- **Mortgage warehouse lending**: interim bank loans that finance mortgage origination by non-bank lenders.
- **Rediscount ceiling**: a cap, in France until 1972, on how much an institution could borrow from the central bank, often varied by sector.

## Mental Models
- **Macroprudential policy looks like fiscal policy.** It benefits some at the expense of others, which is why it is "generally avoided" and why deleveragings make it useful.
- **QE creates pockets of froth.** In the Great Depression and World War II, central bankers used macroprudential measures on those pockets while keeping overall policy easy.
- **The 1990s to mid-2000s were the exception.** Macroprudential tools were "a bread-and-butter tool" for almost the whole modern period.
- **Every control invites a workaround.** Margin rules were bypassed with derivatives. Reserve requirements were bypassed with commercial paper, eurodollars, repos and large CDs. Rate ceilings pushed deposits into shadow banking.

## Anti-patterns
- **Tightening a deleveraging economy to stop a suspected bubble**: US tightening in 1935 to 1936, partly in response to stock gains, "ended up being too much". Stocks fell 60 percent between 1937 and 1938.
- **"Mopping up" only**: regulators did not aggressively slow frothy mortgage lending in 2006 and earlier.
- **Leaving a distortionary rule in place**: Regulation Q was created to help smaller banks, and by the 1950s it was driving deposits into shadow banking. It was phased out by 1986.
- **Deregulating to relieve a squeeze**: S&L deregulation in the early 1980s, partly to help them cope with tight money, contributed to the S&L crisis.
- **Statements without teeth**: the Fed's February 1929 statement against speculative lending was "largely ineffectual".
- **Inflexible caps**: interest rate caps that were not adapted produced negative second-order effects.

## Worked Example

**1954 to 1955: cooling housing without raising rates.**

- Situation: by late 1954 the housing market "looked increasingly frothy". The economy was just recovering from a recession, so the Fed did not want to tighten.
- Step 1: the FHA and VA raised down payment requirements and cut maximum maturities from 30 to 25 years.
- Step 2: field offices were told to survey local markets and restrain federal underwriting where housing surpluses existed.
- Step 3: the FHLBB asked Federal Home Loan banks to curb loan commitments to thrifts, with official restraints on S&L lending in September 1954.
- Step 4: the New York Fed moved to restrain mortgage warehouse lending, which had more than doubled between August 1954 and August 1955.
- Note in the text: these measures were tweaked and eventually reversed in later years.

Reading against the six questions: the bubble was identified in one sector (question 1), general policy was unsuitable (the first of the four tests), both demand tools (down payments, maturities) and supply tools (thrift and warehouse funding) were used (question 4), and several agencies acted together (question 6).

## Key Takeaways
1. Reach for targeted credit tools when the sector that needs restraint and the economy that needs support are different.
2. Combine demand-side and supply-side measures.
3. Give regulators the authority and flexibility to adjust quickly. The cases the text calls most successful (World War II, Volcker's time at the Fed) had both.
4. End tools that distort. Good cases dropped failed policies "often within a matter of months".
5. Expect the regulated activity to migrate to new instruments and institutions, and adapt or abandon the tool.
6. Second- and third-order effects can arrive years later, so plan how to measure them at the start.

# Chapter 1: How I Think about Credit and Debt, and the Template for the Archetypal Big Debt Cycle

*Covers the Introduction and the first three sections of Part 1: "How I Think about Credit and Debt", "The Template for the Archetypal Long-Term/Big Debt Cycle", and "Our Examination of the Cycle". Memory class: method.*

## Core Idea
Credit is buying power granted against a promise to repay (debt). A debt crisis is a mechanical result of debt and debt service rising faster than the incomes that service them. Whether a crisis becomes a disaster depends mostly on whether the debt is in a currency the policy maker controls and whether policy makers know how, and have the authority, to spread the losses over time.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Productive-credit test**: credit is good or bad according to what it produces and how the debt is serviced.
  - When to use: judging any credit boom, at the level of a borrower, a sector or a country.
  - How: ask whether the borrowed money generates enough income to service the debt. Count secondary and indirect benefits as well as direct ones. Too little credit has a cost too: foregone development.
- **The two conditions for spreading losses**: the downside of high debt depends on policy makers' willingness and ability to spread bad-debt losses.
  - How: check 1) whether the debt is denominated in the currency the policy makers control, and 2) whether they have influence over how creditors and debtors behave with each other.
- **The four levers** that bring debt and debt service down relative to income:
  1. Austerity (spending less). Deflationary.
  2. Debt defaults/restructurings. Deflationary.
  3. The central bank "printing money" and making purchases (or providing guarantees). Inflationary, stimulates growth.
  4. Transfers of money and credit from those who have more than they need to those who have less.
  - How: a "beautiful deleveraging" balances the deflationary levers against the inflationary one, so debt-to-income falls while activity and asset prices improve, and nominal income growth moves back above the nominal interest rate.
- **Short-term debt cycles add up to a long-term debt cycle**: each short-term (business) cycle high and low ends with a higher debt-to-income ratio than the one before, until interest-rate cuts can no longer continue (the rate hits about 0 percent). Then deleveraging begins.
- **Two types of big debt crisis**:
  - *Deflationary depression*: the unsustainable debt was mostly financed domestically in local currency. Rates hit about 0 percent, restructuring and austerity dominate without adequate stimulation, and debt burdens rise because incomes fall faster than debts. No currency or balance of payments problem.
  - *Inflationary depression*: the country relies on foreign capital and has much debt in a foreign currency that cannot be monetized. When inflows slow, credit contracts while the currency decline produces inflation. Harder to manage because the ability to spread the pain is limited.
- **Two masters of money**: money is a medium of exchange and a store hold of wealth, so it serves "proletariat-workers" (who earn it by selling time) and "capitalists-investors" (who hold wealth tied to its value). Government sets the rules. Deleveragings pit these groups against each other.

## Key Concepts
- **Credit**: the giving of buying power in exchange for a promise to pay it back.
- **Debt**: the promise to pay.
- **Depression (the book's definition)**: a case where real GDP fell by more than 3 percent in a large country.
- **Deleveraging**: the process of reducing debt burdens once claims on money are too large relative to the money available to meet them.
- **Beautiful deleveraging**: a fall in debt/income ratios with acceptable inflation and growth.
- **Debt burden**: debt and debt service as a percent of income.
- **Monetized**: bought with money printed by the central bank.
- **Year of Jubilee**: the Old Testament practice of wiping out debt once every 50 years, cited as an early form of restructuring.

## Mental Models
- **Monopoly with a bank.** Early in the game "property is king"; later "cash is king". Add a bank that lends and takes deposits, and let players trade on credit. Debt-financed spending grows to multiples of the money in existence. Later the debtors run short of cash, depositors withdraw, and both banks and debtors go broke unless someone intervenes.
- **Borrowing is borrowing from your future self.** Spending more than you earn now creates a future period in which you must spend less than you earn. That pattern is a cycle.
- **Cycles are logic, not astrology.** They are "logically-driven series of events that recur in patterns". The sequence and timing are not pre-destined.
- **Self-reinforcing both ways.** On the way up, lending supports spending, incomes and asset prices, which support more borrowing. At the limit, the same links run in reverse: one person's spending is another's income, so falling spending cuts incomes and creditworthiness.
- **Fixed-investment booms cannot last.** Building an office for $10 million a year adds $10 million a year of demand. When it is finished, that spending falls to $0. Economies driven by debt-financed real estate, fixed investment and infrastructure, typically emerging ones, swing hardest.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating debt as bad in itself**: fiscal conservatism that blocks borrowing for cost-effective things (education, infrastructure) is wrong for society as a whole.
- **Lending on the assumption that above-trend growth continues**: near the peak, loans are based on that expectation. Income eventually falls below the cost of the loans.
- **Borrowing to pay debt service**: "one classic warning sign that a bubble is coming is when an increasing amount of money is being borrowed to make debt service payments".
- **Reading only the aggregate debt chart**: it hides the differences between entities, and it omits liabilities such as pension and health care obligations, "which are much larger".

## Worked Example

**How costly is bad debt? Two calculations from the text.**

1. *The subway.* A policy maker builds a $1 billion subway with debt. Only half the expected revenue arrives, so the debt is written down by 50 percent. The right question is whether the subway is worth $500 million more than budgeted. Over a 25-year life that is about 2 percent more per year. On that basis the subway may still be far better than no subway.
2. *The whole economy.* In really bad cases, roughly 40 percent of a loan's value cannot be repaid. Those bad loans are about 20 percent of all loans, so losses are about 8 percent of total debt. Total debt is about 200 percent of GDP, so the shortfall is roughly 16 percent of GDP. If the loss is "socialized" and spread over 15 years, it costs about 1 percent of GDP per year, "which is tolerable". Not spread out, it is intolerable.

Conclusion the author draws: the risk of high debt depends mostly on the willingness and ability to spread the losses.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask what the credit produced before asking how much there is.
2. First diagnostic for any debt crisis: in whose currency is the debt? Own-currency crises can be managed well "in almost every case".
3. The biggest risks are a) policy makers failing to do the right things through lack of knowledge or authority and b) the political consequences of adjustments that hurt some to help others.
4. A well-managed crisis is still extremely costly to some people.
5. Deleveraging begins when rate cuts run out. Watch for the point where debt service costs exceed the amount that can be borrowed to finance spending.
6. Aggregate debt-to-income is a rough picture only. Vulnerability sits in specific entities.

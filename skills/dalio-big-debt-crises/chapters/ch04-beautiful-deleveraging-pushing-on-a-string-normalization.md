# Chapter 4: The "Beautiful Deleveraging", "Pushing on a String" and Normalization (Stages 5 to 7)

*Covers Part 1, stages 5) to 7) of the deflationary cycle, including the well managed/poorly managed summary and Monetary Policy 1, 2 and 3. Memory class: method.*

## Core Idea
A deleveraging turns "beautiful" when stimulation offsets the deflationary forces and lifts nominal income growth above the nominal interest rate, without so much printing that faith in the currency breaks. Late in the cycle, rates and QE lose power and policy has to put money more directly in the hands of spenders.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The beautiful deleveraging condition**: the four levers are moved in balance to produce positive growth, falling debt burdens and acceptable inflation.
  - How: the right amount of stimulus a) neutralizes what would otherwise be a deflationary credit-market collapse and b) gets nominal growth above the nominal interest rate by enough to relieve debt burdens, "but not by so much that it leads to a run on debt assets".
  - Why it works: spending is paid for with money or credit. When credit contracts, new money can replace it. "A dollar of spending paid for with money has the same effect on prices as a dollar of spending paid for with credit."
  - Failure mode: too much printing produces an "ugly inflationary deleveraging". The risk is highest where much debt is in foreign currency and held by foreigners, because it cannot be monetized or restructured as easily.
- **Two waves of money printing**: central banks first provide liquidity to stressed institutions, then conduct large-scale asset purchases to stimulate the economy broadly.
- **Monetary Policy 1 (MP1)**: interest-rate driven. Most effective, because it has the broadest impact. Works by a) a positive wealth effect, b) cheaper credit purchases, c) lower debt-service burdens. Stops working when short rates hit about 0 percent.
- **Monetary Policy 2 (MP2)**: "quantitative easing", printing money and buying financial assets, typically debt. Works through investors/savers, not borrowers/spenders.
  - When it works best: when risk and liquidity premiums are large, because it makes them fall.
  - Why it fades: once premiums are low and prices high, the pay for taking risk is too small to bid prices further. Cash starts to look appealing. It also widens the wealth gap.
- **Monetary Policy 3 (MP3)**: puts money more directly into the hands of spenders and gives them an incentive to spend it. The continuum, with the text's cases:
  1. More debt-financed fiscal spending, sometimes with QE buying most of the issuance (Japan in the 1930s, US in World War II, US and UK in the 2000s).
  2. Debt-financed fiscal spending where the Treasury is not on the hook, because the central bank prints to cover the payments (Germany in the 1930s) or lends to other entities for stimulus projects (China's development banks in 2008).
  3. Giving newly printed money straight to the government to spend: printing fiat (Imperial China, the American Revolution, the US Civil War, Germany in the 1930s, the UK in World War I) or debasing hard currency (Ancient Rome, Imperial China, 16th-century England).
  4. "Helicopter money": printing and direct transfers to households (US veterans' bonuses in the Great Depression, Imperial China).
  5. A big debt write-down with big money creation, the "year of Jubilee" (Ancient Rome, the Great Depression, Iceland).
  - Author's view: the most effective approaches coordinate fiscal and monetary policy, because both the providing and the spending of money then occur.

## Key Concepts
- **Beautiful deleveraging**: falling debt/income with positive growth and acceptable inflation.
- **Ugly inflationary deleveraging**: the result of abusing stimulants (Weimar in the 1920s, Argentina and Brazil in the 1980s).
- **Pushing on a string**: a 1930s phrase for stimulus that fails to raise spending and private credit growth.
- **"Gas in the tank"**: remaining capacity of MP1 and MP2.
- **Helicopter money**: directing money into the hands of spenders.
- **Macroprudential policies**: regulatory powers to change the cost and availability of credit for targeted parts of the system.
- **Lost decade**: the roughly 5 to 10 years real activity takes to regain its former peak.
- **Zombie banks**: the result of protecting overindebted entities that are not systemically important.

## Mental Models
- **Velocity did not fall. Credit was destroyed and money replaced it.** Fast money growth with contracting credit and activity is offset, not inflation.
- **"In the end, policy makers always print."** Austerity causes more pain than benefit, big restructurings wipe out too much wealth too fast, and transfers do not happen at sufficient size without revolutions.
- **Financial assets are claims on goods and services.** When claims exceed what can be delivered, they are devalued or restructured. Monetization "simply swaps one IOU (debt) for another (newly printed money)". The author calls the situation analogous to a Ponzi scheme.
- **Pegs delay the turn.** Gold-, commodity- or foreign-currency-pegged systems force tighter policy. Eventually the pain makes policy makers break the link and print.

## Anti-patterns
- **Stuttering monetization**: start-stop purchases, and purchases of cash-like instruments that produce no wealth effect.
- **Fiscal austerity that undermines central bank stimulus.**
- **Tightening too early**, or continuing to tighten well after the bubble has burst.
- **Protecting non-systemic overindebted entities.**
- **Printing so much that faith in the currency collapses.**
- **Helicopter money with no incentive to spend**: "typically less adequate" than money given with such incentives.

## Worked Example

**Why nominal growth must exceed the nominal interest rate.**

- Debt is 100, income is 100. Debt-to-income is 100 percent.
- The interest rate is 2 percent. With no repayment, debt is 102 after a year.
- Income grows 1 percent to 101.
- The burden rises from 100/100 to 102/101.
- So for the burden of existing debt not to rise, nominal income growth must be above the nominal interest rate, "and the higher the better", provided it does not produce unacceptable inflation or currency declines.

**Gold as the release valve.** In the Great Depression the dollar was tied to gold. Suspending convertibility, so the currency could be devalued and more money created, was key to the bottoms in stocks, commodities and the economy. In 2008 the same printing, purchases and guarantees needed no legal change of currency regime.

## Key Takeaways
1. Test for a beautiful deleveraging: is nominal growth above the nominal rate, with acceptable inflation, and are debt burdens falling?
2. Printing is not inflationary when its size and character offset the size and character of the credit contraction.
3. Every deleveraging studied eventually led to big waves of money creation, fiscal deficits and currency devaluations against gold, commodities and stocks.
4. Order of tools: MP1 until about 0 percent, then MP2 while risk premiums are wide, then MP3.
5. When MP1 and MP2 are spent and the wealth gap is large, direct stimulus at less wealthy people, who spend more of each increment.
6. Prolonged monetization risks a move to alternative stores of value such as gold.
7. The text places the US late in the long-term debt cycle at the time of writing, comparing it with 1937 to 1938.

# Chapter 8: Case Study: US Debt Crisis and Adjustment (1928–1937)

*Covers Part 2, second case study (the Great Depression). Memory class: case (a historical episode, read against the deflationary template in Ch 2 to Ch 4).*

## Core Idea
The Great Depression is the book's iconic deflationary deleveraging. A leveraged stock bubble, financed largely outside the banks, was popped by Fed tightening. For three years policy makers leaned on deflationary levers while the gold standard limited money printing. The turn came in March 1933, when Roosevelt broke the link to gold, printed, and guaranteed the banks. Premature tightening in 1936 to 1937 then caused a second downturn.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Gold standard as foreign-currency debt**: "Being on a gold standard is akin to having debts denominated in a foreign currency", because creditors could demand gold and policy makers could not print freely. The toolkit stays limited until the link is broken.
- **How central banks inadvertently finance bubbles**: in spring 1927 the Fed cut its discount rate from 4 to 3.5 percent, at the request of France, Germany and the UK, who were losing gold. It was focused on growth and inflation, not on debt used to buy financial assets.
  - Rule stated: "the worst debt bubbles are not accompanied by high and rising inflation, but by asset price inflation financed by debt growth".
- **Shadow-banking leverage test**: new, fast-growing lending markets where much levering up occurs are symptomatic of bubbles. In 1929 these were **call loans** (one-day margin funding that lenders could call at any time) and **investment trusts** (companies issuing shares to buy other shares, often levered, and bought on margin).
- **Global dollar squeeze**: when credit creation collapses in a reserve currency that foreign institutions lend widely, there is a squeeze in that currency. In 1931 falling US imports cut foreigners' dollar income at the same time. The text notes the same dynamic in 2008.
- **Balance-of-payments rate rule**: rate increases large enough to compensate holders of a weak currency for currency risk are too large for the domestic economy to bear, so they do not work. The Fed raised rates from 1.5 to 3.5 percent in October 1931 to hold gold, and the depression deepened.
- **The 1933 bank resolution method**: auditors went through every bank, largest and safest first. An undercapitalized bank was (a) recapitalized through RFC preferred shares, (b) merged with a healthier bank, or (c) closed. Systemically important banks were always supported. A sound bank reopened able to borrow from the Fed against any of its assets, with the Treasury covering Fed losses.
- **First tightening rule**: "It is typically the case that the first tightening does not hurt stocks and the economy." The August 1936 reserve increase did not. The March and May 1937 increases, with fiscal tightening, did.
- **Economic conflict to war ("The Path to War")**: the text reads the 1930s as Thucydides's Trap, "where rivalries between countries lead to wars in order to establish which country is more powerful", followed by peace under the winner's rules until a rival emerges. Its claim: the wars "really started about 10 years before" 1939 and 1941, "as economic conflicts that were at first limited progressively grew into World War II".
  - Sequence shown: depression and internal conflict between haves and have-nots → populist, autocratic, nationalistic and militaristic leaders given special powers → top-down command economies and military build-up → competition for resources and territory → trade war, sanctions, embargoes and asset freezes → shooting war.
  - This restates the conflict cycle in Ch 6. The section adds a dated chronology, not a new method.
- **Template mapping of the 1933 measures**: the text tags each policy with its lever: devaluing and printing money, restructuring debts, deposit insurance, increased regulation, fiscal stimulus.

## Key Concepts
- **New Era**: the 1920s belief in permanent prosperity and continuously rising stock prices.
- **Bankers' Pool**: the group of bankers who committed $125 million to buy shares on Black Thursday.
- **Direct versus indirect relief**: Hoover opposed direct federal relief and favored private-sector pledges, state and local aid, immigration limits and pressure on banks to lend.
- **Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC)**: created 23 January 1932 with $500 million of capital and up to $3 billion of borrowing, able to lend against wider collateral and to more entities than the Fed, but only against "good" collateral.
- **Section 13(3)**: the 1932 emergency lending power later central to the Fed's 2008 response.
- **Standstill agreement**: Hoover's 1931 requirement that banks keep short-term credit to Germany and Central Europe extended.
- **Gold clauses**: contract terms allowing payment in gold, banned on 5 June 1933, which "amounted to a big restructuring of debts".
- **Sterilization**: from 23 December 1936 the Treasury bought gold inflows from its cash account instead of with new money.
- **Tripartite Agreement**: the September 1936 US, British and French agreement to refrain from competitive devaluations.

## Mental Models
- **No specific shock is needed to burst a bubble.** Rising prices needed leveraged buying to keep accelerating. Speculators and lenders were at or near maximum positions, and tightening changed the economics.
- **Banks look healthiest at the top.** In June 1929 capital ratios were 17.2 percent against 14.9 percent before the 1920 to 1921 recession. Problems appear when deposits run and assets fall.
- **Small things look big close-up.** The June 1931 "moratorium rally" of 35 percent is barely visible on the chart of a decline of almost 90 percent.
- **Punishing the banks is a classic, misguided instinct.** Hoover: "This was a banker-made crisis… the bankers must shoulder the burden of the solution, not our taxpayers." The text says it can have disastrous consequences.
- **Never believe expedient promises.** Roosevelt said he would keep the gold standard. Policy makers' promises that conflict with fundamentals "should never be believed".
- **The depression ended "on a dime"** when the peg was broken, even though GDP took until 1936 to match 1929 and the recovery favored the rich.

## Anti-patterns
- **Macroprudential half-measures against a bubble**: the 1929 warning letter and tighter acceptance terms were "largely ineffective".
- **Assuming a 50 percent fall is the bottom**: investors in 1930 anchored on 1907 and 1920.
- **Tariffs and immigration bans in a downturn**: Smoot-Hawley drew retaliation, starting with Canada, and sped the collapse in trade.
- **Letting a systemic bank fail on a solvency argument**: the Bank of the United States (December 1930) and the Guardian group in Michigan (February 1933).
- **Balancing the budget in a depression**: the Revenue Act of 1932 raised taxes. The deficit still grew relative to GDP because the economy shrank faster. The text calls it "a rookie move".
- **Stopping stimulus on the first signs of recovery**: Fed bond purchases ended in July 1932.
- **Publishing the names of borrowers from the rescue fund**: it turned an RFC loan into an advertisement of trouble.
- **Borrowing abroad to defend a peg**: the Bank of England's loans in 1931 "effectively funded the flight out of sterling".
- **Capital controls** under balance of payments pressure: "classic but ineffective".

**The 1933 turn against the template.** Break the peg (print), guarantee bank liabilities, recapitalize or close banks, restructure debts (gold clauses, HOLC), then fiscal stimulus. Result: nominal growth above interest rates and inflation "acceptable rather than horrible".

## Key Takeaways
1. A peg to gold turns an own-currency debt crisis into something close to a foreign-currency one.
2. In a depression, treat each policy announcement as a rally trigger, and ask whether the total money and credit available has changed.
3. Safe assets change. Treasuries rallied in 1929 and 1930 and sold off with stocks in late 1931, when devaluation risk reached the dollar.
4. A devaluation by one large country pushes deflation onto those that hold their pegs. Sterling's fall began the worst eighteen months for the US.
5. Liquidity given freely ends runs. The March 1933 reopening brought deposits in.
6. Late in the cycle, tighten carefully. The 1936 to 1937 mix of reserve increases, sterilization and fiscal tightening cut stocks by nearly 60 percent.
7. Depression, inequality and populism moved together. The text ties them to the conflicts that led to war.

# Chapter 3: The "Depression" (Stage 4) and Managing It with the Four Levers

*Covers Part 1, stage 4) The "Depression", including "Managing Depressions", "Austerity", "'Printing Money' to Stop the Bleeding and Stimulate the Economy", "Debt Defaults/Restructurings" and "Redistributing Wealth". Memory class: method.*

## Core Idea
A depression is a recession in which interest rates cannot be cut enough, so the gap between the money available and the money owed cannot be closed in the usual way. Deflationary forces (defaults and austerity) then dominate until policy makers add enough money printing, guarantees and capital. How fast they get that mix right decides how long and how deep the depression is.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The formula for a depression**: in a normal recession, rate cuts 1) produce a positive wealth effect, 2) stimulate activity and 3) ease debt-service burdens. In a depression rates cannot be cut materially, because they are already near 0 percent, or because currency outflows put a higher floor under them through credit or currency risk.
- **Solvency problem versus cash-flow problem**:
  - *Solvency*: under accounting and regulatory rules the entity lacks equity capital. Fix by a) providing equity capital or b) changing the accounting/regulatory rules, "which hides the problem".
  - *Cash-flow*: the entity lacks cash, typically because its own lenders are running. It can happen with adequate capital if the equity is in illiquid assets. Fix by providing cash or guarantees.
  - When to use: diagnose first. Lack of cash flow is "the trigger and main issue of most debt crises".
- **The four levers in the depression phase**: 1) austerity, 2) debt defaults/restructurings, 3) debt monetization/money printing, 4) wealth transfers. The key is the mix, so that deflationary and depressive forces are balanced by inflationary and stimulative ones.
- **The four immediate actions for systemically important institutions**:
  1. *Curtail panic and guarantee liabilities.* Raise guarantees on deposits and debt issuance. Deposit freezes are generally undesirable because they intensify panic, but sometimes there is no other way.
  2. *Provide liquidity.* The central bank lends against a widening range of collateral and to a widening range of institutions.
  3. *Support solvency.* First push the private sector: mergers of failed banks with healthy ones, regulatory pressure to issue capital, accounting adjustments that buy time.
  4. *Recapitalize/nationalize/cover losses* when the above is not enough.
- **Best-managed restructuring, four conditions**: policy makers a) swiftly recognize the magnitude of the credit problems, b) do not save every expendable institution, c) create or restore credit pipes for creditworthy borrowers, and d) ensure acceptable growth and inflation while bad debts are worked out.
- **Bad-asset management: AMC or original lender**: bad assets are a) transferred to an asset-management company (about 40 percent of cases) or b) left on the original lender's balance sheet (about 60 percent). Disposal levers: a) restructuring such as extended terms, b) debt-for-equity swaps and asset seizures, c) direct sales, d) securitizations.
  - How an AMC helps: it frees banks to lend again and centralizes sales and restructurings. Buying assets above market price is also a way to transfer money to banks. AMCs are usually publicly owned with a target time frame (for example 10 years).
  - Failure mode: an AMC cannot work when legal, political or funding limits stop it recognizing and restructuring bad debts.

## Key Concepts
- **Ugly deleveraging**: the depression phase, where defaults and austerity are not balanced by debt monetization.
- **Run**: lenders or depositors withdrawing money from an institution.
- **Liquidity**: the ability to sell investments for money.
- **Moral hazard**: the belief that excesses will recur if lenders and borrowers do not bear the downside, which makes policy makers slow to help.
- **Systemically important institution**: one whose failure would threaten the ongoing operation of the financial system or the economy.
- **Debt monetization**: printing money to cover debts.
- **Bear-market rally**: temporary relief after early, too-small policy moves.
- **Hollowing out**: high earners leave an area after tax increases, cutting revenues, property values and services.

## Mental Models
- **Most of what people think is money is really credit.** Credit appears out of thin air in good times and disappears in bad times. A store's credit-card receivable is not somewhere else when the customer cannot pay. It is gone.
- **The sleep test.** If everyone woke with no memory, the economy would be in the same position, because debtors' obligations would still be too large relative to their cash inflow. The deleveraging dynamic "is not primarily psychological".
- **Think of key lenders as a shipping port.** You would not lose a port in a depression because the port went broke. Keep the plumbing running by nationalization, loans or capital.
- **Shared budget rule.** "If rich people share a budget with poor people and there is an economic downturn, there will be economic and political conflict." Populism of left and right emerges then.

## Anti-patterns
- **Austerity as the first response**: when spending is cut, incomes are cut too, so deep cuts barely move debt/income.
- **Raising taxes as deficits widen in the contraction**: the text calls both moves "big mistakes".
- **One-off policies that do not move the needle**: policy makers typically take "a couple of years and a lot of unnecessary economic pain" before acting decisively.
- **Believing depressions are psychological** and that coaxing investors back into risk restores the economy.
- **Relying on wealth and inheritance taxes**: they raise very little because wealth is illiquid, and forced sales undermine capital formation. Transfers rarely contribute meaningfully to the deleveraging unless there is a revolution.

## Worked Example

**Why a write-down is worse than it sounds (footnote 7).**

- You own $100 of assets funded with $50 of debt. Net worth is $50. Leverage is 2:1.
- Assets fall 30 percent to $70. Debt is still $50. Net worth is $20.
- Net worth fell 60 percent on a 30 percent asset decline. Leverage of 2:1 doubles the hit, 3:1 triples it.
- Banks are leveraged about 12:1 or 15:1, so the same write-down is devastating for them.

Link to the levers: a write-down must be large enough for the debtor to service the restructured loan, but the creditor's loss is multiplied by the creditor's leverage. That is why restructuring alone destroys demand.

**1980s versus 2008.** In the 1980s the crisis involved loans not traded daily, with less mark-to-market accounting, so banks were not as "insolvent" as in 2008. In 2008 banks needed capital injections and guarantees. Both were managed successfully, by different means.

## Key Takeaways
1. A depression needs "monetary stimulation of a new form", because rate cuts are exhausted.
2. Speed and size of the response are among the most important drivers of severity and length. Fast: the US after 2008. Slow: the 1930s and Japan's "lost decade".
3. Ignorance and lack of authority are bigger problems than the debts themselves.
4. Early policy moves produce rallies. They do not end the bear market until the fundamental imbalance is closed.
5. Accounting rules change how severe the same losses look.
6. Protect the systemically important, let the rest fail in order.
7. Debt burdens rise during the depression even as debts are written down, because incomes fall faster.

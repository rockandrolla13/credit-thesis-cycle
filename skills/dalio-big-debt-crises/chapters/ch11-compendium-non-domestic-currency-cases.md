# Chapter 11: Compendium of 48 Case Studies: the 27 Non-Domestic Currency Debt Crises

*Covers Part 3, "Non-Domestic Currency Debt Crises": 27 case auto-summaries from Germany 1918 to Russia 2014. Memory class: the template is method. The 27 rows are case (historical episodes).*

## Core Idea
The 27 cases with significant foreign-currency debt are labeled inflationary. The auto-text sorts them into three kinds: "transitory inflationary" (the currency is let go, inflation is temporary), "classic inflationary", and "classic hyperinflationary" (tightening was not enough and the currency had to be replaced). The deciding variable in the template is the policy response to adverse capital flows.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The key determinant**: "how policy makers respond to adverse capital flows: whether they let their currency go and allow a tightening of financial conditions to flow through (painful but typically necessary to resolve the crisis), or print money to make up for money leaving (which can be inflationary)".
- **The auto-summary template for an inflationary case**:
  1. *Label*: transitory inflationary, classic inflationary, classic hyperinflationary, or classic wartime hyperinflationary.
  2. *Bubble phase*: either a domestic bubble or the standard sentence that the country "didn't experience a broad-based bubble" but "was tied to other countries, economies, and financial markets that were experiencing bubble-like conditions". Then: debt as a percent of GDP, the share in foreign currencies as a percent of GDP, the current account deficit, and investment inflows as a percent of GDP.
  3. *Depression phase* ("balance of payments/currency crisis"): years, the shock, the fall in capital inflows, the rise in short rates, the fall in real FX, falls in GDP and stock prices, peak inflation, the reserve drawdown in the currency defense, and the rise in debt/GDP "in part because the currency fell" and in part from crisis borrowing (with the peak fiscal deficit).
  4. *Reflation phase*: whether the peg was abandoned, the length of the "ugly" phase, the improvement in the current account, levers pulled, IMF program, structural reforms, the fall in debt/GDP, where real FX bottomed, years for real GDP to regain its peak, and years for equities to recover in USD.
- **Inflation "risk factors" score**: each summary says whether the country had "most" or "around half" of the "classic 'risk factors' for bigger inflation spirals" and names the biggest. Across the summaries the biggest factor named is: a long history of poorly-controlled inflation (12 cases), low real short rates (5), high foreign-denominated debts (3), the fiscal deficit (2), the current account deficit (2), low central bank reserves (2).
  - When to use: as a pre-screen for whether a currency crisis is likely to stay transitory.
- **Hyperinflation exit**: the hyperinflationary summaries all end the same way. "Stopping the inflationary spiral ultimately required [the country] to make more significant structural changes", meaning a new or re-pegged currency.

## Key Concepts
- **Transitory inflationary deleveraging**: "a classic example of a country 'letting its currency go' in the face of external pressures, which produces some temporary inflation but ultimately gives policy makers more flexibility to set interest rates".
- **Balance of payments/currency crisis**: the template's name for the depression phase in these cases.
- **Currency defense**: spending reserves before abandoning the peg. Each summary gives the reserve drawdown.
- **"Ugly" phase length**: described relative to average ("relatively short", "slightly longer than average").
- **Real FX bottom**: how cheap the currency became in the adjustment, which "set up the country for renewed competitiveness".
- **Structural reforms**: several summaries cite reforms "designed to increase labor market flexibility".
- **Contagion shock**: many shocks are named as "ripples" from another crisis (the 1980s Latin American Debt Crisis, the 1997 Asian financial crisis, falling oil prices).

## Mental Models
- **A country need not have its own bubble.** Being tied to others' bubble conditions while depending on foreign funding is enough.
- **Let it go early or replace it later.** Transitory cases abandoned pegs and let tightening flow through. Hyperinflation cases ended with new currencies.
- **Debt/GDP falls afterwards "mostly from nominal income rising".** In the hyperinflation cases the driver is stated as "higher inflation".

## Anti-patterns
- **Printing to replace departing capital** where the risk factors are present.
- **A long reserve-funded defense**: reserve drawdowns before the peg went were large (for example 70 percent in Brazil 1977–1987 and 55 percent in Russia 1996–2006).
- **Reading "transitory" as painless**: stock prices fell 46 to 96 percent in these cases.

## Worked Example

The summary for Russia stresses a deep, fast real depreciation and a large current account swing. That matches the Chapter 5 rule that a large, quick devaluation shortens the adjustment. The comparison is drawn here from the two summaries. The book does not pair these two cases.

## Key Takeaways
1. For a foreign-currency debtor, record FX debt as a percent of GDP, the current account, inflows and reserves before the crisis. Those are the template's vulnerability slots.
2. Score the inflation risk factors. A long history of poorly controlled inflation is the one most often named as biggest.
3. Transitory cases in the table regained their real GDP peak in 1.7 to 8 years where a figure is given. The fastest (1.7 to 2 years: Korea, Malaysia 1981 and 1994, Russia 1996) abandoned pegs and let the currency go.
4. An IMF program is mentioned in 21 of the 27 summaries.
5. Hyperinflation cases share low lever counts (2 to 5 of 9) and all end with a currency change.
6. Stock price falls in this group run from 33 to 97 percent, and most exceed 50 percent.

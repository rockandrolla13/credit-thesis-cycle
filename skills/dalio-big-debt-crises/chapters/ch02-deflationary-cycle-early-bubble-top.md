# Chapter 2: The Classic Deflationary Debt Cycle, Phases 1 to 3 (Early Part, Bubble, Top)

*Covers Part 1, "The Phases of the Classic Deflationary Debt Cycle", stages 1) to 3). Memory class: method.*

## Core Idea
A long-term debt cycle runs through seven stages. The first three build the problem: a healthy early phase, a self-reinforcing bubble in which debt outruns income, and a top that is usually pricked by central bank tightening. Bubbles can be measured, and the size of the bust depends on leverage, prices and each sector's sensitivity to tightening, not on economy-wide averages.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The seven stages of the archetypal long-term debt cycle**: 1) the early part of the cycle, 2) the bubble, 3) the top, 4) the "depression", 5) the "beautiful deleveraging", 6) "pushing on a string", 7) normalization.
  - How: track total debt as a percent of GDP and debt service as a percent of GDP. The archetype chart runs from month -60 to month +84 around the top (recovered from EPUB image 00002.jpeg). On that chart the stage boundaries sit near months -48 (early part ends), -12 (bubble ends), 0 (top ends), +36 (depression ends) and about +80 (beautiful deleveraging ends). Debt service peaks shortly after month 0. Total debt/GDP keeps rising to about month +40 because incomes fall.
- **The three lending criteria that rise together**: lenders size loans on the borrower's 1) projected income/cash flows, 2) net worth/collateral, and 3) the lender's own capacity to lend. In a bubble all three rise together, which makes the process self-reinforcing.
- **The bubble checklist ("Spotting Bubbles")**: the most defining measurable characteristics of a bubble.
  1. Prices are high relative to traditional measures.
  2. Prices are discounting future rapid price appreciation from these high levels.
  3. There is broad bullish sentiment.
  4. Purchases are being financed by high leverage.
  5. Buyers have made exceptionally extended forward purchases (built inventory, contracted for supplies) to speculate or to protect against future price gains.
  6. New buyers (those not previously in the market) have entered.
  7. Stimulative monetary policy threatens to inflate the bubble even more (and tight policy to cause its popping).
  - When to use: scan all the big markets, find which are in bubbles, then map what is connected to them and would be hit when they pop.
  - Why it works: bubbles "are much more similar than they are different". Failure mode: the author states he will not explain exactly how his systematic measures work, so the checklist gives the questions, not the thresholds.
- **Four asset-liability mismatches that build in a bubble**: a) borrowing short to lend long, b) liquid liabilities funding illiquid assets, c) borrowed money in riskier debt or assets, d) borrowing in one currency and lending in another. All are done to pick up a perceived spread.
- **Sector cascade over averages**: to size the downturn, the magnitude of the tightening matters less than each sector's sensitivity to tightening and how losses will cascade.

## Key Concepts
- **"Goldilocks" period**: the early part of the cycle. Debt growth is strong but not faster than income, because it finances activity that produces fast income growth.
- **Up-wave**: the multi-decade period in which promises to deliver money rise relative to the money supply and to debtors' cash inflows.
- **Present value effect**: lower interest rates raise asset prices, which is how progressive rate cuts sustain the long cycle.
- **Shadow banking system**: new, largely unregulated non-bank lenders that develop in a bubble, typically outside government protections.
- **Wealth effect (negative)**: wealth declines lead to less lending and spending, through worry and through lower collateral (footnote 6).
- **Extrapolation**: the consensus is priced in and believed, and people weigh recent experience more heavily than is appropriate.

## Mental Models
- **Bubbles start as over-extrapolations of justified bull markets.** The first leg is justified: lower rates lift assets, the economy and profits improve. The error is extending it.
- **The $50,000 earner.** Someone earning $50,000 with capacity to borrow $10,000 a year can spend $60,000 for some years. Because borrowing only pulls spending forward, that person later has to cut spending to $40,000 for as many years as they spent $60,000, all else equal.
- **Tops form when things cannot get better, yet everyone believes they will.**
- **Think of the central bank as the bubble's financier.** It controls money and credit. If it targets only inflation and growth, the debt it enables can finance bubbles whenever inflation and real growth look acceptable.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using one metric to call a debt crisis**: economy-wide debt-to-income, or even debt service to income (which is better), is "useful but ultimately inadequate". A high average is less dangerous when evenly spread than when concentrated in key entities.
- **Central banks claiming bubbles are not their job**: the author notes the debt-service perspective on investment assets is "nowhere to be found in the Taylor Rule" (footnote 4).
- **Buying the first dip after a bubble bursts**: when prices fall before earnings do, stocks look cheap against past and expected earnings. That misses the earnings decline still to come.
- **Reading mixed data at the top as health**: early in the top some parts of the credit system suffer while others stay strong, so the weakening is not obvious.

## Worked Example

**Sequence at the top, as the text walks it.**

1. The market is fully long, leveraged and overpriced.
2. The trigger is most often the central bank tightening as growth and inflation rise and capacity pinches. For a country that borrows abroad, the trigger can be external: tightening in the currency the debt is in, or a rise in that currency against the currency incomes are in.
3. Rising short rates narrow or remove the spread to long rates. A flat or inverted yield curve lowers the incentive to lend and raises the incentive to hold cash.
4. About six months before the economy peaks, the riskiest debtors start to miss payments. Credit spreads tick up and risky lending slows.
5. Higher short rates raise the discount rate on future cash flows, which weakens riskier assets, and make credit purchases more expensive.
6. Wealth falls first, incomes later. Creditworthiness worsens, lending contracts, earnings disappoint, and selling feeds on itself.

Stated rule: "The more leverage that exists and the higher the prices, the less tightening it takes to prick the bubble and the bigger the bust that follows."

## Key Takeaways
1. Run the seven-question checklist per market, not per economy.
2. The clearest unsustainability tell: interest payments covered by new borrowing instead of income growth.
3. Expect monetary policy to feed the bubble when inflation and growth both look good. That pattern held in Japan in the late 1980s and in much of the world in the late 1920s and mid-2000s.
4. In the immediate post-bubble period the wealth effect of asset prices moves growth more than monetary policy does. People underestimate it.
5. Size the bust by sector sensitivity and loss cascade.
6. A flat or inverted curve late in a leveraged boom is a signal to expect a move to cash.

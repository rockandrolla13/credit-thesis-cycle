# Chapter 6: The Spiral to Hyperinflation, War Economies, and the Part 1 Summary

*Covers Part 1, "The Spiral from a More Transitory Inflationary Depression to Hyperinflation", "War Economies" and "In Summary". Memory class: method.*

## Core Idea
An inflationary depression becomes a hyperinflation when policy makers do not close the gap between external income, external spending and debt service, and keep funding it by printing. Each round of printing works less well, because money moves into real and foreign assets instead of spending. War debts are a special case: the worst outcome is to borrow heavily and lose.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The defining condition of a hyperinflation spiral**: policy makers "don't close the imbalance between external income, external spending, and debt service, and keep funding external spending over sustained periods of time by printing lots of money".
  - Involuntary version: an external burden that cannot be defaulted on (Weimar reparations).
  - Voluntary version: printing to prop up growth instead of bringing spending in line with income, repeated over years at large scale.
- **The spiral mechanism**, in the order the text gives it:
  1. Currency declines trigger capital flight, which sets off a loop of depreciation, inflation and printing.
  2. With each round, more printed money goes to real or foreign assets instead of goods and services.
  3. Inflation reaches wages: a **wage-cost spiral**, sometimes mechanical through wage indexing.
  4. Savers who were burned short cash sooner and buy foreign and physical assets.
  5. Depositors and lenders shorten maturities. "During inflationary deleveragings, average debt maturities always fall."
  6. Shorting cash is cheap because real rates are low. Banks cannot meet the demand for cash.
  7. The central bank chooses between extreme illiquidity and printing faster. It prints, and that liquidity funds more flight into foreign assets and inflation hedges.
  8. Foreign-currency debt burdens rise with each decline, and lenders demand higher rates.
  9. Tax rises on income and wealth produce evasion and more capital flight.
  10. Money stops working as a store of value, then as a unit of account, then as a medium of exchange. Producers demand foreign currency or barter. This illiquidity "can't be relieved by money printing".
- **Investing during a hyperinflation**: "get short the currency, do whatever you can to get your money out of the country, buy commodities, and invest in commodity industries (like gold, coal, and metals)".
  - Equities are "a mixed bag". As inflation becomes hyperinflation, share prices diverge from the exchange rate. Shares rise in local currency and are still "a disaster". Bonds are wiped out. Gold becomes the preferred asset.
- **How it ends**: the currency never regains its status as a store hold of wealth. The classic path is "creating a new currency with very hard backing while phasing out the old currency".
- **The economic/geopolitical conflict cycle**: when 1) within countries, conflict between rich and poor brings populist, autocratic, nationalistic and militaristic leaders to power, and 2) between countries, conflicts arise among comparably strong powers, economics and politics intertwine and the probability of war is much higher than normal.
- **Two types of relationship between powers**:
  - *Cooperative-competitive* (win-win): tough negotiation with respect, like "two friendly merchants in a bazaar".
  - *Mutually threatening* (lose-lose): the parties exchange painful acts to force the other to give in.
  - Rule: either side can force the second path, and it takes both to follow the first. In the second, power is set by the ability to endure pain as much as to inflict it.
- **War finance priorities**: during the war, keep access to the financial and non-financial resources the effort needs. No country can fund a war and tolerable non-war spending from current income, so it needs borrowing capacity or very large reserves. Borrowing capacity depends on creditworthiness and the soundness of the local-currency debt market.

## Key Concepts
- **Hyperinflation**: goods and services prices more than doubling every year or worse, with extreme losses of wealth and severe hardship.
- **Transitory balance of payments crisis**: an inflationary depression from which policy makers engineer a recovery.
- **Inflationary psychology**: the shift in saver and investor behavior once currency declines persist.
- **Wage indexing**: contracts that raise wages with inflation.
- **State of siege**: the Weimar government's grant of greater domestic powers to military authorities in response to disorder.
- **War economy**: an economy whose production, consumption and accounting differ from peacetime, so its statistics need "a whole different orientation".
- **Paying back period**: the postwar period in which the debts and the outcome of the war drive markets.

## Mental Models
- **Stopping printing is not easy.** With capital flowing out, stopping causes extreme illiquidity and a deep contraction. By late October 1923 Germany's entire 1913 stock of money "would have just about gotten you a one-kilo loaf of rye bread". Stopping would have halted commerce.
- **Devaluation stops stimulating.** Early currency declines lift growth. Later ones do not, because stocks no longer give a wealth effect and money leaks abroad.
- **Stores of value replace investment.** People buy gold, factories, imports, "even rocks in the case of the Weimar Republic", and machinery as stores of value, not because they are needed. Much of that spending does not add to GDP.
- **Debt crises fade, productivity persists.** Over three to ten years a big debt crisis can be devastating. In the long run it matters less than productivity growth. The political consequences can matter more than the crisis itself.
- **Checks and balances cut both ways.** They protect against concentrated power and can slow decisions and let narrow interests block necessary moves in a crisis.

## Anti-patterns
- **Printing to fund external spending for years** instead of closing the external gap.
- **Reading local-currency equity gains as wealth** in a hyperinflation.
- **Getting into a lot of debt and losing a war.** The text puts it in capitals: "ABOVE ALL ELSE, DON'T DO THAT."
- **Reading war-period statistics as if they were peacetime ones**: GDP from armaments that get destroyed, unemployment lowered by military service.
- **Expecting rules to anticipate every crisis**: unforeseen circumstances must be met "often in hours" inside a system without crystal-clear rules.

## Worked Example

**One turn of the spiral, traced from the text.**

- Start: the currency has been falling and the central bank has printed before. The last round helped, so it is remembered as beneficial.
- Savers move deposits to short-term accounts and shorten lending. Banks face cash demands they cannot meet. Businesses cannot fulfill contracts.
- The central bank prints to support banks and often lends directly to businesses.
- Interest rates do not compensate for expected depreciation. Borrowers use the new liquidity to buy foreign assets, gold and real assets.
- The currency falls again. Import prices rise. Indexed wages rise. Producers raise prices.
- Foreign-currency debt costs more in local terms. The government raises taxes. The wealthy evade and move capital out.
- Result: more inflation and depreciation, and less growth per unit of printing than the round before.

The loop ends only with loss of faith in the currency and its replacement.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask whether the external gap is being closed or printed over. That separates a transitory balance of payments crisis from a hyperinflation path.
2. Falling average debt maturity is a marker of an inflationary deleveraging.
3. In a hyperinflation hold gold, commodities and foreign assets. Avoid bonds and do not trust local equity gains.
4. An undefaultable external burden makes big inflation "all but destined".
5. For war periods, judge a country on borrowing capacity, reserves and access to critical resources. Afterwards, judge it on its debts and on whether it won.
6. Internal wealth conflict plus external rivalry between comparable powers raises the probability of war.
7. Statistics for World War II losers (Germany, Japan) are missing from the book because they were unreliable or unavailable.

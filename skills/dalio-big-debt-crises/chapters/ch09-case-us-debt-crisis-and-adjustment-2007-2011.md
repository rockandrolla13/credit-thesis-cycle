# Chapter 9: Case Study: US Debt Crisis and Adjustment (2007–2011)

*Covers Part 2, third case study (the 2008 financial crisis). Memory class: case (a historical episode, read against the deflationary template in Ch 2 to Ch 4).*

## Core Idea
The 2008 crisis followed the deflationary template stage by stage: a housing debt bubble financed through shadow banking, a top made by Fed tightening, a depression once rates neared 0 percent, then a fast turn to a beautiful deleveraging through guarantees, capital and money printing. The text's judgment: the 1930 to 1933 depression lasted so long "because policy makers were so slow to react—not because their problems were worse, because they weren't". The main obstacle in 2008 was missing legal authority, and the crisis "would have been a lot less painful if the policy makers had acted even earlier".

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Pro forma financial stress test**: debt bubbles "are often hidden beneath the averages".
  - How: for each significant sector and institution, project whether it can obtain the new lending needed to a) roll over maturing debt and b) keep increasing borrowing to sustain what it is doing. If not, a debt crisis follows.
  - For lenders: take balance sheet data and apply liquid market prices as proxies for illiquid holdings, to estimate what they will have to report "long before they would have to report it".
  - Checks of the method: the Fed's stress test of 7 May 2009 was "nearly the same" as Bridgewater's. The main difference was horizon: the Fed counted losses over the next two years, Bridgewater over the life of the assets. The same projection method flagged Europe in February 2010.
- **The "Depression gauge"**: "a simple algorithm based on the proximity of interest rates to 0 percent, a few measures of debt vulnerability, and indications of the beginning of debt deleveraging". When triggered it changes the overall portfolio and risk controls, including counterparty risk. No thresholds are given.
- **"R" versus "D"**: an "R" is a contraction in real GDP brought on by tight central bank policy that ends when the central bank eases. A "D" results from a financial deleveraging: asset sales cut prices and equity, force more sales, contract credit and activity, and worsen cash flows. "The financial deleveraging causes a financial crisis that causes an economic crisis."
- **Five components of shadow-bank leverage**: 1) repo agreements and commercial paper, 2) large institutional depositors outside the protected banking system, 3) money market funds, 4) globalization of dollar lending, 5) securitization.
  - Counterfactual stated: had the boom been financed mostly by banks, the crisis would have been "dramatically easier to manage", because deposit insurance, the discount window and FDIC guarantee powers would have reached it.
- **Three asset/liability mismatches**: borrowing short and lending long, lending to risky borrowers for the credit spread, and borrowing in one currency while lending in another.
- **Leverage beats VAR**: Value at Risk sizes positions from recent volatility. An investor who never wants to lose more than 20 percent, seeing a worst month of 5 percent, levers about three times. Low volatility invites leverage, and rising volatility forces selling. "Leverage is a much better indicator of future volatility than VAR." Some leverage ratios were nearing 100:1.
- **Moral hazard rule in a crisis**: keep the systemically important parts alive first. "It is far better to err on the side of providing too much than to provide too little." Reason given: if everything is allowed to burn, the government ends up taking all the risk through a costlier nationalization.
- **Solvency needs equity, not loans**: the Fed could lend to Lehman against collateral "secured to their satisfaction". It could not supply equity. "Loans were not equity." Without a private buyer, no authority existed to save a failing investment bank.
- **Guarantee, not backstop**: Paulson's reading of why the money market fund guarantee turned markets at once: a stated guarantee reassures more than a backstop.
- **Capital levers further than purchases**: $700 billion of asset purchases was too small. Put into banks as capital, the same money supports "many times" that amount because banks lever it.
- **Stigma-free recapitalization**: offer preferred stock on attractive terms to all banks and make the nine largest take it. Capital went into 700 banks quickly.
- **Flow, not stock, of QE**: "merely slowing or ending quantitative easing was equivalent to tightening". The flow of purchases sustained asset prices and lending.
- **Reflation without general inflation**: "inflation" is an average. In a depressed economy reflation produces little inflation in labor costs and productive assets, and inflation in assets that gain from a cheaper currency (traded commodities, gold).
- **Tops and bottoms rule**: tops are made when the rate of buying is unsustainable, bottoms when the rate of selling is unsustainable, typically when most people are bearish.
- **Price identity**: price equals spending divided by quantity sold (P = $/Q). In a squeeze, prices move on who is forced in or out of positions, not on news.

## Key Concepts
- **Securitization machine**: mortgages pooled (for example 1,000 loans) and sliced: 70 to 80 percent AAA, 10 to 15 percent AA, 5 to 10 percent BBB, plus an unrated "first-loss" piece that banks typically kept.
- **Short-term wholesale funding**: funding that behaves like an uninsured deposit.
- **Early payment defaults**: buy-back clauses that sank subprime lenders when borrowers missed first payments.
- **Breaking the buck**: a money market fund's value falling below the amount deposited.
- **GSEs**: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. An implicit government guarantee made their paper trade near Treasuries.
- **Conservatorship**: the form of the GSE takeover, paired with guaranteed preferred stock purchases.
- **TARP**: the $700 billion Troubled Asset Relief Program, proposed for asset purchases and used for capital.
- **TSLF, PDCF, CPFF, TALF, PPIP**: facilities that lent against, bought, or financed progressively riskier assets.
- **TLGP**: the FDIC's Temporary Liquidity Guarantee Program, "a guarantee of nearly all bank debt".
- **Section 13(3)**: the Federal Reserve Act power to lend beyond depository institutions.
- **"Avalanche" stage**: a problem in one corner building in self-reinforcing ways into much bigger ones.
- **"Sweet spot"**: the first two years of a recovery, with slack and low inflation pressure.

## Mental Models
- **Debt for consumption is a red flag**, "since consumption doesn't produce an income, while investment might".
- **The worst bubbles show up in asset prices, not the CPI.** The Fed, "looking at the average numbers, remained unconcerned".
- **Boring years sow instability.** "One should bet on the opposite of what happened lately."
- **Data-mining history is not logic.** People levered up on a bet that had "never happened before".
- **Guarantees manufacture safe-looking assets.** Politics creates implicit or explicit guarantees, investors lever up in them, and bad debt grows. The bigger the GSEs grew, the more certain a rescue became.
- **A debt is a short cash position.** Dollar debt built up abroad produced a dollar squeeze, and the dollar rose.
- **QE is adrenalin for a heart attack.** Rates and M0 hit these extremes only twice since 1900, and both times markets and the economy bottomed right after the printing.
- **Emergency-ward rule.** Arguing over who pays is "like doctors in the emergency ward arguing over who will pay the bill". Save the patient first.
- **Battlefield medicine** (attributed to Larry Summers): it is never perfect and mistakes will be found.
- **Policy makers are reactive.** They act when "there is a broad consensus that there is an intolerable problem", and political systems decide "literally just hours before disaster".
- **Know when not to bet.** In the downtrend every rally looked like a bottom. In April 2009 pricing already discounted terrible conditions and the range of outcomes was enormous, so Bridgewater took almost no bets.
- **Regulation tightens after each crisis and is flouted about 25 years later.**

## Anti-patterns
- **Using economy-wide rates to fix sector debt problems**: "very inefficient at best". Macroprudential tools would have fit in 2007.
- **Shifting to inflation worries mid-crisis**: the Fed held rates on 25 June and 16 September 2008. Bernanke later called the September decision "certainly a mistake".
- **Letting a systemic firm fail for want of authority**: Lehman.
- **Relying on a legislature in the emergency**: the first TARP vote failed. The text says regulators need broad emergency authorities.
- **Asset purchases at market prices as the main remedy**: they change no one's condition materially. At a premium they are a subsidy and buy less.
- **Punishing bankers so hard they stop lending or leave.**
- **Treating the end of stimulus as neutral**: in early 2010 markets priced two or three Fed hikes with unemployment near post-war highs.
- **Europe's "rookie mistakes"**: austerity, no printing, and no protection for systemic entities until the pain became intolerable.

## Worked Example

**How authority was found, step by step.** The case shows each rescue as a legal problem as much as a financial one.

| Problem | Constraint | Workaround used |
|---|---|---|
| Bear Stearns run, March 2008 | The Treasury could spend only as Congress designated. No one could put capital into an investment bank | The Fed used Section 13(3), lent $30 billion non-recourse against Bear's mortgage pool, and a private buyer took the firm |
| GSEs, July to September 2008 | A large dollar request would "spook" Congress, and the authority granted was temporary | Paulson asked for "unspecified" authority. The Treasury issued long-term preferred stock at once, turning temporary authority into a lasting backstop, and gave the firms no warning |
| Lehman, September 2008 | Solvency problem, no buyer, loans limited by collateral | None. The firm failed |
| AIG, 16 September 2008 | Not enough safe collateral | The Fed lent against the insurance subsidiaries and nearly everything else AIG owned, on the view that AIG was solvent |
| Money market fund run | No appropriated cash | The Exchange Stabilization Fund, meant to defend the dollar, backed a guarantee, limited to funds in trouble as of 19 September so that bank deposits would not flee |
| Frozen commercial paper | The Fed may not take much unsecured credit risk | The CPFF, technically a separate entity funded by Fed loans under 13(3), "to the edge of its statutory authority or perhaps a bit beyond" |
| Bank debt run, October 2008 | The FDIC insures deposits. Its general counsel called the plan illegal | The FDIC head backed the TLGP. Paulson called it the most powerful step taken |
| Bank capital | TARP was sold as asset purchases, and weak banks would be stigmatized | Authority for capital was written in, and the nine largest banks were all made to take it |
| Auto makers, December 2008 | TARP money could go only to financial institutions | Funds went through the car makers' finance arms, and GMAC became a bank. In 2009 a "cushioned bankruptcy" for GM and Chrysler |

Lesson stated in the text: there were "numerous close calls in which the parties involved did the things that needed to be done, even if that required them to get around the rules".

**The cascade in brief.** Leverage on home equity ($50,000 down on a $250,000 house that rises to $350,000 triples the stake) → tightening and rate resets → early payment defaults at subprime lenders → mark-to-market losses cut capital and force sales → wholesale funding runs → Bear, the GSEs, Lehman → the avalanche into AIG, money funds, commercial paper and non-financial firms → blanket guarantees, capital, liquidity, QE and fiscal stimulus → selling exhausted on 9 March 2009.

## Key Takeaways
1. Look for bubbles sector by sector with cash-flow projections. Averages hide them.
2. Measure fragility by leverage, funding maturity and who holds what. Counterparty exposures can be seen only "one level deep".
3. Once rates are near 0 percent and deleveraging has begun, expect the 1930s and Japan-1990s dynamic, and expect policy makers to print.
4. Authority is the binding constraint. Track what each agency is legally able to do, not only what is needed.
5. Rallies on policy news recur inside the decline: 19 percent in late October 2008 and 24 percent in the last six weeks of 2008 were both given back.
6. Speed is the difference between a short depression and a long one.
7. Ending or slowing asset purchases is a tightening.
8. Three kinds of investors appear in a bear market: those who sell as they lose, those who hold on faith, and the few who understand it. Size bets by the gap between pricing and likely outcomes.

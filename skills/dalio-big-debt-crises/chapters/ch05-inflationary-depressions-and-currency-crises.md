# Chapter 5: Inflationary Depressions and Currency Crises (the Classic Inflationary Debt Cycle)

*Covers Part 1, "Inflationary Depressions and Currency Crises" and "The Phases of the Classic Inflationary Debt Cycle", stages 1) to 5). Memory class: method.*

## Core Idea
An inflationary depression is a debt crisis in which money leaves the currency. It happens where a country depends on foreign capital and owes foreign-currency debt it cannot print. The archetype, an average of 27 cases, runs through five stages. The decisive policy choice is how the currency is let go: a large, surprising, one-off devaluation with tight enough policy afterwards works, and a slow defended decline does not.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The currency dynamic**: if a currency falls against another at a rate greater than its interest rate, holders of debt in it lose money. If they expect that to continue without higher rates as compensation, they sell. That dynamic "is what produces inflationary depressions".
- **Inflow/outflow trade-off rule**: the central bank's trade-off between inflation and growth is easier when money flows into the currency/debt and harder when it flows out. With outflows, "real interest rates need to rise less if real exchange rates fall more (and vice versa)".
- **Vulnerability checklist**: inflationary depressions are far more likely in countries that:
  1. Do not have a reserve currency.
  2. Have low foreign-exchange reserves.
  3. Have a large foreign debt.
  4. Have a large and increasing budget and/or current account deficit.
  5. Have negative real interest rates.
  6. Have a history of high inflation and negative total returns in the currency.
  - How: the more of these are present, and the stronger, the more severe the inflationary depression.
- **The five stages of the inflationary cycle**: 1) the early part of the cycle, 2) the bubble, 3) the top and currency defense, 4) the depression (often when the currency is let go), 5) normalization. They mirror the deflationary stages up to stage 4.
- **Why currency defenses fail (interest parity)**: the expected decline is priced into how far the forward sits below the spot. An expected 5 percent fall over a year needs a 5 percent higher yield. An expected 5 percent fall over a month needs 5 percent for the month, about 80 percent annualized with compounding (footnote 10). So a small expected depreciation means an intolerable rate premium.
  - Tell: the forward declines ahead of the spot ("a whip-like effect"). After the currency is let go, the spot catches up and the forward rallies relative to it.
- **The one-off devaluation rule**: it is "generally better to have a large, one-off devaluation" that gets the currency to a level with a two-way market. If it is a surprise, no reserves or rate rises are spent defending beforehand.
- **Three ends of the squeeze**: the squeeze ends when a) debts are defaulted on and/or enough money is created, b) debt service is reduced some other way (for example forbearance), and/or c) the currency depreciates much more than inflation picks up, so the balance of payments improves.
- **Positive total return test**: to keep capital in, policy makers must produce a positive total return for the currency (spot change plus interest rate difference) at an interest rate that is not too high for domestic conditions. A currency level that is a) good for the trade balance, b) produces a positive total return and c) allows an appropriate interest rate "is a low one".

## Key Concepts
- **Foreign-exchange reserves**: foreign-currency assets the central bank holds, "like savings", used to bridge gaps between currency demand and supply.
- **Balance of payments crisis**: the spiral of weakening inflows and asset prices that follows the top.
- **Two-way market**: a level where people both buy and sell the currency because no broad expectation of further weakness exists.
- **Catharsis**: the author's word for hitting bottom, when pain forces a change in pricing and policy.
- **Structurally long currency positions**: exposures entities build in the hot currency, often without fully recognizing them.
- **Capital controls**: a third, often last-ditch lever that "seldom works".
- **GDP gap**: the level of economic activity relative to potential.

## Mental Models
- **A country is a family that can change its currency.** A 30 percent pay cut ruins a family. A 30 percent devaluation is a pay cut only relative to the rest of the world. It lets a country cut prices to foreigners without domestic deflation.
- **Devaluation is cocaine.** "It provides short-term stimulation but is ruinous when abused."
- **Capital controls are a bank-run trigger.** Fear of being trapped leads people to want to escape.
- **Domestic and foreign savers watch different things (footnote 11).** Domestic investors compare inflation with interest rates. Foreign lenders compare the rate of change in the currency with the interest rate change. For stabilizing the balance of payments, inflation is secondary to giving positive expected returns in the currency.
- **Watch capital flows first.** They are "typically the most important flows to watch because they are the most volatile", and weakening capital flows are often the first shoe to drop.

## Anti-patterns
- **Defending the currency with reserves and rate rises**: it gives sellers an opportunity without moving the currency and rates to sustainable levels.
- **A gradual, persistent decline**: it makes the market expect more, widens the balance of payments gap and feeds an inflation psychology.
- **Running reserves too low or rates too high in defense**: those dangers are greater than the dangers of devaluation.
- **Favoring domestic conditions with loose money during the adjustment**: it postpones pain and stokes inflation.
- **Using reserve sales to keep spending high.**
- **Disorderly defaults** that raise uncertainty and capital flight.

## Worked Example

**The currency defense, step by step.**

1. Inflows slow. Growth weakens, borrowers look less creditworthy, foreigners lend less.
2. The central bank fills the gap from reserves and raises rates. Officials "make boldly confident statements" that the currency will hold.
3. The market expects, say, 5 percent depreciation in a month. Parity requires a 5 percent monthly premium, about 80 percent a year. The economy is already weak, so that rate is intolerable.
4. The forward falls ahead of the spot as the rate differential widens.
5. After roughly six months and a 10 to 20 percent loss of reserves, the defense is abandoned.
6. The currency falls about 30 percent in real terms. FX debtors' burdens jump, they sell local currency to repay or hedge, and the decline feeds itself.
7. Imports collapse, the current account swings by about 8 percent of GDP, and the currency is cheap enough that holding it pays. Returns to foreigners start negative, then rally about a year after the devaluation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Score a country on the six vulnerability items before anything else.
2. In the depression, printing is small (1 to 2 percent of GDP) because printing funds capital flight. That is the opposite of the deflationary case.
3. The best defense of a currency is to make it cheap quickly, then run policy tight enough that holding it pays.
4. A falling forward relative to spot signals a defense that is likely to end.
5. Negative returns to currency holders for too long is "frequently the break-point" between a transitory crisis and an inflationary spiral.
6. A reserve-currency country can have an inflationary depression too. It comes later and more slowly, after "sustained and repeated overuse of stimulation".
7. Before going long after a devaluation, watch what the central bank does.

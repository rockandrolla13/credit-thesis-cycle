# Patterns and Techniques: A Template for Understanding Big Debt Crises

Concrete techniques the book states. Each names its chapter file.

## Classify the crisis by currency of the debt
**When to use**: first step for any debt problem.
**How**: ask whether the debt is denominated in a currency the country's policy makers control, and whether they can influence how creditors and debtors deal with each other. Domestic-currency debt points to the deflationary template (Ch 2 to Ch 4). Significant foreign-currency debt points to the inflationary template (Ch 5, Ch 6). Treat a gold peg as akin to foreign-currency debt (Ch 8).
**Trade-offs**: the two groups overlap. The book reports about a 75 percent correlation between foreign debt and inflation, not a clean split.

## Seven-stage placement (deflationary)
**When to use**: locating an economy in the long-term debt cycle.
**How**: track total debt/GDP and debt service/GDP. Place the economy in one of: early part, bubble, top, depression, beautiful deleveraging, pushing on a string, normalization (Ch 2 to Ch 4).
**Trade-offs**: archetype charts exclude about a third of cases as outliers. Timing is not pre-destined.

## Bubble checklist
**When to use**: testing a single market for a bubble.
**How**: seven questions on price versus traditional measures, discounted future appreciation, bullish sentiment, leverage, forward purchases, new buyers, and the role of monetary policy (Ch 2). Then map what is connected to that market.
**Trade-offs**: the book gives the questions, not Bridgewater's thresholds.

## Pro forma cash-flow stress test
**When to use**: finding debt problems hidden under averages.
**How**: for each important sector and institution, project whether it can get the new lending needed to roll maturing debt and sustain its activity. For lenders, mark illiquid holdings using prices of analogous liquid assets (Ch 9).
**Trade-offs**: exposures can be seen only "one level deep", especially through derivatives.

## Solvency or cash-flow diagnosis
**When to use**: deciding how to rescue an institution.
**How**: a solvency problem needs equity capital or changed accounting rules. A cash-flow problem needs cash or guarantees (Ch 3).
**Trade-offs**: accounting rule changes hide the problem, they do not remove losses.

## Four immediate actions for systemic institutions
**When to use**: once systemically important institutions are at risk.
**How**: 1) curtail panic and guarantee liabilities, 2) provide liquidity against wider collateral and to more institutions, 3) support solvency through mergers, capital raising and accounting relief, 4) recapitalize, nationalize or cover losses (Ch 3).
**Trade-offs**: public anger and moral hazard. The book's position is to err on the side of too much support.

## Balance the four levers
**When to use**: managing a deleveraging in own-currency debt.
**How**: offset austerity and defaults with enough money printing to bring nominal growth above the nominal interest rate, without causing a run on debt assets (Ch 4).
**Trade-offs**: too little gives a long depression. Too much gives an ugly inflationary deleveraging.

## MP1, then MP2, then MP3
**When to use**: choosing the form of monetary stimulus.
**How**: use rates until about 0 percent. Use QE while risk and liquidity premiums are wide. When both are spent, put money more directly with spenders, ideally through coordinated fiscal and monetary action (Ch 4).
**Trade-offs**: QE widens the wealth gap and loses force as premiums compress.

## Vulnerability checklist for inflationary depressions
**When to use**: screening a country for currency-crisis risk.
**How**: score six conditions: no reserve currency, low reserves, large foreign debt, large and rising budget and/or current account deficit, negative real rates, history of high inflation and negative currency returns (Ch 5).
**Trade-offs**: reserve-currency countries can still have inflationary depressions, later and more slowly.

## Forward-versus-spot watch
**When to use**: judging whether a currency defense will hold.
**How**: the expected depreciation is priced into the forward discount and the interest differential. A forward falling ahead of spot, with reserves draining, signals a defense near its end (Ch 5).
**Trade-offs**: the text gives the mechanism and typical magnitudes, not a timing rule.

## One-off surprise devaluation
**When to use**: a policy maker closing an external imbalance.
**How**: devalue once, by enough to create a two-way market, without signaling it. Then run policy tight enough that holding the currency gives a positive total return (Ch 5).
**Trade-offs**: it stings savers and raises FX debt burdens immediately.

## Seven-part hyperinflation stabilization
**When to use**: ending a hyperinflation.
**How**: restructure the external debt, create a hard-backed new currency, cap issuance, end monetization, close the deficit, tighten credit, build reserves (Ch 7).
**Trade-offs**: deep austerity. It works only once the external burden is cut.

## Bank triage and reopening
**When to use**: a system-wide bank run.
**How**: close the banks briefly, audit each, then recapitalize, merge or close. Reopen sound banks with access to central bank funding against all assets and a government guarantee behind the central bank (Ch 8).
**Trade-offs**: requires breaking any constraint on money creation first.

## Stigma-free recapitalization
**When to use**: capital is needed across the banking system quickly.
**How**: offer preferred equity on attractive terms to all banks and require the largest to take it, so participation does not mark a bank as weak (Ch 9).
**Trade-offs**: politically unpopular.

## Case auto-summary schema
**When to use**: recording a new episode for comparison.
**How**: fill the fixed slots: label, build-up, shock, depression statistics, reflation statistics, levers pulled, recovery time (Ch 10, Ch 11).
**Trade-offs**: a deliberately simplified lens.

## Targeted credit restraint or easing
**When to use**: one sector is frothy or starved while the whole economy needs the opposite.
**How**: apply the six questions and the four tests, then use demand-side tools (loan-to-value, debt service-to-income, maturities, margins) and supply-side tools (capital and reserve requirements, permitted assets, accounting, supervisory pressure, rate ceilings) (Ch 12).
**Trade-offs**: picks winners and losers, invites workarounds, and can have second-order effects years later.

## Equity, not loans, for an insolvent institution
**When to use**: judging whether a lender-of-last-resort action can save a failing firm.
**How**: separate a liquidity gap from a capital gap. A central bank can lend against collateral. It cannot supply equity. "Loans were not equity" (Ch 9).
**Trade-offs**: without a private buyer or legal authority to inject capital, the firm fails.

## Guarantee over backstop
**When to use**: designing a measure to stop a run.
**How**: state a guarantee outright. Paulson's reading of the money market fund guarantee is that a stated guarantee reassures more than a backstop (Ch 9).
**Trade-offs**: the government takes the risk onto its own balance sheet.

## Capital injection over asset purchases
**When to use**: a fixed rescue budget is too small to buy the bad assets.
**How**: put the money into banks as capital. Banks lever it, so the same sum supports "many times" that amount of assets (Ch 9).
**Trade-offs**: the state becomes an owner. See "Stigma-free recapitalization".

## Leverage over VAR as a risk gauge
**When to use**: judging how fragile positions are after a calm period.
**How**: read leverage, not recent volatility. VAR sizes positions from recent volatility, so low volatility invites leverage and rising volatility forces selling (Ch 9).
**Trade-offs**: leverage outside the regulated banks is hard to measure. See the five components of shadow-bank leverage in Ch 9.

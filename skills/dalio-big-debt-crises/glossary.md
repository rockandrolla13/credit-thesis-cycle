# Glossary: A Template for Understanding Big Debt Crises

Alphabetical. Definitions follow the book's own wording where it gives one. (Ch N) is the chapter file that covers the term.

**AMC (asset-management company)** — a usually public entity that takes bad assets from failed or existing lenders, sells the performing ones quickly and works out the rest (Ch 3)
**Archetype** — the average path across cases: 21 deflationary and 27 inflationary, from 5 years before the bottom to 7 years after (Ch 1)
**Austerity** — spending less; the first of the four levers, deflationary (Ch 1, Ch 3)
**Balance of payments crisis** — the country's entities lack buying power in world markets; it "has essentially run out of cash and credit" (Ch 5, Ch 10)
**Beautiful deleveraging** — debt/income falls with positive growth and acceptable inflation, because nominal growth is above the nominal interest rate (Ch 4)
**Bubble** — a stage with self-reinforcing, unsustainable rises in debt, asset prices and growth (Ch 2, Ch 10)
**Capital controls** — limits on moving money out; a last-ditch lever that "seldom works" (Ch 5, Ch 7)
**Capitalists-investors / proletariat-workers** — the two groups money serves: holders of stored wealth and those who earn by selling time (Ch 1)
**Cash-flow problem** — an entity lacks cash, typically because its lenders are running; fixed with cash or guarantees (Ch 3)
**Credit** — the giving of buying power in exchange for a promise to pay it back (Ch 1)
**Currency defense** — spending reserves and raising rates to hold a currency; typically about six months before it is abandoned (Ch 5)
**"D" versus "R"** — a "D" is a contraction caused by financial deleveraging; an "R" is a recession caused by tight policy that ends when policy eases (Ch 9)
**Debt** — the promise to pay (Ch 1)
**Debt monetization** — the central bank printing money to buy or cover debt (Ch 3, Ch 4)
**Deflationary depression** — a debt crisis in mostly domestic-currency debt, where rates hit about 0 percent and defaults and austerity dominate (Ch 1, Ch 3)
**Deleveraging** — the process of reducing debt burdens (Ch 1, Ch 10)
**Deleveraging attribution** — Bridgewater's breakdown of what raised or lowered debt as a percent of GDP (Ch 10)
**Depression** — in the study design, a case where real GDP fell more than 3 percent; in the glossary, a severe downturn when central banks are limited in easing (Ch 1, Ch 10)
**Depression gauge** — Bridgewater's algorithm based on closeness of rates to 0 percent, debt vulnerability measures and signs of deleveraging beginning (Ch 9)
**Foreign-exchange reserves** — foreign-currency assets held by the central bank, "like savings" (Ch 5)
**Four levers** — austerity, debt defaults/restructurings, central bank money printing, and transfers from those with more to those with less (Ch 1, Ch 3)
**GDP gap** — an imprecise measure of activity relative to potential; positive means little slack (Ch 5, Ch 10)
**Goldilocks period** — the early part of the cycle: growth, debt growth and inflation neither too hot nor too cold (Ch 2)
**Helicopter money** — directing newly printed money into the hands of spenders (Ch 4)
**Hyperinflation** — prices more than doubling every year or worse, with extreme losses of wealth (Ch 6)
**Inflationary depression** — a debt crisis in a country reliant on foreign capital with foreign-currency debt, where currency falls produce inflation (Ch 1, Ch 5)
**Long-term debt cycle** — the sum of short-term cycles, each ending with higher debt-to-income, until rate cuts run out (Ch 1)
**Macroprudential policies** — tools that direct credit through the central bank's regulatory authorities (Ch 4, Ch 12)
**Monetary Policy 1 / 2 / 3** — interest-rate policy; quantitative easing; money put more directly in the hands of spenders (Ch 4)
**Money 0 (M0)** — physical currency in circulation plus reserves at the central bank (Ch 10)
**Moral hazard** — the concern that rescues encourage future excess; the text says to set it aside for systemic institutions in a crisis (Ch 3, Ch 9)
**Pushing on a string** — stimulus that fails to raise spending and private credit, late in the cycle (Ch 4)
**Real FX** — an imprecise measure of whether a currency is cheap or expensive against its past, usually trade-weighted (Ch 10)
**Rentenmark** — the hard-backed German currency of November 1923, pegged at 4.2 to the dollar (Ch 7)
**Run** — lenders or depositors withdrawing money from an institution (Ch 3)
**Section 13(3)** — the Federal Reserve Act power to lend beyond banks in an emergency; granted 1932, used in 2008 (Ch 8, Ch 9)
**Shadow banking** — lending outside the regulated and protected banking system (Ch 2, Ch 8, Ch 9)
**Solvency problem** — an entity lacks equity capital under accounting and regulatory rules; fixed with capital or rule changes (Ch 3)
**Systemically important institution** — one whose failure would threaten the financial system or economy (Ch 3)
**Transitory inflationary deleveraging** — the currency is let go, inflation is temporary, and policy makers regain flexibility on rates (Ch 11)
**Two-way market** — a currency level with both buyers and sellers, because further weakness is not broadly expected (Ch 5)
**Ugly deleveraging** — the depression phase, dominated by defaults and austerity; "ugly inflationary deleveraging" is the over-printing version (Ch 3, Ch 4)
**Wage-cost spiral** — wages rise to offset lost purchasing power, and producers raise prices in turn (Ch 6)

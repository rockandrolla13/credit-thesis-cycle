# Section III: Ownership and Financing of AI Infrastructure

**Type**: METHOD (capital-stack map) | **Source pages**: 6-8

## Core Idea
Capex now exceeds internal cash flow. The marginal dollar comes from outside capital. The structures move ownership and leverage away from the operating company. They do not fully move the economic risk.

## The financing constraint (Sec III, Fig 1)
- Five firms: Oracle, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, Alphabet.
- Capex rose from roughly $97bn (2020) to more than $400bn (2025). The projection for 2026 exceeds $800bn.
- "On current estimates, capex will surpass the five firms' combined operating cash flow for the first time in 2026."

## Who can borrow (Sec III)
- Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, Alphabet and Oracle are investment-grade. Long-term agreements with them produce predictable cash flows that support real estate, infrastructure and project-level financing.
- Frontier AI firms such as OpenAI and Anthropic "generally lack comparable standalone credit profiles". They get compute through contracts with hyperscalers.

## The two models (Sec III)
| | Old model | Marginal model now |
|---|---|---|
| Funding | Retained earnings and unsecured corporate debt | Third-party equity plus project-level debt |
| Owner | Hyperscaler | Developers, REITs, infrastructure funds, private equity |
| Who bears construction, utilisation and residual-value risk | Hyperscaler | Split by contract. Hyperscaler stays central through leases, capacity commitments and guarantees |
| Still used for | IT equipment especially | Shells, power infrastructure, real estate. Now extending to GPUs |

## Financing structures named in the paper
1. **On balance sheet**: retained earnings and unsecured corporate debt. (Sec III)
2. **Leases** from third-party owners: developers, REITs, infrastructure funds, private equity. (Sec III)
3. **Joint ventures** with a financial sponsor. (Sec III, Sec IV)
4. **Bank loans**: mortgage loans backed by data-centre assets, and syndicated credit lines. (Sec III)
5. **Private credit** and **structured finance**, "usually backed by long-term contracted revenues". A growing share of project funding. (Sec III)
6. **Special-purpose vehicles**, bankruptcy-remote, that issue debt against lease payments. (Sec IV)
7. **GPU and hardware finance** through leases or asset-backed structures. (Sec III)
8. **Vendor-linked finance** with a supplier residual value guarantee. (Sec V, footnote 9)

## GPU collateral warning (Sec III)
GPU collateral "differs from conventional real estate or aircraft". Its economic life is shorter. Rapid technical change creates obsolescence risk. Resale value is less certain. The author cites Moody's Ratings (2025).

## Size of external finance (Sec III). Morgan Stanley Research (2025) numbers, not the author's
- About $2.9tn is needed for hyperscalers' incremental compute over 2025-2028. More than half comes from outside capital.
- Across the full investment: about a 60-40 split between equity and debt.
- Within debt: private credit about $800bn, corporate debt about $200bn, structured finance about $150bn.

## The key analytic point: aggregate split hides asset-level leverage (Sec III)
- Hyperscalers direct internal capital mostly to IT equipment.
- Shells, power infrastructure and real estate go into project debt, leases and asset-backed structures.
- So loan-to-value and debt-to-equity on the underlying infrastructure can rise while reported hyperscaler leverage stays low.
- The shift "may also increase leverage within the broader AI infrastructure system and make that leverage less visible in operating-company balance sheets".

## Three-way separation (Sec III)
Users of compute. Owners of the assets. Investors who bear the financial risk. The separation draws in deeper capital and speeds scaling. It also creates "a more layered and potentially opaque allocation of risk".

# Chapter 8: How to Trade Equities: A Framework

## Core Idea
The default equity position is long. The job is to avoid the 20%-plus drawdowns, which come from recessions, deflating bubbles, stagflation and financial accidents. Momentum, the 200-day moving average, earnings revisions and bank reserves help. Valuation does not.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Drawdown taxonomy** (SPX total return over T-bills from the 1950s, drawdowns of at least 20%, a new drawdown only after a 25% rally from the low): 14 episodes. Seven were coincident with or directly preceding a recession. Others: deflating bubbles (1961-62, 2000-02), stagflation (1966, 1983-84, 2021-22), financial accidents (1987, 1998). Recession and deflating bubbles are the worst regimes.
  - Response: when recession indicators flash, do not cut risk wholesale. Take breaks of the 200dma more seriously.
- **Equities around Fed turning points** (median paths):

| Event | Pattern |
|---|---|
| First cut | Pullback three months before. Rally in the month before. Chop afterwards, then take-off about two months later. Skew to the downside |
| First cut, soft landing (1995, 1998, 2024) | Mixed returns into the cut. Very strong returns after. All three cycles totalled 75bp. "Cuts without a recession are the best-case outcome for equities" |
| Last cut | Bottom about two months before. Rally into it. A month sideways, then strong. Best entry is the first meeting where the Fed pauses |
| First hike | No clear pattern into it. Median pullback of around 5% after, quickly recovered. Skew to the upside |
| Last hike (from 1986) | Bullish after a few days of indigestion |

- **Rates and equities**:
  - Level of 10-year real rates (quintiles since TIPS inception): every quintile has a positive active IR. It deteriorates sharply above 1.8% (active IR 0.10 and 0.05 in the top two quintiles against 1.06 in the lowest). The authors set the warning light "at best to amber".
  - Change in 10-year real rates: the top quintile, a rise of more than 14bp in a month, has a negative active IR (-0.04). Consistently negative for equities since late 2014.
  - Breakevens: all quintiles positive. It does not matter much whether a sell-off is driven by real rates or breakevens.
  - Term premium (Kim-Wright model): a rise of more than 10bp in a month gives an active IR of -0.20.
  - Curve regimes (rolling 1m changes in 2y and 10y, SPX lagged one day): bull flattening is best (active IR 0.99) and holds the large majority of SPX gains. Bear flattening is worst (-0.12), concentrated in 1987 and 2022. Bear steepening 0.44, bull steepening 0.38. Bear steepening is "not as negative as widely believed".
- **Fed put**: after a 25% drawdown from the one-year high the Fed is cutting in the median case. A 20% drawdown is usually enough when inflation is low. In high-inflation episodes (1973, 2022) hikes continue longer. Credit, liquidity and market functioning can move the Fed earlier or later.
- **Equities, GDP and earnings lead-lag**:
  - Contemporaneous correlation of nominal GDP and equity returns is 0.02 (from 1947). Low GDP growth precedes higher equity returns two quarters later. High equity returns precede higher GDP one or two quarters later.
  - Earnings and GDP: contemporaneous correlation 0.125. Weak GDP precedes higher EPS growth with a three to five quarter lag.
  - Equities lead earnings by one to two quarters. The highest correlation is at the two-month lag. At lags beyond one year the relationship is negative.
- **Earnings revision index (ERI) rules** (Citi ERI, data from 2000):
  - Seasonal: estimates are revised down in pre-reporting season and up during reporting season. Full-year revisions typically fall slightly.
  - Trend use: equities have a higher IR with a positive ERI (0.45 vs 0.34 on the US ERI). The global ERI beats the US ERI even for US equities (0.87 when positive, 0.17 when negative, buy-and-hold 0.37). It adds alpha mostly by avoiding 2000 and 2008. It kept investors largely out from 2012 to 2016. "We would therefore use the ERI as a trend signal only if it is in line with the 200dma."
  - Contrarian use: buy the SPX when the global ERI moves into its fifth lowest percentile. Works for 1-month to 12-month holds (active IR 0.73 to 0.83 against 0.38). In the market 32% of the time on the 12-month hold. A depressed ERI is a better countersignal than depressed forward EPS. Works best for Asia ex Japan and EM.
  - Caveat stated by the authors: the percentile uses the full sample, so the test includes future information.
- **Liquidity rule (bank reserves)**: use the four-week moving average of weekly changes in bank reserves at the Fed to position for the following week. Total Fed balance sheet, reverse repos and the TGA have little relationship with returns.
- **Bubble framework** (GMO definition, Willer et al., 2024e):
  - Entry: price more than two standard deviations above the long-term real trend.
  - Exit: the market falls 15% from the peak, or price drops back inside two standard deviations.
  - Count: nine SPX bubbles from 1929. The internet bubble counts twice (split by 1998). The AI bubble counts twice (split by liberation day).
  - Rule: "not to sell equities when they enter bubble territory but to buy them." The second internet leg ran more than 470 trading days after entry. 1929 is the exception.
  - After exit, selling is safer. In almost all cases the large majority of the rally after entry was eventually reversed. Exception: 1958 went sideways.
  - Peak warnings: an aggressive Fed hiking cycle (necessary, not sufficient), falling liquidity, extreme bull-bear sentiment, a quick rise in margin debt, IPO activity, high retail participation (Dalio, 2021).
- **Generals rule** (Willer et al., 2024f): a general "fails" when it breaks below its 200dma and stays there at least five days. Cut equity risk when the fourth of the seven largest-cap leaders fails.
  - Backtest inside bubble periods plus one year after exit: IR moves from 0.67 unconditional to 1.04. With four or more generals failed the IR is -0.6. Better than a 200dma rule on the SPX itself.
  - In 2000 three failures put the bull market on notice and four called its end.
- **Value vs momentum sizing test** (Shiller data from 1950):
  - Value: size 50% to 150% by the rolling 30-year percentile of CAPE yield (1/CAPE). Underperforms buy-and-hold on return and IR. A seven-year CAPE gives a small IR benefit and lower returns. The rule averaged 85% invested because CAPE has drifted up.
  - Momentum: 150% when the x-month return is above its 50th percentile, 50% below. Beats buy-and-hold on return and risk/reward for 3, 6 and 12 months (0.69, 0.69, 0.63 against 0.52). Weaker at 84 months (0.57).
- **200d MA rule**: SPX IR rises from 0.44 to 0.69. Total return slightly lower. Maximum drawdown about half. An investor comfortable with SPX volatility of 17% should "lever up the SPX by 55% whenever the SPX is above the 200d MA".
- **Small-cap rules**: RTY vs SPX outperforms mostly when the manufacturing ISM is below 50 and above its 3-month average. It does much worse when the PMI is below 50 and falling. Small caps underperform when the NDX deviation from its 200dma is above its 85th percentile (active IR -1.04) and outperform when it is below the 15th (0.44). IRs for the ISM version are weak.

## Key Concepts
- **ERI**: breadth of analyst earnings revisions, up minus down.
- **Bubble (GMO)**: price two standard deviations above long-term real trend.
- **Generals**: the few large caps driving a narrowing market.
- **Fed put**: the equity drawdown at which the Fed cuts.
- **Bank reserves**: the Fed liability most reliably linked to market performance.
- **Post-earnings drift**: stocks keep moving in the direction of the earnings-day reaction. The authors found no profitable index-level rule from it.
- **CAPE**: cyclically adjusted price to earnings ratio.
- **Bear flattening**: 10-year yield rising, 2-year rising more.

## Mental Models
- Think of the 200dma stop as protection against selling even lower. Bear markets end when almost everyone has given up.
- Think of a bubble as real scarcity (bandwidth in the 1990s, computing power now) that inflates earnings as well as prices. A valuation-only test can miss it.
- Use the 2000 bubble as a ceiling for valuations, not as the average analogue.
- Think of the global cycle as more important than the local one for most equity markets.
- A value-plus-momentum alignment is a once-in-a-generation trade.

## Anti-patterns
- **Timing with CAPE**: no alpha in 20 years by the authors' account.
- **Shorting a bubble on entry**: the most explosive returns come after the warning.
- **Using breadth to time tops**: low breadth mostly occurs at bottoms. Breadth improved into the 2000 and 2007 tops.
- **Buy-and-hold through drawdowns**: few investors can endure them.
- **Requiring both ERI negative and SPX below 200dma to exit**: worse active IR than negative ERI alone.
- **Relying on the small-cap effect**: the Russell 2000 peaked against the SPX in July 1983.
- **Trading the SPX off total Fed balance sheet, TGA or reverse repos**: no reliable link.

## Worked Example
*"The Mother of All Bubbles", 1999 to 2002.*
1. The Fed hiked 175bp during the second leg of the internet bubble.
2. In Q4 1999 the Fed expanded its balance sheet by 19% to guard against the Y2K bug. This was the largest increase on record at the time. The Nasdaq rose 53% in the quarter.
3. From January 2000 the Fed withdrew that liquidity aggressively. The NDX peaked at the end of March at a trailing P/E of around 200.
4. The SPX recovered almost to its March level by September 2000. The NDX did not come close. The failure of the former leaders was the tell.
5. By August the manufacturing ISM was falling below 50. Fundamentals, proxied by semiconductor sales, peaked only seven months after the equity peak.
6. Among the seven generals of 2000 (Microsoft, Cisco, GE, Walmart, Intel, Nokia, Pfizer) three breaks below the 200dma warned and the fourth confirmed.
7. Both indexes fell until October 2002.
Lessons the authors draw: liquidity withdrawal and Fed hikes are the peak conditions, the leaders fail first, and equities lead fundamentals by months, not years.

## Key Takeaways
1. Stay long unless a recession, a deflating bubble, stagflation or an accident is in play.
2. Respect the 200dma. It halves the maximum drawdown.
3. Buy the first Fed pause after the last cut, and buy after the last hike.
4. Worry about a monthly rise of more than 14bp in real yields or 10bp in term premium, not about rate levels.
5. Be long when reserves are rising and the global ERI is positive. Buy hard when the ERI is in its lowest percentiles.
6. Ride a bubble after entry. Use the generals rule to get out.
7. Favour momentum over value for timing.

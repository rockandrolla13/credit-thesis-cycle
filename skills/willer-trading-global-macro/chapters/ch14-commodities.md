# Chapter 14: How to Trade Commodities

## Core Idea
Commodities are poor buy-and-hold assets and good episodic ones. They pay in Fed hiking cycles and inflation episodes, lose in recessions, and reward carry (backwardation) and trend. Each commodity has one dominant macro driver: the business cycle for copper, the USD and unaddressed inflation for gold, supply and curve shape for oil.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Why commodities lag equities**: equities gain from productivity. Productivity lowers marginal costs and raises commodity supply. Very high inflation helps commodities more, because it compresses equity multiples.
- **Roll yield problem**: after the financialization of 2004-2005, index investors concentrated in front contracts and kept curves in contango. BCOM energy total return was -0.80% a year from 1992 to 2025 while energy spot rose 4.48%. The BCOM total return index is still 50% below its 2008 peak at the time of writing. The S&P is up more than 150% over the same period.
- **Two "moments"**:
  - *Super cycle, 2000 to 2008*: Chinese industrialization (China's share of base metal demand went from 3% to 40%), years of underinvestment after the Asian crisis and the tech bubble, financialization, and a DXY down more than 40% from 2002 to 2008. The peak came in July 2008, well after the US recession began.
  - *The 1970s*: BCOM total return of about 24% a year. Two pegs broke. Oil: the 1973 embargo lifted prices almost 400% by January 1974, and the 1979 Iranian revolution another 400% by 1980. Gold: Nixon suspended convertibility in August 1971. The USD fell 37% against the DEM from 1971 to 1973. Gold rose more than 300% from 1970 to the end of 1974.
  - Conclusion: a repeat of that magnitude "seems very unlikely". The 1970s may mislead on the optimal commodity weight.
- **Inflation rule for commodities**: long BCOM when inflation is above 3% and the CPI is above its 5-month average. IR 0.66 vs 0.32 unconditional. On 22% of the time. It overstays after the 1970s.
- **Commodities and the Fed cycle**: commodities other than gold trade well in hiking cycles (Table 14.3). Outliers: 1997 (a solitary hike before the Asian and Russian crises) and 2022 (hikes driven by inflation fear, not growth).
- **Commodities in recessions**: BCOM sell-offs are comparable in size to SPX sell-offs. Commodities bottom after the recession starts. Gold bottoms a median 24 days before the start. Base metals bottom 226 days after, slightly after equities. Energy is in between. Gold outperforms copper into and during recessions and gives it back when the recession ends.
- **Trend following**: long or short 18 contracts by position relative to an x-day moving average, volatility adjusted, 10% target volatility. A combination of moving averages (22d, 66d, 200d, 252d) gives a Sharpe ratio of 0.74. Single averages give 0.55 to 0.68. Performance has weakened since the early 2000s.
- **Cross-commodity carry**: long the most backwardated markets, short those most in contango, across 7 to 10 futures markets, positions volatility adjusted. IR 1.11. The BCOM-weighted version is lower at 0.75.
- **Curve carry**: short the front and long the back of each curve in equal amounts, USD neutral. IR 1.04. The risk-adjusted version sizes each curve by carry over volatility and makes front and back beta neutral. IR 2.04.
- **Bottom-up checklist**:
  1. Supply concentration. More concentrated means more exposed to a shock.
  2. Speed of supply response. Soft commodities are fast. A new copper mine takes 10 to 16 years.
  3. Demand elasticity. Oil demand is relatively elastic, so price spikes destroy demand. Where the commodity is a small share of budgets, prices must move far more.
  4. Inventories (exchange, trade, strategic) and spare capacity.
  5. Futures structure. Physical delivery makes shortages more extreme.
  6. Think in real prices. WTI at 120 USD after the Ukraine invasion compares to 150 USD in early 1980 in today's money.
  7. Storability. Gold and copper store easily. Energy does not, so surpluses press prices harder.
  8. Marginal cost. Bottoms occur near it, but it rises in booms and falls in busts.
- **Copper rules**:
  - Buy the front-month future the day after Citi's global data change index moves above its 55dma. Active IR 1.06 vs 0.41. Adding DXY below its 55dma gives 1.10. "Copper is mostly about the business cycle, and much less about FX."
  - Adding the Li Keqiang index above its 3-month average (lagged 15 business days) to data momentum above its 3-month average gives IR 1.09. Alone they give 0.89 and 0.86, against 0.33.
  - Structural: the energy transition "could be the new China", with a projected 45% to 50% of copper demand by 2030. Position more aggressively in upcycles than in downcycles.
  - Marginal cost for copper: the 75th percentile mine's C1 cash cost plus sustaining capital. News of mine closures helps confirm a bottom.
- **Copper/gold ratio**: best when the data change index is positive and DXY is above its 55dma, lagged one day. Active IR 1.05 vs 0.1. Both conditions matter: data momentum alone gives 0.82, and with a weak USD 0.57. The ratio confirms ISM turns at higher frequency but does not always lead.
- **Gold rules**:
  - Not a risk-aversion trade: long gold when VIX is above its 55dma gives active IR 0.37 vs 0.15. With VIX also above 25 the chart shows 0.35. Gold above its own 200dma gives 0.39.
  - Not an inflation hedge: CPI above its 3-month average barely improves the IR (0.21), with or without a 3% threshold (0.16), against 0.15.
  - What drives it: "it is not so much inflation that supports gold, but inflation that is not addressed by the Fed". Gold is a hedge for USD debasement fears and benefits from excess liquidity.
  - Real rates and USD rule: long gold when five-year real rates and DXY are both below their 55dma. Contemporaneous active IR 1.60 vs 0.47. With a one-day lag it collapses to 0.11. Use it to express a forward view, not as a mechanical signal.
  - Relative data momentum: EM data momentum above the US is bullish for gold and works with a one-day delay. Active IR 0.79 vs 0.47, and 0.17 when EM data lag. Lower return.
  - Overbought rule: gold 20% above its 200dma for the first time in three months tends to reverse in short order. Tactical profit-taking only.
  - Structural: central bank buying to diversify away from the USD. Bullish episodes will be more bullish than the model says and bearish ones less bearish.
  - Supply overhang rule of thumb: "removing 50% of a large supply overhang is enough for the relevant asset price to bottom."
- **Oil rules**:
  - Oil is the most important commodity for macro because it drives headline and, with a delay, core inflation. Supply shocks explain more of its variance than for metals. OPEC controls around 40% of supply and OPEC+ more than 50%.
  - ISM: best when the PMI is below 50 and above its 3-month average (active IR 1.05). Worst when below 50 and falling (0.01).
  - Curve: backwardation bullish, contango bearish. Best spec is 12 months vs 1 month, active IR 0.49 against 0.20 for always-long BCOM WTI. Weaker since COVID. Reduce longs when it moves into contango.
  - OPEC meeting days: price action follows through. Rallies on the day of a cut or an increase have strong follow-through. Sell-offs have short, shallow follow-through and turn up a few weeks later. Meetings do not change the prior trend, which starts about a month before.
  - Positioning: when the combined Brent and WTI speculative long/short ratio falls below 2, Brent was 20% higher after 100 trading days in the median case. The skew is negative at first. Strongest with a trigger that challenges consensus.

## Key Concepts
- **Contango / backwardation**: upward or downward sloping futures curve. Contango gives negative roll yield.
- **Roll yield**: the carry earned or lost as a contract moves down the curve.
- **Financialization**: institutional index inflows into commodities from 2004-2005.
- **Depeg**: the break of a long-standing fixed price. It releases positions built on the peg.
- **Spare capacity**: inventories held below ground.
- **Marginal cost of production**: the cost of the marginal producer, the theoretical floor.
- **Demand destruction**: a price spike that removes its own demand.
- **Washington agreement on gold**: September 1999 pact of fifteen European central banks limiting gold sales.
- **Curve carry**: short front, long back on the same commodity.

## Mental Models
- Think of commodities as a hedge for specific times, not a portfolio core.
- Think of gold as "just another currency" whose supply governments cannot manipulate. It holds its own against the USD but not against equity or bond total returns.
- When things go extreme "the curve will just not price enough steepness".
- OPEC meetings are "the FOMC days of the oil market". OPEC raises supply when the market can bear it, as issuers do in credit.
- A structural buyer (energy transition for copper, central banks for gold) skews the cycle: trade it, with more size on the upside.

## Anti-patterns
- **Buy-and-hold commodity indexes**: most have negative or near-zero Sharpe ratios from 1975.
- **Gold as an inflation hedge**: it fell in 2022 once the Fed hiked.
- **Mechanical gold rules on DXY and real rates**: destroyed by a one-day lag.
- **Calling a bottom from boom-time marginal cost**: the cost has fallen since.
- **Trading oil on the business cycle alone**: IRs are low.
- **Fading Middle East violence when speculators are at maximum short**: oil rose 9% in five days in late September 2024.
- **Front-month oil ETFs in a storage glut**: roll and contract choice dominate returns.

## Worked Example
*"Oil Goes Negative", April 2020.*
1. Spot WTI fell from just above 60 USD in early 2020 to around 20 USD by the end of March. Producers were slow to shut in because closing and reopening wells is costly.
2. The S&P bottomed on March 23. Oil did not. Storage filled.
3. On April 20 the deliverable May contract, one day from its last trading day, hit around -38 USD. The June contract traded at 20.40 the same day.
4. Buying June looked expensive against the front. It settled at 32.50 at expiry on May 19, an almost 60% gain in about a month.
5. Buying the February 2021 contract, to avoid the storage problem, returned 45% to year-end against 31% for the S&P 500.
6. Holders of the USO ETF gained much less, because the fund had moved from front contracts to a more even spread in late April.
Lesson: in an extreme, the deferred contracts still underprice the recovery. Contract choice is the trade.

*"Brown's Bottom"* (second example). The BoE announced gold auctions on May 7, 1999. Gold fell 15% in three months. The Washington agreement in September 1999 lifted gold 27% in two to three weeks, mostly unwound. Gold retested the lows in early 2001, when about 65% of the planned sales were done, and then began a multi-year bull market. The BoE sold 395 of 415 planned tons. Gold is up more than 800% since the announcement against around 150% for Treasuries. The authors' forward view: "the PBoC will set the top" when China finishes diversifying into gold.

## Key Takeaways
1. Own commodities for hiking cycles and unaddressed inflation, not for the long run.
2. Prefer backwardated markets and carry strategies. Avoid contango roll.
3. Trade copper with global data momentum. Trade copper/gold with data momentum and a strong USD.
4. Trade gold on the Fed and the USD, not on CPI. Take profit 20% above the 200dma.
5. For oil combine ISM turn, curve shape, OPEC-day price action and speculative positioning. None is enough alone.
6. Expect commodities to bottom after a recession starts, gold excepted.
7. Run the supply checklist before sizing a shortage trade.

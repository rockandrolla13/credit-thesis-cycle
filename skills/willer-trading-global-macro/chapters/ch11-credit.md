# Chapter 11: How to Trade Credit

## Core Idea
Trade credit as spread return, separate from duration. Spread returns behave like equities with a capped upside and a fat left tail, so the edge comes from getting out before trouble (volatility, lending standards, recession) and from structural pockets such as fallen angels. Credit is a poor late-cycle asset and a good hedge and warning signal for equities.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Split total return into duration and spread**: credit excess returns (over duration-matched Treasuries) are nearly always negatively correlated with government bond returns and strongly positively correlated with equities. "Investors, therefore, should choose carefully which risk to take, rather than taking the joint duration and credit risk." The rest of the chapter uses spread returns.
- **Credit by macro regime** (Figure 11.3): corporate bond excess returns are best late in a recession, when spreads are wide and the recession is mature. Goldilocks is decent. Early recession is worst, "though not that negative on average". Equities beat IG excess returns in almost all regimes on return and on IR. Stagflation is the exception, where IG is slightly better. "This limits the case for credit from a regime investing point of view."
- **Default cycle facts**:
  - IG 12-month trailing defaults peak at 0.4% to 0.6%, because most IG is downgraded before default.
  - HY defaults peak at the same time, at 10% to 15%. Cross-cycle median is closer to 3.5%. Low was 0.85% in December 2021.
  - Defaults lag. HY default peaks came after the recession ended: January 2002 vs November 2001, November 2009 vs June 2009, September 2020 vs April 2020.
- **SLOOS rule**: be long credit only if the previous Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey shows a loosening of credit standards.
  - Result: IR 1.10 vs 0.46 buy-and-hold. It works mostly by cutting out part of the GFC and COVID.
  - Chain: SLOOS leads defaults and lags market prices. Tighter standards mean less lending, more rollover risk, then defaults.
  - Corollary: "When the first SLOOS is published, that suggests improving conditions after a spike higher, the credit market usually keeps rallying." Exception: 2002 (Worldcom, Enron, valuation).
- **Max-volatility cut-out rule** (from the authors' EM book):
  1. Take implied volatility for equities (VIX), bonds (MOVE), oil (OVX), FX (G10 and EM), and high-yield credit spreads.
  2. Compute the one-year z-score of each.
  3. Take the maximum, because "we can never be certain in which corner of the market volatility strikes".
  4. Cut long credit when the maximum is above 2 standard deviations. Buy back when it falls below 2.
  5. Hold the position at least a month. Exit on the next day's close.
  - Result: IR 1.11 vs 0.42 unconditional. IRs are negative with the indicator above 2 and more negative above 3. Holding only below -1 has an even better IR but far lower total return.
- **Credit and the Fed** (data from 1989, daily from 1998):
  - Credit does not rally into the first cut. It underperforms the unconditional case. The real underperformance starts about 100 days later as recession fears rise.
  - The average spread return in US IG a year after the first cut is still negative. The skew is much more negative than for equities.
  - Around the last hike, credit vs equities (volatility matched) goes sideways with an extremely negative skew. "Credit is therefore not an attractive asset class for late-cycle investing."
- **Valuation**: use index OAS as the value proxy. In IG the level of spreads has very little relationship with the next quarter's return. In HY wider spreads mean higher expected returns. Starting both in 2002, the IG correlation is half the HY one.
- **Carry**: HY outperforms IG on total return and on IR (0.41 vs 0.35 on spread returns). Volatility targeting does not work well in credit: under monthly targeting on trailing three-month volatility IG ends up with more risk, because it sells off from very low volatility. Daily adjustment improves it. Neither competes with the implied-volatility cut-out.
- **Junk or quality (ISM rule)**: when the manufacturing ISM is above 50, US HY outperforms US IG on a risk-adjusted basis, and vice versa. Active IR 0.63 above 50 and -0.78 below 50.
- **Fallen angel effect**: Ba-rated credit has the best IR of any rating bucket. Downgrades from IG to HY force IG funds to sell, and hedge funds front-run. Prices then recover.
  - CAIA study (Benson and Hayes, 2019), January 2005 to March 2019: bonds entering a fallen angels index are priced about 150bp cheaper than HY peers. Over the following 12 months they outperform the BB index by almost 10 percentage points. Biggest after down years (2009, 2016).
  - Reasons given: longer duration and bullet structures, large companies in IG industries motivated to regain IG, and already-oversold prices at downgrade.
- **Rising star pattern**: rising stars are almost twice as likely to come from fallen angels as from the rest of HY (Insight Investments). They outperform for a long time before the upgrade. OAS compresses by 20bp in the final month (Columbia Threadneedle). After the upgrade little is left. Buying fallen angels is the practical way to be long rising stars. Regaining IG took around 3.5 years between 2000 and 2021 and less than one year for the 2019 cohort.
- **Supply fallacy**: supply is endogenous to the credit environment. Correlation of total returns with same-month supply averages -0.12. With returns lagged one month it is +0.22. "We typically ignore supply forecasts."
- **Credit canary**:
  - Spreads are tightest around 12 months before a recession. The SPX peaks around six months before (seven recessions since 1973).
  - The largest part of the credit sell-off occurs during the recession. Do not cover credit shorts too early.
  - US IG spread returns underperform equities, risk-adjusted, into recessions in the median case. Exception: 2001, an equity-bubble recession.
  - **VIX jump plus credit rule**: cut SPX risk for one month when VIX jumps 3 points or more, but only if HY spreads were wider over the last six months at the time of the local SPX high. Active IR 0.94 vs 0.53 buy-and-hold. The VIX jump alone gives 0.55.
- **Credit as an equity hedge** (Figure 11.19): in major equity drawdowns (2008 GFC, 2011 euro crisis, 2018 risk-off, COVID, 2022 bond sell-off), volatility-weighted shorts in US IG and global HY protected in every episode. Treasuries were less consistent and lost in 2022, as did short USDJPY. Gold was slightly worse than Treasuries. IG and HY shorts were similar on average.
- **Is the cycle credit-driven?**: check how fast household and corporate credit grew in the preceding bull years. Without major private credit growth, credit shorts are unlikely to protect fully against equity downside.
- **Private credit monitor**: watch the traded equities of private credit funds. Above their 200dma, "investors should probably not worry too much about private credit". Below it risks could increase. The authors call this "very speculative".
- **Mind the IG tails**: in acute stress the lowest IG ratings underperform the most. Single-A and Baa can do as badly as Ba and B, or worse, even without volatility adjustment.
- **Loans logic**: levered loans are floating rate with credit risk similar to HY. They outperform HY when Fed funds are above their 55-day moving average, underperform in easing cycles, and bounce back after easing cycles. Loans are volatility-adjusted at about 60% of HY volatility.
- **EM vs US credit**: long EM credit vs US IG, risk-adjusted, when DXY is below its 55dma (one-day lag). IR 0.78 vs 0.37 unconditional.
- **Japanese lens on US IG**: four regimes by (a) whether US IG yield hedged into JPY exceeds JGB yields and (b) whether USDJPY is above its 55dma. Best: attractive relative carry and rising USDJPY. Worst: poor relative carry and falling USDJPY.

## Key Concepts
- **Spread return / excess return**: credit return after hedging Treasury duration.
- **OAS**: option-adjusted spread, the valuation proxy.
- **SLOOS**: the Fed's quarterly survey of bank lending standards.
- **Fallen angel**: issuer downgraded from IG to HY.
- **Rising star**: issuer upgraded from HY to IG.
- **Levered loans**: floating-rate sub-investment-grade loans.
- **Private credit**: non-bank, directly negotiated, mostly floating-rate lending with three to seven year maturity. Not traded.
- **Negative skew**: upside capped at the coupon, downside up to 100%.
- **Endogenous supply**: issuance rises when demand is strong and is pulled when it is weak.

## Mental Models
- Think of credit as the equity trade with the upside cut off. Own it when the left tail is unlikely, not late in the cycle.
- Think of credit investors as faster to react: their payoff is asymmetric, refinancing depends on debt markets, and there is no "other mountain to climb" after default.
- Use lagging indicators to locate the cycle. "If credit did not react negatively to a tightening of lending standards, how could spreads possibly widen when the lending environment improves?"
- Government balance sheets can print money. Private ones cannot. Liabilities socialized in COVID left household and corporate balance sheets clean.
- Use the Japan regime as a demand lens, not as the main driver.

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying credit into the first Fed cut**: no rally, negative skew.
- **Owning credit late cycle instead of equities**: flat relative performance with a severe left tail.
- **Timing IG on spread level**: little relationship with forward returns.
- **Volatility targeting credit monthly**: IG ends up riskier.
- **Forecasting returns from issuance**: the sign is wrong one month later.
- **Hedging an equity-bubble recession with credit shorts**: 2001 showed credit outperforming.
- **Covering credit shorts at the start of a recession**: most of the widening comes during it.
- **Treating single-A and Baa as safe in acute stress**: they can gap as much as HY.
- **Chasing rising stars after the upgrade**: the trade is done.

## Worked Example
*Using a lagging indicator: SLOOS in 2021 to 2023.*
1. Banks started tightening credit standards in late October 2021. Tightening continued until July 2023.
2. Spreads peaked in October 2022 and retested the peak in the March 2023 banking crisis. The market led the survey by about a year.
3. The October 2023 SLOOS, published in early November, was the first print showing the extent of tightening reversing.
4. The argument for staying long: spreads had not widened much while standards tightened, so they were unlikely to widen as lending conditions improved.
5. Outcome: US IG spreads tightened a further 40bp.
6. The same logic failed in 2002, when equities kept falling on corporate governance scandals and dragged credit lower even as conditions improved.
The rule works because the turn in the survey marks a regime, and "some money is typically left" after it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Decide duration and spread separately. Do not buy corporate bonds as one bet.
2. Hold spread risk when the maximum cross-asset volatility z-score is below 2 and the last SLOOS loosened.
3. Prefer equities to credit late in the cycle and around the first Fed cut.
4. Prefer HY to IG when the ISM is above 50. Prefer IG below 50.
5. Buy fallen angels. Ba is the best risk-adjusted bucket.
6. Ignore supply forecasts.
7. Use credit to warn on equities: a VIX jump matters when HY spreads were already wider.
8. Prefer loans to HY while the Fed is hiking, and EM credit to US IG while the USD is weak.

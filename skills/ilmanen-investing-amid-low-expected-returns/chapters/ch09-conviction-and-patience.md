# Chapter 9: Sustaining Conviction and Patience on Long-run Return Sources

## Core Idea
Investors demand more consistency than competitive markets can give, and they quit good strategies too early. The chapter gives the statistics of underperformance, ways to build patience, and a rule for discounting backtests.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The core question**: how do you weigh three bad years against a century of positive evidence? Statisticians say decades are needed. Investors judge on their own recent experience.
- **Law of small numbers** (Kahneman): expecting a long-run edge to show in a short sample.
- **Myopic loss aversion**: frequent review plus losses weighing about twice as much as gains.
- **Regression to the mean versus mean reversion** (note 3): after an extreme result the best forecast is the long-run average. Mean reversion is the stronger claim that above-average is followed by below-average.
- **Years needed for significance** (note 6): t-statistic ≈ Sharpe ratio × √years. A t of 2 needs about 25 years at SR 0.4 and about 8 years at SR 0.7.
- **Three costs of impatience**: missing the long-run premium, turnover costs (including the forfeited high-water-mark option), and mean reversion against return chasers. Investors "act like momentum investors at reversal horizons".
- **Five ways to build patience at a given Sharpe ratio** (9.1):
  1. Education: realistic odds of bad outcomes, and the strategic case.
  2. Review broadly and infrequently. Avoid "line-item thinking". Report contribution, not standalone results.
  3. Organizational commitment: due diligence, written beliefs, the strategy in the benchmark, pre-set plans such as rebalancing.
  4. Conventionality: only the equity premium "is easily forgiven a losing decade".
  5. Gradualism: phase in new strategies to reduce first-year regret.
- **Two reasons to discount history** (9.3): overfitting (the edge was never that big) and overcrowding (it was, and the world changed). "Pick your poison!" The author worries more about overfitting.
- **Persistent / pervasive / robust**: the author's preferred answer to the factor zoo, over statistical multiple-testing fixes.
- **Discount rule of thumb**: "A common practice of halving the gross SR is crude but not a bad starting place." Apply larger discounts to older returns and to more complex strategies.

## Worked Example: Buffett's bad patches (Figure 9.2)
- Berkshire Hathaway beat the S&P 500 by 9.5% a year over 44 years (1977 to March 2021).
- The rolling 3-year relative return was negative 30% of the time.
- It was negative by double digits, annualized, in three episodes.
The author's point: if the most admired investor tests patience this way, any good strategy will.

## Key Concepts
- **Outcome bias**: see Ch 1.
- **Disposition effect**: holding losers too long. The opposite error to impatience.
- **Peso problem**: rare severe losses absent from the sample.
- **Factor zoo**: the hundreds of published factors.
- **Description-experience gap**: people overweight what they lived through.

## Mental Models
- A bad patch with rising valuations points to crowding being arbitraged away. A bad patch with *cheapening*, like value in 2018-2020 with widening spreads, does not.
- "This time is different" is right sometimes. Markets say it too often. Structural changes can reverse through competition, regulation or policy.
- Smooth returns help patience. Questionable sources of smoothness: unmarked illiquids, rare-loss strategies, fraud. Cash is smooth and fails slowly.
- Patience is not always right. Reasons to leave after losses: unintended exposures, errors, poor controls, changed objectives, persistently high fees.

## Anti-patterns
- **Three-to-five-year evaluation windows**: too short for statistics.
- **Line-item thinking**: cutting the most diversifying or most capital-efficient holding because it stands out.
- **Judging a strategy by your own entry date** instead of its full history.
- **Greedy consistency demands**: even well-rewarded factors have long bad stretches.

## Evidence on out-of-sample decay (note 14)
- McLean-Pontiff (2016): US factor returns fell after publication and stayed positive.
- Jacobs-Muller (2020): no such decay outside the US.
- Ilmanen et al. (2021a): about one-third lower returns outside the original sample. Decay was stronger *before* the sample than after it, which fits overfitting more than learning.
- Suhonen et al. (2017): larger decay for bank premia products.

## Key Takeaways
1. Impatience is "by far the more common problem than excessive patience".
2. Loss-averse investors who avoid rewarded risk raise the risk of missing long-term goals.
3. Aim overfitting worries at novel, complex, proprietary strategies. Aim crowding worries at the well-known ones.
4. "Discounting is an art, not a science."

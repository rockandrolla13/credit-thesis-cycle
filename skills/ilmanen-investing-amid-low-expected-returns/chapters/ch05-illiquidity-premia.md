# Chapter 5: Illiquidity Premia

## Core Idea
Investors treat illiquidity premia as nearly as reliable as the equity premium. The evidence shows "surprisingly modest" premia. The author's explanation: investors like smooth, unmarked returns and pay for them, which offsets the premium they should demand for lock-up.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three kinds of illiquidity premia**: (5.1) illiquid private assets, (5.2) less liquid public assets, (5.3) liquidity provision strategies. Only the third shows promising evidence, and it is capacity constrained.
- **Three facets of market liquidity**: tightness, depth, resilience.
- **Liquidity ranking**: cash, futures, bonds, stocks, hedge funds, private assets. Within groups: government before credit, large-cap before small-cap, developed before emerging.
- **Common traits of illiquid alternatives** (5.1): not traditional, often private, locked up, long-only in practice, smoothed returns with understated risk, poor data, no investable index so investors must be active, wide manager dispersion, hidden equity beta.
- **Smoothing service offsets the fair illiquidity premium** (Figure 5.3): `realized premium = fair illiquidity premium − what investors overpay for smoothing`.
  - Normative: investors "perhaps should demand, say, 3-5% excess return" for a decade lock-up (note 14, in line with Ang-Papanikolaou-Westerfield 2014).
  - Descriptive: "receiving as much is not a realistic expectation".
  - Asness (2019b) raises the chance of an illiquidity *discount* for private equity.
- **Slow beta** (note 17): reported private equity returns have a large equity beta to persistent market moves and a small beta to temporary ones. Smoothing helps in fast, V-shaped bear markets and not in protracted ones.
- **Real estate return rule**: real estate "may earn all (and more than all) of its long-run return from income". The net rental yield is "likely the ceiling on its long-run expected return, not the floor".
- **Private equity edge decomposition**: growth, leverage, valuation and yield advantages, less the fee disadvantage. Observers say the edge came from the valuation gap twenty years ago, leverage a decade ago, and growth in 2021. The author finds growth "hardest to sustain".
- **Public-market equivalent (PME)**: how much a fund vintage beat a public index over its life. PME 1.2 means 20% total outperformance, roughly 3% a year over six to seven years of capital use.

## Private credit and other private assets: what the book says
- Private credit is listed as one of the three largest illiquid classes. The author covers it "only briefly because the histories are too short, markets too small, and data too dubious for conclusive analysis."
- Size: private debt $0.9trn of $7.3trn investable private markets in mid-2020 (Box 5.1, Preqin). One third of the $7.3trn is dry powder.
- His rule for newer classes: extrapolate from the larger, older illiquid markets. The seesawing verdicts on private equity show why it is "premature to form judgments based on the historical performance of newer illiquid assets such as infrastructure and private debt" (note 10).
- Smaller private classes "may have even better-looking historical performance", over shorter histories, with IRR biases and a benign window of falling real yields and growing institutional demand.
- "My message that fair illiquidity premia get partly offset by our collective preference for smooth returns applies to all private assets."
- Dry powder is "one symptom of related absorption challenges". Markets are too opaque to show how far valuations rose or deal quality fell. "Anecdotal evidence and expert comments suggest caution is warranted."
- In listed credit, Richardson-Palhares (2019) find "little evidence that, after controlling for credit quality, less liquid bonds earn higher returns" (5.2).
- Required illiquidity premia may have been larger in the past and in distress (autumn 2008, spring 2020). "In more complacent times, required illiquidity premia may vanish."

## Worked Example: commercial real estate, backward and forward (Figure 5.4)
Historical NCREIF 1978-2020, extending Pagliari (2017):
1. Gross income yield 7.6%.
2. Less expenses: net income 5.1%.
3. Real rent growth -0.8% a year ("insufficient inflation passthrough").
4. Plus windfall from the cap rate halving, about 8% to 4%, and other effects.
5. Realized real return 5.4%, near average net income.

Prospective:
1. Recent gross income yield near 4%.
2. Deduct one third for costs: 2.7% net.
3. Assume zero real cash flow growth (kinder than history).
4. Assume zero valuation change.
5. Expected real return 2.7%. It "could easily be lower". It still beats Treasuries with negative expected real returns.

## Key Concepts
- **IRR**: internal rate of return. "Opaque and gameable".
- **Dry powder**: committed capital not yet called.
- **De-smoothing**: statistical correction using autocorrelation or lagged betas.
- **Liquidity provision**: taking the other side of investors with liquidity needs. Examples in 5.3: short-term reversal, merger, convertible and fixed-income arbitrage, index rebalancing, bond auctions, fallen angels, futures rolls, back-contract trades.
- **Calendar effects** (Box 5.2): same-month seasonality, announcement days, turn of the month, overnight versus intraday. Costs make most unusable for most investors.

## Mental Models
- "The word 'investable' makes a difference of one or two orders of magnitude." Investable real estate is near $1trn. Total global real estate may be $228trn. Total global wealth may be $400-500trn: half real estate, a quarter debt, almost a quarter public equity.
- Think of private returns as traditional risks (equity beta, rate risk) plus a small and unreliable illiquidity term, once smoothing is undone.
- Illiquidity premia "may be more clearly identified by asset performance during" flight-to-quality episodes than by long-run averages.
- Smoothness has real benefits: it improves organizational patience and lets institutions hold more equity risk. It is not free.

## Anti-patterns
- **Trusting IRR-based track records**.
- **Counting on top-quartile managers**: early evidence of alpha persistence "has later been overturned". Half of funds claim top quartile.
- **Extrapolating house price gains** that came from falling real yields.
- **Treating smoothed volatility as true risk** in optimization.
- **Co-investments as a free fee cut**: Ivashina-Lerner (2019) find no higher net returns.

## Key Takeaways
1. The longest clean test of a private illiquidity premium, REITs against direct property, shows none.
2. Private equity's valuation edge over public equity narrowed after institutions piled in from the mid-2000s. The return edge narrowed with it.
3. "It is not just bonds – also private assets have expected returns near record-low levels."
4. A modest positive expected return over public assets is still welcome. Those who can pick superior managers in advance "will fare much better".
5. Small-cap stocks offer no premium beyond beta except in micro-caps (Box 6.1).

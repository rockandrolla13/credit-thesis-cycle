# Chapter 8: Theories Explaining Long-run Return Sources

## Core Idea
A premium is more credible when there is a reason for it and a reason arbitrage does not remove it. Most premia have both risk-based and behavioral causes. Simple tests show that standard risk measures explain the cross-section of Sharpe ratios poorly.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Risk versus mispricing** (8.1): for most premia "both" matter. The equity premium and catastrophe-insurance-selling strategies are mostly risk. Trend and quality look behavioral because they pay in bad times.
  - Risk explanations tend to fit one asset class. Behavioral ones fit many. That pervasiveness makes the author lean behavioral for style premia.
- **Limits of arbitrage**: noise trader risk ("rich things can get richer"), trading costs, short-selling limits, leverage limits. Leverage limits bind most on low-volatility and highly diversified strategies. A diversified style portfolio may need 5-10x leverage for a 10% volatility target (note 3).
- **"No pain, no premium"**: investors capitulate after a few bad years. Those painful periods are what stop a premium from being arbitraged away.
- **Bad returns in bad times** (8.2): when an asset loses matters more than how much. Assets that lose in recessions and crises deserve high required returns. Safe havens can justify returns below cash.
  - Traits of bad times: high volatility, falling liquidity, forced deleveraging. GDP and unemployment lag.
- **Four generic candidate factors beyond equity beta** (8.3): tail risk, skewness preference, liquidity preference, leverage aversion.
- **Three areas of behavioral finance** (8.3): biased beliefs, non-standard preferences, cognitive limits. Limits of arbitrage sustain all three.
- **The author's single storyline for styles**: momentum and trend exploit initial underreaction to public news and later overreaction to past returns. Over-extrapolation then corrects and pays patient contrarians (the rubber-band effect).
- **Market ecology**: extrapolators (trend followers, stop-loss sellers, some volatility targeters) balance stabilizers (contrarians, central banks, rebalancers, target-date funds). If one side dominates, it underperforms next.
- **Adding-up constraint** (8.4, Box 8.1): "for every buyer there is a seller". A change in aggregate preference shows in valuations, not in net flows. The second valve is net issuance.
  - Rule: "if you worry about crowding, I suggest checking valuations as the best crowding indicator."
  - Di Bartolomeo's version: "A factor doesn't become crowded – what may become crowded is the type of investor holding that particular factor." Check whether holders share leverage or liquidity needs.

Holdings evidence (Asness et al., unfinished work): small non-13F investors are the other side of momentum. Hedge funds are one other side of defensive. No broad group leans strongly for or against value. Tilts persist over time.

## Worked Example: do risk measures explain Sharpe ratios? (Figures 8.2 and 8.3, 20 premia, 1990-2020)
1. Return against volatility and against equity beta: "only modestly positive".
2. Sharpe ratio against equity correlation: negative, -0.3.
3. Sharpe ratio against bad-times return (ten worst equity drawdowns): counterintuitive. Some safer premia had higher Sharpe ratios than equities.
4. Sharpe ratio against skewness: no relationship.
5. Sharpe ratio against an illiquidity proxy: "very mild positive".
6. Sharpe ratio against volatility within Treasuries, credit and equities: inverse. This one is robust.
The author's conclusion: the weak links "may imply opportunities for investors to improve risk-adjusted returns". Caveat he gives: 1990-2020 was disinflationary and friendly to risk.

## Key Concepts
- **Prospect theory**: gains and losses judged narrowly, loss aversion, overweighting of small probabilities.
- **Inelastic markets hypothesis** (Gabaix-Koijen 2021): flows move prices a lot.
- **Smart money**: hedge funds and short sellers. Their flows predicted returns. They are also the source of deleveraging risk, so hedging crowding means betting against them.
- **Theory mining**: the author's term for the surplus of explanations.
- **Macro-consistent**: only the cap-weighted portfolio can be held by everyone.

## Mental Models
- Ask of any premium: who loses, and why would they keep losing?
- Underreaction is the norm for mundane news ("frog in the pan"). Overreaction comes with salient news and lottery-like assets.
- Liquid assets show monthly and quarterly momentum and mild multiyear reversal (Figure 8.4). Smoothed real estate shows much higher autocorrelation.
- Since 2008 central bank purchases are net "issuance" in reverse. They may have richened assets with no change in private preferences.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading net flows as demand**: they sum to zero outside issuance.
- **Assuming known anomalies vanish**: limits of arbitrage and slow learning sustain them.
- **Hedging crowding risk cheaply**: it means going against historically profitable smart-money positions.

## Key Takeaways
1. A persistent, identifiable other side lowers the worry that a premium will disappear.
2. There was no richening trend in aggregate factor valuations in the 2010s, only in pockets such as defensive stocks.
3. Government bonds were safe havens from 2000 to 2020. Their returns were helped by falling yields.
4. The inverse link between Sharpe ratio and volatility inside an asset class fits leverage aversion and lottery preferences.

# Chapter 18: Concluding Remarks

## Core Idea
Low expected returns do not tell you to take more risk or less. The defensible answer is to keep a broadly similar portfolio, lower expectations and cut waste. Six principles of good investing close the book.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **More risk or less?** Taking more risk raises exposure when valuations are high. Moving to cash needs "huge confidence" in a timing call when negative real cash rates are the root problem. The author's answer: "hold a broadly similar portfolio as in normal times", accept that markets offer less, and invest efficiently.
- **Why everything is expensive at once**: discount rates reflect very low real riskless yields, "while most asset or factor premia on top of them are more or less ordinary".
- **Six principles of good investing**:
  1. Select return sources thoughtfully. Form beliefs you can stick with.
  2. Size risks appropriately, on risk-adjusted expected excess returns.
  3. Invest strategically and patiently. At most "sin a little" in timing.
  4. Diversify boldly. Build portfolios that survive most scenarios.
  5. Execute cost-effectively. Consider costs, fees and taxes.
  6. Govern well, inside the organization and with its agents.
- **Stable confidence**: resist the effect of recent success or failure on confidence. Luck dominates the short term and extreme outcomes. Lucky runs breed overconfidence and excess risk. Unlucky runs breed capitulation.

## The author's dated view (mid-2021)
| View | Status in the book |
|---|---|
| For years he leaned to slow pain. "Writing this in mid-2021, the fast-pain scenario is becoming more plausible." | Unresolved |
| "We face a bubbly situation where high asset valuations are supported by central bank policies, despite rising inflation." De-anchored inflation expectations "could force central banks to make hard choices for the first time in ages". | Unresolved |
| Relative value opportunities he names: the gap between value and growth stocks, and between US and other equity markets | Unresolved |

He adds that a generation or two of investors "do not remember inflation, rising bond yields, or persistent equity bear markets". Their main fear is missing out.

## Key Concepts
- **Slow pain / fast pain**: see Ch 2.
- **Debiasing**: adjusting historical averages when the sample ends at extreme valuations. He notes the book's sample ends with near record rich equities, low yields and an extreme value-growth spread.
- **Equanimity**: "You are never as good as your best year, and never as bad as your worst year."

## Mental Models
- Control the ex-ante process. Accept luck in ex-post outcomes.
- Expertise and effort give a stable anchor for confidence. Results do not.
- A strategy is good "only if you can stick with it".

## Anti-patterns
- **Adding risk without "open eyes"**.
- **Going to cash on a valuation view**.
- **Demanding constant validation from realized success**.
- **Excessive humility**: the author does not endorse it either.

## Key Takeaways
1. The prescriptions are meant to be timeless. Low returns make them matter more.
2. The low-return outcome is the author's base case for "the next decade or longer". The path is unknown.
3. Acronyms the book uses throughout: ARP, BAB, BRP, CAEY, CAPE, DB, DC, FLAM, QMJ, SR. See glossary.md.

# Chapter 3: Major Investor Types and Their Responses to This Challenge

## Core Idea
Every investor saves to meet a liability or spending plan. High market returns let them contribute little. Low returns break that arithmetic, and most institutions respond by adding risk, which has long historical precedent and is "hardly riskless".

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three stylized investor types** (3.1): defined-benefit (DB) pension plan, individual defined-contribution (DC) saver, university endowment.
  - DB: explicit, legally binding liability. Sponsor bears investment and longevity risk.
  - DC: the liability is planned consumption. The saver bears both risks and decides how much to save.
  - Endowment: no binding liability. Typical rule spends 5% of the three-year moving average value.
- **Funding ratio**: FR = A/L. Surplus = A - L. A lower liability discount rate raises L and lowers FR.
- **Discount rate regime drives behaviour**: US corporate DB plans discount at market bond yields (4% or less). US public DB plans discount at the expected return on assets (7-8%). Market-yield discounting pushes toward liability-driven investing. Expected-return discounting lets underfunded plans take more risk.
- **Two DB glide paths** (Figure 3.2):
  - US corporate: de-risk "when can afford it". Example given: 65% equities at 80% FR, 50% bonds at 90% FR, no equities near 100% FR.
  - Dutch and Danish: start overfunded and must cut equities if FR threatens to fall below 100%.
- **DC glide path** (Figure 3.1): about 90% equities early, near 50% at retirement, falling to about 30% in a "through" design. Rationale: human capital is bond-like for most people, and the young can adjust their work.
- **Three risk measures an institution may weigh**: total portfolio volatility, surplus volatility against the liability, tracking error against peers.
- **Taxonomy of investment models** (Figure 3.7): 60/40 Model (liquid, external), Norway Model (liquid, internal, low cost), Yale or Endowment Model (illiquid, external), Canada Model (illiquid, internal), and liability-driven investing.
- **Three institutional answers to low expected returns** (Figure 3.11):
  | Answer | Motivation | Challenge the author names |
  |---|---|---|
  | More equities | Highest-conviction long-run premium | Concentration. Already the dominant risk. Not cheap. |
  | Add illiquid / private assets | Endowment Model beliefs. High past returns. Perceived illiquidity premium. | Illiquidity. Much equity exposure. Historically rich. "Overrated illiquidity premia". |
  | Add factor tilts and alternative risk premia | Evidence on several rewarded factors. Diversification. | Leverage and other tools are needed to hit return targets. |

## Key Concepts
- **Liability-driven investing (LDI)**: matching asset duration or cash flows to liabilities. Often an end-game before closing a plan.
- **Global market portfolio** (Box 3.1): about $106trn listed equities at end-2020 and about $120trn bonds. Investable private assets $7.3trn in mid-2020 per Preqin: buyouts $2.3trn, and about $1trn each for venture, growth/other PE, private credit, real estate, and infrastructure/natural resources. Doeswijk-Lam-Swinkels count $160trn investable at end-2020.
- **Delegation**: PwC (2020) estimates end-investor assets at $258trn, of which $112trn (43%) is delegated. $14trn goes to alternative managers.
- **Sequencing risk**: a bad market outcome when the savings pot is largest.

## Worked Example: the DC saver's required saving rate (3.3, Figure 3.9)
1. Target: 75% replacement of final salary. Savings must cover 45%. The other 30% comes from social security or elsewhere (note 21).
2. At a 5.5% expected real return on a balanced portfolio, the median outcome needs saving 8% of salary a year.
3. At 3.5% real, the required rate is 15%.
4. For better than 50/50 odds, the rate "can easily rise to 20%".
5. Figure 3.9 bars across 6.5%, 5.5%, 4.5%, 3.5%, 2.5% real: 6%, 8%, 11%, 15%, 19%.
The author's summary: a 2% fall in expected return almost doubles the saving rate. Source cited: Ilmanen-Rauseo-Truax (2016).

## History the chapter records (3.2)
- Two post-WWII trends: bonds to equities, then liquid to illiquid alternatives. Large US endowments led both.
- US households held over 90% of US equities directly around 1950. By 2020 mutual funds and households each held just over 30%.
- Large endowments held over 50% in alternatives from the late 2000s, 54% in 2020. Yale approached 80%.
- Global pension fund alternatives rose from 5% to 26% between 1995 and 2020.
- US public DB alternatives recently reached about 25%, corporate DB about 20%. Both were 60/30/10 equity/fixed income/alternatives in 2001-2007.
- Origin of 60/40: "unclear". Marty Leibowitz's suggestion: 9% average return and 9% volatility since WWII with few drawdowns over 20%.

## Funding evidence (3.3)
- US corporate DB average FR: above 100% in 2007, 105% to 79% during 2008, 83% average in the 2010s.
- US public DB average FR: 102% in 2001, 76% in 2010, 72% in 2020, even with high discount rates.
- Public plan assumed returns fell only from 8¼% to 7¼% while yield-based 60/40 expected returns fell much faster (Figure 3.10).

## Mental Models
- Test every portfolio against two stress scenarios (note 20). Asset-liability investors fear 2008: assets down, liabilities up. Asset-only investors fear risky assets down with yields up.
- Declining public-equity weights can hide equity beta that moved into private equity, hedge funds and lower-rated credit.
- "All investors cannot simultaneously increase their equity exposures."

## Anti-patterns
- **Overpromising and under-contributing**: easy when the bill comes later.
- **Extrapolating the 1990s** into return assumptions.
- **A fixed 4% real withdrawal rule** when returns are sustainably lower.
- **A 5% spending rule** when markets offer nearer 3% real. US foundations must spend 5% to keep tax-exempt status.

## Historical reach-for-yield precedents (3.4)
- Dutch investors in the 1600s-1700s moved to real estate as government yields fell.
- UK life assurers in the 1850s moved from consols to railway and dock debentures for 50-150bp more yield with "material default risk".
- Britain 1930-1945: the same pattern, per Turnbull (2017).

## Key Takeaways
1. Corporate DB plans already took the hit through liabilities. DC savers and endowments have most of the pain ahead.
2. The author calls all three institutional answers evidence-based. He ranks belief in the illiquidity premium lowest.
3. Collective saving more could push valuations higher and worsen the problem.
4. Banks estimate quant allocations doubled to nearly 10% in the 2010s.

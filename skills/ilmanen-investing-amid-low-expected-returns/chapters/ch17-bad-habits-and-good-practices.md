# Chapter 17: Bad Habits and Good Practices

## Core Idea
Good investing needs good investors. The "premier bad habit" is multiyear return chasing: buying what did well over three to five years and selling what did badly, at the horizon where returns tend to reverse.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Momentum investors at reversal horizons** (17.1): markets show continuation up to about one year and mild mean reversion over several years. Reallocation decisions are usually made on three-to-five-year results, the wrong horizon.
- **Four responses the author offers**:
  1. Spend more time evaluating a strategy or manager before investing. Pick only those you can stay with.
  2. Use criteria beyond past performance: people, philosophy, process. Know the reasonable range of outcomes.
  3. When a manager disappoints, look at more than return.
  4. Check correlations to common factors and to other managers to see whether the manager did what the mandate implied.
- **Table 17.1: behavioral forces and what they cause**
  | Underlying force | Resulting bad habit or opportunity |
  |---|---|
  | Impatience, hyperbolic discounting | Undersaving. Multiyear return chasing, fire-and-hire |
  | Over-extrapolation of past growth and returns | Momentum, trend and value opportunities |
  | Anchoring, conservatism | Underreaction, so momentum and trend |
  | Limited attention and cognitive processing | Underreaction. Narrow framing |
  | Narrow framing | Underdiversification: home bias, concentrated risks |
  | Overweighting low-probability events | Skewness and lottery preferences, so BAB and similar |
  | Overconfidence | Overtrading |
  | Moods, emotions, sentiment | Herding, bubbles |
  Row pairing is rebuilt from a run-together table and the prose.
- **Three underdiversification channels**: home bias (with ambiguity aversion), equity concentration (with leverage aversion), single-stock concentration (with overconfidence).
- **Governance failures** (17.2): misaligned incentives, a board that micromanages manager selection or market views instead of setting policy, and mismatches between responsibility, authority and accountability.

## Evidence
| Finding | Source named |
|---|---|
| US plan sponsors fire recent laggards and hire recent winners. Fired managers then mildly outperform their replacements (1996-2003) | Goyal-Wahal (2008), Figure 17.1 |
| Mutual funds that won over three years lag the losers over the next three | Cornell-Hsu-Nanigian (2017) |
| Dollar-weighted US stock returns were 1.3% below buy-and-hold, 1926-2002. NASDAQ: 5.3% below, 1973-2002 | Dichev (2007), note 2 |
| Products receiving institutional contributions later underperform those with withdrawals over one to five years. The gap is modest | Stewart et al. (2009), note 3 |
| US stock deciles sorted on past 2-12 month return show momentum. Sorted on past 13-60 month return they show weaker reversal (1931-2018) | Figure 17.2 |

The author prefers direct flow evidence over the money-weighted gap. He lists the gap's flaws in note 2: part reflects rising inflows over time with falling returns, it is large even for index funds, and reinvestment assumptions are infeasible.

## Key Concepts
- **Prospect theory features**: loss aversion, narrow framing, probability weighting.
- **Get-evenitis**: risk seeking after losses.
- **FOMO**: fear of missing out.
- **Hedonic treadmill**: expectations rise with income.
- **Commitment device**: Odysseus tied to the mast.

## Mental Models
- "A good strategy is one you can stick with."
- "Inconsistently believing in right things might turn out even worse than consistently believing in wrong things."
- Any behavioral pattern is a habit to fix in yourself and an opportunity created by others.
- Narrow framing and probability weighting matter more for investing than loss aversion does.
- Underreaction is stronger for bread-and-butter news than for salient news.
- Housel: "Save like a pessimist, invest like an optimist."

## Anti-patterns
- **Three-to-five-year evaluation cycles** that trigger firing at the point of likely reversal.
- **Outcome-only judgment** of a manager or strategy.
- **Story-driven decisions** without calibration against statistical evidence.
- **Overconfident entrepreneurs** who expect double-digit returns from mediocre managers after selling a business (note 8).

## Key Takeaways
1. Multi-month momentum chasing can pay. Multiyear chasing costs.
2. Systematic investing "has a clear edge" on discipline against moods.
3. Save more in a low-return world. Some risk taking is necessary when low cash rates are the cause.
4. The author admits the remedies are hard: "Nobody said it would be easy."

# Chapter 11: Diversification – Its Power and Its Dark Sides

## Core Idea
Breadth across lowly correlated return sources can multiply a Sharpe ratio. Most portfolios do not use it because full use needs leverage, shorting and a tolerance for looking different. "Many investors talk diversification but walk concentration."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three diversification cases** (11.2):
  1. Global equity versus home bias. Weak breadth gain because equity markets are highly correlated. Worse for investors in small countries.
  2. Risk parity versus 60/40. A 60/40 portfolio is roughly 90/10 in risk. Equal risk in stocks, bonds and commodities with SR 0.3 each and zero correlation gives SR 0.5 (0.3 × √3).
  3. Alternative risk premia. Four styles in one asset class double the per-style SR from 0.2 to 0.4. Four asset classes double it again to 0.8.
- **Nine-premia ideal**: equal risk in the nine premia of Figure 1.2, levered to the investor's risk tolerance. The author says it is "not realistic" for a whole portfolio. Reaching 10% portfolio volatility would need each sleeve at 25-30% volatility and gross positions up to ten times assets (note 8). Move toward it "as far as you sustainably can".
- **Risk allocation alongside notional allocation** (note 3): track both. Volatility shares are acceptable. Risk contributions with correlations are better.
- **Ten craft features for systematic strategies** ("craftsmanship alpha"):
  1. Strategic risk diversification over tactical timing.
  2. Long/short, or mildly relaxed constraints.
  3. Apply the edge in many markets.
  4. Several signals per factor.
  5. Continuous signal-to-position mapping, such as rank weights.
  6. Integrate factor signals before trading. Do not mix separate factor portfolios.
  7. Hedge industry or sector exposures.
  8. Target equal volatility across strategies and over time.
  9. Spread model rebalancing dates to cut "rebalancing timing luck".
  10. Spread trade execution over time.
- **Rebalancing** (Box 11.1): return to target weights to hold risk targets. It is "defensively contrarian" and so "short structural changes".
  - The author's preferred rule: "often-but-little", such as quarterly rebalancing one quarter of the way to target. It roughly equals a full annual rebalance, averages the timing, trades smaller sizes and lets momentum play out.
- **Three dark sides** (11.3): leverage, unconventionality ("maverick risk"), and lack of stories.

## Key Concepts
- **Diworsification**: the claim that diversification dilutes best ideas. The author mostly reads concentration as overconfidence.
- **Maverick risk**: losing alone because the portfolio differs from peers.
- **130/30**: a partial relaxation of the long-only constraint. Market risk still dominates.
- **Diversification return / rebalancing bonus**: the commodity basket's 3% geometric edge over its average member.
- **Dragon risk**: unknown risk in alternative asset classes (used in Ch 12).

## Mental Models
- Test your belief in diversification by asking whether you would use leverage to get it.
- If stocks carry ten times the risk share of other assets, their Sharpe ratio would need to be about ten times higher for that to be optimal. "It is not."
- Correlations within equities rise in sharp falls. International diversification still works over persistent declines, because business cycles and bubbles differ (Japan 1990, US 2000, China 2007).
- The Bessembinder (2018) result, that 4% of stocks made all net US wealth creation since 1926, argues for holding everything, not for picking winners.
- Stories feed base-rate neglect and hindsight bias. Being short on stories may be a strength.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging diversification after a decade when it lost**, as with non-US assets after the 2010s.
- **Concentrated bets as proof of conviction**.
- **Monthly full rebalancing**: tight tracking, high cost, and it cuts momentum short.
- **Mixing single-factor portfolios** instead of integrating signals.

## Key Takeaways
1. Breadth is the cheaper route to a higher Sharpe ratio than skill.
2. The Sharpe gain is in the denominator. Turning it into return needs leverage.
3. Complement diversification with tail hedges, liquidity and leverage limits, and drawdown rules (Ch 13).
4. Leverage limits, unconventionality and abstraction may be why the diversified style Sharpe ratio has lasted, "at least on paper".

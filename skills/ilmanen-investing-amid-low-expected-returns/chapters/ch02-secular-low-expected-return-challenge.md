# Chapter 2: The Secular Low Expected Return Challenge

## Core Idea
A record-low riskless rate makes every asset look expensive at once. Past returns were inflated by the fall in discount rates, so they overstate what investors expected then and what they can expect now.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Common discount rate** (2.1): any long-only asset is priced as expected cash flows divided by a discount rate. That rate is a riskless rate plus risk premia. When the common riskless part is very low, "everything looks expensive". The author's reading: "a world of low expected total returns rather than a world of compressed risk premia."
- **Discount rate effect / windfall gains** (2.2): a fall in an asset's own yield raises its realized return and lowers its prospective return.
  - How (notes 2 and 3): regress monthly excess returns on contemporaneous changes in the asset's yield. The slope is the empirical duration. Slope times net yield change, spread over the sample years, is the annual windfall. The regression intercept is the "neutral" excess return.
  - Why estimate and not assume 1-for-1: for many long/short factors the link between valuation change and return is weak.
- **Rearview-mirror expectations**: subjective expectations rise after windfall gains, just when objective prospective returns fall (the author cites Greenwood-Shleifer 2014).
- **Slow pain versus fast pain**: low coupons and dividends for decades with no more richening, or mean reversion with speedy capital losses and fairer returns after.
- **Horizon rule**: the yield-based approach fits a ten-year horizon best. For longer horizons starting yields matter less. For shorter horizons momentum and macro forces matter more.
- **Total versus excess**: low cash rates mean expected total returns are low while expected excess returns over cash "may not be particularly low". If real cash rates normalize by about 2030, the same equity total return implies about 2% less equity premium over cash.
- **Saving glut explanations for low real yields** (2.3): reserve accumulation, pension saving in ageing societies, and saving by the rich (note 10 cites Mian-Straub-Sufi 2020). The first two favour safe assets, so they point to low safe yields and not to compressed risk premia.

## Worked Example: decomposing 1981-2020 returns (Figure 2.1)
| | US 10-year Treasury | US equities (S&P 500) |
|---|---|---|
| Average excess return over cash | 3.3% | 7.8% |
| Own-yield change | 12.4% to 0.9% | CAEY 10.3% to 2.9% |
| Regression slope (empirical duration) | 5.3 | 14.6 |
| Correlation of return with yield change | about -0.95 | about -0.9 |
| Annual windfall gain | 1.5% | 2.7% |
| Neutral excess return (intercept) | 1.8% | 5.1% |

Average T-bill rate in the period: 4.2%. The author's forward assumption: T-bill rates near 1% for the next decade, no further windfall, and a slightly lower normal excess return. He does not assume mean-reverting losses. Equities gained more than bonds from a smaller yield fall because of their longer "duration".

## Key Concepts
- **CAEY**: cyclically adjusted earnings yield, the inverse of the Shiller CAPE.
- **60/40 expected real return**: below 2% in 2021, against about 5% typical of the 20th century (Figure 2.2).
- **Survey expectations** (Figure 2.3): Survey of Professional Forecasters next-decade nominal equity return fell from 10% to 5% between 1992 and 2021. Expected bill return fell from 5% to 1.8%. Graham-Harvey CFO equity forecasts fell from 10% to 6% since 2000 with an equity-bond premium near 4%.
- **Wet logs economies**: economies where any tightening can cause recession or crashes.
- **Financial repression**: capped yields while inflation rises, as after WWII.

## Mental Models
- Use the bond logic on every asset: high starting yield plus repricing gains gives high realized return and lower return after.
- Classic theory equates real yields and real growth. The long record shows the opposite: near-zero growth with double-digit yields before the Industrial Revolution, then about 2% growth with far lower yields (2.3, Figure 2.4).
- Decades partly mean-revert (2.4). Double-digit decades for the S&P 500 (1920s, 1950s, 1990s) were followed by weak ones. The weakest decades (1930s, 1970s, 2000s) were followed by strong ones. The pattern is not reliable: the 1940s-50s were both strong and the 1960s-70s both weak. Inflation cycles in bond and commodity returns last longer.

## Anti-patterns
- **Extrapolating the last four decades**: "doubly misleading". Realized returns beat expected returns, and starting yields are now lower.
- **Thinking the problem is only in bonds**.
- **Relying on compounding**: it "cannot help as much" with persistent low returns.

## Key Takeaways
1. Strip the valuation windfall out of any historical average before using it as an expected return.
2. Low riskless rates explain most of the richening. Many measures of the equity premium and term premium over cash show no similar downtrend.
3. The pain may fall hardest on savers if inflation erodes the windfall while policy holds short rates down.
4. In the 2010s almost any diversification away from US 60/40 hurt. The author says not to expect that every decade.

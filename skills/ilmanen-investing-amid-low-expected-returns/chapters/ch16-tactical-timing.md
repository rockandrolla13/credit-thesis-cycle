# Chapter 16: Tactical Timing on Medium-term Expected Returns

## Core Idea
Valuation charts make contrarian market timing look reliable. A tradeable, out-of-sample version barely beats buy-and-hold over 120 years and not at all in recent decades. The rule is "sin a little": time modestly, with several signals. Strategic diversification adds far more.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"Market Timing: Sin a Little"** (Asness-Ilmanen-Maloney 2017, after Samuelson): zero timing may not be right. Overconfident aggressive timing "is almost certainly wrong".
- **Three seductive visuals** (16.1): the time series of CAEY against next-decade return, the scatter plot, and quintile bars. Each overstates what a trader could have earned.
- **In-sample versus out-of-sample (OOS) signal**: in-sample judges today's valuation against the full history, including the future. The OOS signal ranks CAEY against the previous 60 years and scales exposure between 0.5 and 1.5.
- **A long horizon is a chain of short ones**: a 10-year view traded with 1/120th of wealth each month equals trading monthly on the average of the past decade's CAEY.
- **Why contrarian timing underwhelms**:
  1. "Early equals wrong." Contrarian signals fight short-term momentum. Blindly delaying action 6-12 months, or waiting for trend confirmation, improves results.
  2. Long-run trends, meaning structural change. Valuations richened from 1950 to 2000. The timer stayed underinvested in the equity premium.
  3. The signal is too coarse. A timer who exits in the bottom CAEY quintile left in the early 1990s. The signal was bearish "almost all the time since the late 1980s".
- **Econometric problems with long-horizon regressions** (Boudoukh-Israel-Richardson): few independent observations (ten 10-year returns a century). Overlapping data adds little. Newey-West corrections understate the problem. Persistent price-scaled predictors bias the slope upward (Stambaugh 1999). So "priors matter a lot".
- **Low breadth of timing** (note 7): one market, one signal, and a slow signal. Improve breadth with more signals, faster signals, and more markets, directional and cross-sectional.
- **Signal menu for multi-month timing** (16.2): value, momentum or trend, yield curve slope. Credit growth and the variance risk premium are named as candidates from the literature.

## Worked Example: reconciling the pretty charts with the poor strategy
1. Start with Figure 16.3: sorted by CAEY quintile, cheap markets earned more, in sample and out of sample.
2. Build the strategy honestly: OOS signal, monthly trading, exposure 0.5 to 1.5.
3. Result (Figure 16.4): mild outperformance over 120 years, "but just not in our lifetime".
4. The author thought it was "a spreadsheet error".
5. Explanation: the quintile chart hides that the signal sat in the rich bucket for decades. The timer was early in the 1990s, missed a near-decade rally, and stayed underinvested.
6. Variants tried: longer horizon, trade only at extremes. None changed the picture.
7. Real managers would have done worse, since few could hold a bearish stance that long.
The author's own case: he avoided stocks in the 1990s on dividend yield signals. Greenspan's "irrational exuberance" warning came three and a half years before the top.

## Timing other things (16.2)
- **Duration**: rich bonds on low real yields "have become ever richer".
- **Styles**: a contrarian would have underweighted defensive before its superb decade and overweighted value well before 2020.
- **Credit**: unpublished work on credit-Treasury spread timing "disappointed, due to too early bullish positioning in 2001 and 2008" (note 9). The author had thought credit more amenable because it is cyclical with fewer structural changes.
- **Macro regime timing**: more reliable for asset classes than for styles (Ch 12).

## Key Concepts
- **Lookahead bias**: using information not available at the time.
- **Capital market assumptions**: 5-10 year forecasts. Yield-based inputs remain "the most useful" for these, despite the cautions.
- **Equity share** (note 3): equities as a share of a broad market portfolio. One study finds it predicts better than CAPE.
- **Rebalancing timing luck**: arbitrary start dates. Average across dates.

## Mental Models
- A better statistical fit at long horizons does not mean timing works better at long horizons. It is easier to fit smooth 10-year averages.
- Low yields can justify high equity valuations and still mean low expected return through carry. If yields rise, the same argument pressures valuations.
- "There are no old market timers in the Forbes billionaires list."
- Market timing invites overfitting more than other systematic strategies: one series, and hindsight makes it easy to find a signal that avoided 2008.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading quintile bar charts as strategy returns**.
- **Acting on a rich valuation signal alone**.
- **Adjusting CAPE until it justifies today's price**: shorter history, more generous earnings, margins, intangibles, lower yields. The author lists these as fitting risks.
- **Expecting factor timing to rescue a factor portfolio**.

## Key Takeaways
1. Combine value with momentum in timing. Even then, whipsaws hurt.
2. Yield-based estimates remain the right input for decade-ahead return assumptions. They are weak timing tools.
3. Diversifying across styles and assets raised the Sharpe ratio almost fourfold. Timing styles raised it by under a tenth.
4. No timing result was "highly robust across subperiods and asset classes".
5. The author says his 2011 book left a too-positive impression and this chapter is partly a correction.

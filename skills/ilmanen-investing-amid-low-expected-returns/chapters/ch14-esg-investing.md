# Chapter 14: ESG Investing

## Core Idea
ESG preferences change prices. Shunned assets fall in a transition and then offer a higher expected return. So ESG can win during the transition and should cost a little in the long run. The evidence is mixed, except that ESG tilts have reduced risk and governance signals have helped returns. The author notes the chapter leans on AQR colleagues' work.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **ESG framework** (Figure 14.1, following UN PRI Principles 1 and 2):
  - Responsible asset selection: screening (norms-based, static, thematic) and ESG integration (valuation, risk).
  - Responsible ownership: voting, engagement, activism, direct management.
- **Screen versus tilt**: a screen removes the worst names and cuts breadth. A tilt, or a portfolio-level ESG constraint, reaches the goal "with less investment distortion". Combine a tilt with a few screens to avoid the worst offenders.
- **Two views on returns** (14.2):
  - Win-win: we are early in a transition. Green firms keep re-rating and brown firms keep falling.
  - Textbook: a constraint cannot help. If ESG were a pure win, you would hold the same portfolio without the preference.
  - Reconciliation: they differ on how long the transition lasts and which inning we are in.
- **Sin premium**: once shunned assets are cheap enough, they offer a higher expected return. The same logic holds if ESG proxies for real risks such as climate risk. This is the discount rate effect of Ch 2.
- **Asness (2017) argument** (note 4): to cut sinful activity, ESG investors must raise the sinners' discount rate. That discount rate is the expected return of investors who hold them. Virtue has a price.
- **ESG-Sharpe ratio frontier** (Figure 14.2, Pedersen-Fitzgibbons-Pomorski 2020): for each ESG score, the best achievable Sharpe ratio. It is hump-shaped. The peak is the ESG-agnostic tangency portfolio. Left of the peak there is no trade-off. Right of it, each gain in ESG score costs Sharpe ratio.
- **Carbon scopes** (note 7): Scope 1 own operations. Scope 2 purchased energy. Scope 3 the value chain, most relevant and hardest to measure.
- **Exclusion paradox** (14.3): emissions sit in a few industries and firms. Excluding them cuts portfolio emissions cheaply and removes the vote and the engagement channel.

## Key Concepts
- **Greenwashing**: appearance without substance.
- **Green versus brown**: the author's shorthand for more versus less responsible firms.
- **UN PRI**: over 3,000 signatories by 2020 with over $100trn in assets.
- **ESG themes** (Table 14.1): E covers climate, resource use, water, waste, policy. S covers community, employees, consumers, product safety, health. G covers board structure, shareowner rights, transparency, accounting, executive pay.

## Evidence the chapter cites
| Finding | Source named |
|---|---|
| Governance signals relate positively to returns, perhaps through overlap with quality | Gompers-Ishii-Metrick (2003). Later studies less robust |
| High accruals predict low returns | Sloan (1996) |
| Employee satisfaction relates to higher returns | Green et al. (2019) |
| Environmental and climate signals | "at best mixed" |
| Excluding sin stocks was costly | Hong-Kacperczyk (2009) |
| Activist investors performed well | Brav et al. (2008), Dimson-Karakis-Li (2015) |
| Composite ESG scores reduced risk. Return effect unclear | Dimson-Marsh-Staunton (2020), Dunn-Fitzgibbons-Pomorski (2018) |
| Provider scores correlate weakly | Berg-Kölbel-Rigobon (2020) |

## Mental Models
- Ask which inning the repricing is in before assuming green outperformance continues.
- Realized ESG outperformance during a re-rating overstates future return, for the same reason as Figure 2.1.
- For an aggressive carbon goal, underweighting is not enough because almost every firm emits. Short the worst offenders or trade offsets.
- "I rarely see demonstrators on the street waving flags on G." The one clear win-win is the least exciting.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming no trade-off ever**.
- **Trusting short, backfilled, self-reported ESG data**.
- **Searching for positive ESG-return links**: selection bias applies here too.
- **Net-zero pledges** made without checking what is feasible.
- **Broad screens** that leave a concentrated portfolio and no guarantee of lower emissions than the benchmark.

## Key Takeaways
1. Long-run logic says ESG-motivated investors accept somewhat lower returns and get non-financial utility in exchange.
2. ESG sinners "may lag in a transition phase".
3. ESG can strengthen patience: holders stay with value-aligned strategies through weak performance (Ch 9).
4. The author predicts in Ch 2 that the rise of ESG is the 2010s trend most certain to continue.

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. One discount rate makes everything expensive (Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 18)
Every long-only asset is priced as expected cash flows over a riskless rate plus premia. A record-low riskless rate lowers expected total returns everywhere. The author reads 2021 as "low expected total returns rather than ... compressed risk premia". The outcome is **slow pain** (low income for decades) or **fast pain** (repricing). In mid-2021 he says fast pain "is becoming more plausible".

### 2. Discount rate effect and windfall gains (Ch 2, Ch 10)
A fall in an asset's own yield raises realized return and lowers expected return. To remove it, regress returns on contemporaneous yield changes. The slope is empirical duration and the intercept is the neutral premium.
- US 10-year Treasury 1981-2020: 3.3% excess return, of which 1.5% windfall. Neutral 1.8%.
- US equities 1981-2020: 7.8% excess return, of which 2.7% windfall. Neutral 5.1%.
Extrapolating past returns is "doubly misleading": the windfall inflated them and starting yields are now lower.

### 3. Pyramid of return sources and the four equations (Ch 4, Ch 10)
Cash, then market premia (equity, term, credit, commodity), then alternative risk premia, then alpha. Illiquids sit at the side.
1. Return = income + growth + valuation change. Expected capital gain is not always positive: it is negative for credit, volatility selling and emerging currency carry.
2. Expected return = riskless rate + beta × market premium. Premia are pay for "bad returns in bad times".
3. Maximize expected return − risk aversion × variance.
4. `IR = IC × √N × TC`. Breadth is easier to add than skill.

### 4. Yield-based expected returns (Ch 4, Ch 16)
Real return ≈ income yield + real growth, with zero assumed valuation change. Equities: dividend yield + 1.5%, or CAEY. Mid-2021 US equities: about 3.2% real, 5.5% nominal. 60/40: below 2% real against about 5% in the 20th century. Use these for 5-10 year assumptions. They are poor timing tools.

### 5. Credit premium (Ch 4)
Defined as excess return over duration-matched government bonds.
- IG premium by sample: near 1.5% (1936-2014), 1.0% (1973-2020), 0.7% (1989-2020).
- Spread capture 1989-2020: IG earned 75bp of a 132bp average OAS (56%). HY earned about 2.8% of 5.2% (54%).
- Default break-even: 15-25bp IG, 200-250bp HY. The rest of IG leakage is forced selling of **fallen angels**, which earned double the HY index excess return (4.6% versus 2.3%, 1997-2020).
- Short-dated credit has the best risk-adjusted return (IR 0.45 for 1-3 year against 0.05 beyond 25 years). It needs leverage.
- Credit beats a matched equity/Treasury mix because it is "effectively short volatility" and benefits from leverage aversion. Illiquidity does not explain it.
- HY spreads anticipate defaults. In 2021 "credit spreads are well below historical averages".

### 6. Illiquidity premia are overrated (Ch 5)
`Realized premium = fair illiquidity premium − what investors pay for smoothed returns.` A 3-5% premium for a decade lock-up is what investors should demand and "not a realistic expectation". Listed REITs beat direct property 7% to 4% over cash (1978-2020). Buyouts roughly match small-cap value after 5-6% fees, and vintages since 2006 struggled to beat public equity. Private credit: histories "too short, markets too small, and data too dubious". In complacent times "required illiquidity premia may vanish".

### 7. Four styles, diversified (Ch 6, Ch 11)
Value, momentum/trend, carry, defensive. Each is persistent, pervasive and robust. Single style in one asset class: SR about 0.4. Multi-style: 0.8. Multi-style and multi-asset: about 1.5, gross. "Contrarian strategies are inherently short a structural change." Carry reflects the beta of what it buys. Full use needs leverage, shorting and tolerance for looking different.

### 8. Who is on the other side (Ch 8)
Aggregate preference shifts show in valuations, not flows. Check valuations to detect crowding. "A factor doesn't become crowded – what may become crowded is the type of investor holding" it.

### 9. Patience and discounting (Ch 9, Ch 17)
An SR 0.4 strategy underperforms over three years 24% of the time. Halve a gross backtest Sharpe ratio as a start. The premier bad habit is multiyear return chasing: investors "act like momentum investors at reversal horizons".

### 10. Survival, tail hedges, timing (Ch 13, Ch 16)
Diversify first, then hedge. Trend (SR 0.84, 1985-2020) protected in slow bear markets. Monthly index puts lost 6.4% a year at the same volatility. Contrarian equity timing had SR 0.36-0.39 against 0.38 for buy-and-hold: "sin a little".

## Use for Credit and Macro Questions

- **Credit spreads**: a method to turn a spread into an expected excess return (default break-even, downgrade bias, fallen-angel leakage, spread capture), long-run credit premium estimates, and the rule that carry overstates return. It also finds that spread-based credit timing was too early in 2001 and 2008 (ch04, ch10, ch16).
- **Debt-financed investment booms**: nothing direct on issuance or supply. Indirect only: the adding-up constraint and net issuance as a valve (ch08), reach-for-yield precedents (ch03), and leveraged loan and private credit market sizes for 2020 (ch04, ch05).
- **Inflation and Fed regimes**: a three-way yield decomposition, drivers of the bond risk premium, the stock-bond correlation rule, asset inflation sensitivities, and Sharpe ratios in four growth-inflation environments (ch04, ch12). It is not a central-bank reaction-function source.
- **Issuer screening**: nothing at issuer level. Only factor-level findings: value-based issuer selection and defensive tilts work in corporate bonds (ch06).
- **Private credit and illiquids**: the smoothing argument and the evidence against a reliable illiquidity premium (ch05).

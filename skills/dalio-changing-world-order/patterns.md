# Patterns: The Changing World Order (Dalio, 2020 serialized draft)

All patterns are the author's. Thresholds are his.

## Archetype comparison
**When to use**: an event with no precedent in your lifetime. (Intro)
**How**: (1) Collect past cases of the same type. (2) Average them into an archetype. (3) Compare the present case and explain the differences. (4) Write if/then rules. (5) Track events against the template and correct when they deviate.
**Trade-offs**: imprecise by design. The author calls his own outlook low-confidence. Timing is "less disputable" in fact than in date.

## Eight-measure power ladder
**When to use**: ranking a country's position in its Big Cycle. (Ch 1)
**How**: score education, competitiveness, technology, output, trade share, military, financial center, reserve currency. Read them in order. Education leads. Reserve status lags.
**Trade-offs**: the indices are "broadly indicative". Average and total differ for large populations (Ch 6).

## Income statement and balance sheet test
**When to use**: any person, firm, city, state or country. (Ch 2)
**How**: income against expenses plus debt service. Assets against liabilities, including pensions and healthcare. Stress it: income stops and assets halve. Count how long savings last and how liquid they are.
**Trade-offs**: needs non-debt obligations, which are often off the balance sheet.

## Long-term debt cycle placement
**When to use**: deciding how much room policy has. (Ch 2, Ch 3)
**How**: check three things together. Debt is high. Rates cannot fall enough. Money creation lifts asset prices more than activity. If all three hold, the cycle is late. Then ask if the debt is in a currency the borrower can print.
**Trade-offs**: footnote in Ch 2 says to judge by conditions, not by years elapsed.

## Print-or-default fork
**When to use**: any over-indebted government. (Ch 2, Ch 8, Ch 9)
**How**: own-currency debt → printing and devaluation. Foreign-currency or hard-money debt → austerity, default, hollowing-out. For the second group with wide wealth gaps, the author expects political upheaval.
**Trade-offs**: the author learned this by losing money in 1982 (Ch 5b).

## Four-lever deleveraging read
**When to use**: judging how a debt burden will be cut. (Ch 3)
**How**: weigh austerity, default/restructuring, transfers (taxes), and printing. The author says printing is the most expedient and most common. Classify the result as systemically beneficial or destructive using the three late-cycle conditions.
**Trade-offs**: the detailed method sits in *Big Debt Crises*, not here.

## Reserve-currency run watch
**When to use**: assessing a pegged or reserve currency. (Ch 3, Ch 4, Ch 7)
**How**: watch (1) net reserves falling, (2) convertibility limits or capital controls, (3) monetized war or deficit debt, (4) a rising rival, (5) a rate defence that hurts the economy. Expect capitulation, devaluation, then rate cuts. Check who holds the debt. Captive or guaranteed holders delay the exit.
**Trade-offs**: reserve status lags for decades. The guilder went in under ten years, sterling took over twenty.

## Economic-war escalation ladder
**When to use**: tracking a great-power conflict. (Ch 5a, Ch 7)
**How**: mark which of the three techniques are in use: asset freezes, capital market blocks, embargoes. Then check the five wars (trade, technology, geopolitical, capital, military). The author's key marker is a cut-off of essential imports or technology.
**Trade-offs**: the author's template is the 1940-41 US-Japan sequence. One case carries much of the weight.

## Fight-early, fight-late timing
**When to use**: asking which side wants delay. (Ch 7)
**How**: the power whose relative strength is rising prefers later. The declining one is tempted to act sooner. Add leadership transitions and internal disorder as windows of risk.
**Trade-offs**: stated as logic, not as a count of cases.

## Balance of power steps (A to E)
**When to use**: domestic politics after an election or regime change. (Ch 8)
**How**: locate the step: alliance, fight, purge among winners, peace, renewed conflict. After a win, watch whether extremes or moderates take each side.
**Trade-offs**: the same steps are claimed for offices, parties and nations.

## Six-stage internal diagnosis
**When to use**: placing a country, state or city in the internal cycle. (Ch 9)
**How**: (1) Check government finances and who buys the debt. (2) Check the three toxic-mix ingredients. (3) Count the ten markers. (4) Read the red-flag bucket: 60-80% → about 1 in 6. Over 80% → about 1 in 3. (5) Fatalities in political fighting mark Stage 6.
**Trade-offs**: the full factor table is lost from this text. Odds come from nine powers over about 2,200 years.

## Policy use-of-funds score
**When to use**: judging a fiscal or monetary programme. (Ch 5a, Ch 9)
**How**: label fiscal and monetary each easy or tight. Then ask whether the money goes to uses with a return above its cost and broad productivity gains (education, infrastructure, research) or to consumption.
**Trade-offs**: long lead times. The author gives no numeric return threshold.

## Capital-controls exit rule
**When to use**: a country with a domestic debt problem. (Ch 6, Ch 9)
**How**: the author's rule is to leave a currency when capital controls appear, and to "get out" of fiat in debt crises and wars. "When in doubt get out."
**Trade-offs**: his rule, stated without a tested hit rate.

## 100-point negotiation
**When to use**: two parties with different priorities. (Ch 7, footnote 11)
**How**: each lists its top 10 wants and spreads 100 points across them. Trade what is cheap to you and dear to the other side.
**Trade-offs**: the author calls it oversimplified.

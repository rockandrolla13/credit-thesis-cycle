# Chapter 1: The Big Cycles in a Tiny Nutshell (published 29 March 2020)

## Core Idea
Empires rise and decline in one "Big Cycle" made of mutually reinforcing strengths and weaknesses. Productivity sets the long uptrend. Money and credit cycles, wealth gaps, fights over power and acts of nature make the swings around it. The author places the world in 2020 near the end of a long-term debt cycle, 75 years after the 1945 order began.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Eight measures of wealth and power** (section "Our Measures of Wealth and Power"). The single index per country is "a roughly equal average" of:
  1. education
  2. competitiveness
  3. technology
  4. economic output
  5. share of world trade
  6. military strength
  7. financial center strength
  8. reserve currency
  - Order matters. **Education is the long-leading strength. Reserve currency is the long-lagging strength.** The currency stays "because the habit of usage lasts longer than the strengths that made it so commonly used".
  - Chart lines are 30-year moving averages, shifted to remove lag (footnote 1). Indicators are scaled so 1 is the historical peak and 0 the trough (footnote 2).
- **The Big Cycle in three phases** (section "The Big Cycle"):
  1. **Ascent**: gaining competitive advantages.
  2. **Top**: sustaining strength while sowing the seeds of decline.
  3. **Decline**: self-reinforcing loss of all the strengths.
- **The ascent chain** (author's order): strong leadership → strong education → character, civility, work ethic → low corruption and rule of law → a good resource-allocation system → openness to global thinking → competitiveness → income growth → investment in infrastructure, education, R&D → productivity → new technologies → share of world trade → strong military → leading financial center → strong equity, currency and credit markets → reserve currency.
- **Six seeds of decline at the top**:
  1. Prosperity makes labour more expensive and less competitive.
  2. Competitors copy the leader. Copying costs less than inventing.
  3. The rich work less hard. Later generations are less battle-hardened.
  4. The reserve currency gives the "exorbitant privilege" of borrowing more. Borrowing makes the empire look strong while its finances weaken.
  5. The empire extends until defence costs exceed revenue.
  6. Wealth gaps widen until the split is "perceived as intolerably unfair".
- **The decline sequence** (the author's list of "when" conditions): large debts with central banks unable to stimulate, plus a downturn, lead to printing and devaluation. Wealth and values gaps plus economic stress lead to populism of left and right. The rich move money and themselves. Governments then outlaw the outflows. Disorder cuts productivity. A rival is strong enough to challenge. Empire costs exceed revenue. Acts of nature hit a vulnerable system. Leadership is too weak.
- **The 17 forces.** The author says 17 forces explain almost all the movements. The four he ranks most important are the debt cycle, the money and credit cycle, the wealth gap cycle and the global geopolitical cycle. The chapter does not list all 17.
- **The 1-10 scoring exercise.** Score a country 10 (far left, ascent trait) to 1 (far right, decline trait) on each force and add. "The higher the number, the greater the probability of the country rising on a relative basis." The author promises a systematic version for the 20 largest countries later. This text does not contain it.

## Key Concepts
- **Three sources of wealth**: making it, taking it from others, finding it in the ground.
- **Boutique countries**: rich but too small to be empires (Switzerland, Singapore).
- **Destruction/reconstruction period**: depression, revolution and war that "tear down the old system". Typical length "about 10 to 20 years".
- **Peace and prosperity period**: "about 40 to 80 years".
- **Short-term debt/business cycle**: "about 7 to 10 years".
- **Stress test**: the author's word for 1930-45 and for 2020.
- **Hollowing-out**: the rich leave, tax revenue falls, conditions worsen, taxes rise, more leave.

## Mental Models
- Use the eight measures as a leading-to-lagging ladder. A country can hold the reserve currency long after education and competitiveness fell.
- "When the richest get into debt by borrowing from the poorest, it is a very early sign of a relative wealth shift."
- How bad a turbulent period gets "depends almost exclusively on how strong the countries are to endure them": savings, low debt, a reserve currency, capable leadership, inventiveness.
- "The quicker the printing of money to fill the debt holes, the quicker the closing of the deflationary depression and the sooner the worrying about the value of money begins."
- "Destiny, more than the leader, is in control." A leader inherits the cycle position.

## Anti-patterns
- **Seeking precision in a 500-year picture.** The author says detail blocks the pattern.
- **Calling a war period productive** because output per person rose. Destruction offsets it.
- **Extrapolating the uptrend and over-borrowing.** The author gives this as the cause of "almost all debt busts".
- **Reading reserve status as current strength.** It lags.

## Worked Example
**The 1930-45 stress test** (author's numbers):
- The Roaring '20s became a debt-financed bubble that popped in 1929.
- The economy fell "about 10%". The stock market fell "about 85%".
- Past economic and market declines "each lasted about three years" until a restructuring of debt, money, fiscal policy and political power reversed them.
- The US market and economy bottomed the day Roosevelt announced the break of the gold promise and the creation of enough money and credit.
- War followed. By the end, global GDP per capita had fallen "about 12%".
- A new order began in 1945. The US then held "80% of the world's gold stock" and "about half of world production".

**The same pattern earlier**: Panic of 1907 → wealth redistribution, monetised deficits and world war → Spanish flu → new order at Versailles in 1919 → the 1920s debt boom.

**US borrowing from China**: in the 1980s US income per head was "40 times" China's, and the US began to borrow from Chinese savers who wanted dollars.

## Key Takeaways
1. Rank a country on the eight measures. Note which lead and which lag.
2. Check the six seeds of decline for the leading power. The author says overextension and wealth gaps both apply to the US.
3. Expect debt crises, wealth conflict and external conflict to arrive together "as a perfect storm". They reinforce each other.
4. Economic destruction periods and wars "tend to last roughly two or three years". The full transition takes 10 to 20.
5. The author's 2020 placement: near the end of a long-term debt cycle, with central banks monetising government debt.
6. The author's stated bias: invest in "humanity's adaptability and inventiveness".

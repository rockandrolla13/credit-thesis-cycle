# Chapter 5, Part 2: The Big Cycles of the United States and the Dollar, 1945-2020 (published 22 July 2020)

## Core Idea
The 1945 order gave the US the reserve currency, about two-thirds of government-held gold and a 75-year run. The US then spent its reserves, built foreign debt in its own currency, and moved from MP1 to MP2 in 2008 and MP3 in 2020. The author judges the US "roughly 75% through that cycle, +/- 10%". CASE chapter with several rules the author draws from it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The seven-step cycle and the 75% reading** (section "Where the US Is in Its Big Cycle"). After a war and a new order:
  a. Peace and productivity. Debt is used productively and repaid. Equities do well.
  b. Excessive debt funds speculation and over-consumption. Incomes cannot service it.
  c. Central banks cut rates and supply credit. Wealth gaps and debt grow.
  d. Central banks lose the ability to create **self-funding debt growth**, meaning debt that does not accelerate against income without central bank subsidy.
  e. Severe downturn with large wealth gaps. Internal conflict.
  f. Printing, debt restructuring, redistribution by tax.
  g. Vulnerability to emerging powers. Wars set the new order.
  - The author's placement of the US: about 75% through, plus or minus 10%.
- **The run condition on a linked currency** (section "The Late-1960s Weakening Fundamentals"). Holding claims in place of gold ends when (a) claims on real money "substantially exceed" real money in the bank and (b) reserves are visibly falling. Then "no interest rate can be high enough" to hold the debt.
- **The own-currency debt rule** (section "The 1979-82 Move to Tight Money"). The author's lesson from his 1982 error: "when debts are in the currencies that central banks have the ability to print and restructure, debt crises can be well-managed, so they are not systemically threatening."
- **The reciprocal rule.** "The value of assets is the reciprocal of the value of money and credit" and "the value of money is the reciprocal of the quantity of it". The author's stated conclusion: when central banks make a lot of cheap money and credit, "it is wise to be more aggressive in owning assets".
- **The reflation announcement pattern.** The author groups: Roosevelt (March 1933), Nixon (August 1971), Volcker (August 1982), Bernanke (November 2008), Draghi (July 2012), and March 2020. He calls it "standard operating procedure by central banks that will persist until that approach no longer works".
- **A country's income statement.** Exports are revenue. Exports minus imports are net income. Deficits are financed from reserves (savings) or by borrowing. Net international investment position is the balance sheet.
- **Jungle rule.** Inside countries law governs. Between countries "raw power matters most" and treaties and bodies such as the UN and WTO "don't matter much".
- **Left-right pendulum.** Politics swings "as the excesses of each become intolerable and the memories of the problems of the other fade". When one extreme is very popular, expect the opposite move.
- **Aid as reserve-currency building.** Marshall and Dodge plans helped recipients, US exports, US influence and dollar usage. The author says China's Belt and Road offers China "similar advantages".

## Key Concepts
- **Financial repression 1933-1951** (author's description, not his term): the Fed printed to buy debt, capped lending rates and controlled where money could go. Explicit yield curve control ran 1942-1947 (footnote 3).
- **Gold ownership ban**: Americans were by and large not allowed to own gold from 1933 to 1974 (footnote 2).
- **Days of imports in reserves**: US and UK about 70 days. Russia about 700. China about 600.
- **Reserve composition**: gold fell from 65% of world reserves in 1945 to about 10%. Dollar over 50%. European currencies 20-25%. Yen and sterling about 5%. RMB 2%.
- **Vulnerability of the reserve issuer**: the US is "very powerful because it can print the world's money and would be very vulnerable if it lost its reserve currency status".

## Mental Models
- Cheap stocks follow trauma. After 1945 stocks were cheap against bonds because depression and war survivors were risk-averse. That set up a multi-decade bull market.
- Peak confidence marks the top. In the mid-1960s people believed economics was a science and the market always rose. Stocks peaked in 1966 and did not bottom until 1982.
- "The biggest risk for the leading powers is that they lose the economic and technology races over time." The winner of wars "outspends, out-invests, and outlasts".
- If the dollar lost reserve status, the author names the winners: "those with dollar-debt liabilities and those with non-dollar assets".
- Voters watch what they get, not who pays. Elected officials therefore take on debt and non-debt liabilities.

## Anti-patterns
- **Betting on a default-led depression when the debt is in a printable currency.** The author's 1982 loss.
- **Buying the first dip after a secular top** (1966).
- **Price and wage controls.** The author says Nixon's controls "created great economic distortions".
- **Judging the US by its reserves alone or its printing power alone.** Both sides matter.

## Worked Example
**1. The author's 1982 mistake**
1. October 1979: Volcker announced a 5.5% limit on M1 growth. The author calculated that this meant a shortage of money and very high rates.
2. He foresaw the emerging-market debt crisis. Mexico defaulted on 23 August 1982.
3. He then bet on a debt-default depression. US stocks rallied on the day of the default, "a straw in the wind that I missed".
4. He lost money, dismissed his staff and borrowed $4,000 from his father.
5. Cause of error: the Fed could fund US banks, and accounting rules did not force loss recognition. Result: the own-currency debt rule and the reciprocal rule above.

**2. The 1971-91 cycle in markets**
| Phase | Markets |
|---|---|
| 1970s | Inflation and inflation hedges soared. Bonds fell. Gold went from $35 to "a then-peak of $850 in 1980". |
| 1979-81 | "Bone-crushing" tightening. Cash was the best investment. Non-US debtors restructured. |
| 1980s | Falling inflation. Bonds and stocks did very well. Brady agreement in 1989 began to end the "lost decade". |

**3. Numbers on the US position**
- 1945: reserves equal to about 8 to 8.5 years of imports. Gold about 10 times the UK's.
- Demobilisation 1945-47: over 20 million released. Unemployment rose only from 1.9% to 3.9%.
- US banks with foreign branches: 13 (1965), 79 (1970), 787 branches by 1980.
- Exports: UK from about 30% of world exports in 1900 to under 5%. US steady at 20-25% until about 2000, now about 14%. China from about 5% to about 15%.
- Trade balance turned negative around 1970.
- China holds about $1.1 trillion of US debt, about a third of its reserves and under 5% of US debt. Japan holds about $1.2-1.3 trillion.
- China's output is about 70% of US output at market rates and larger at purchasing power parity.
- Party-line voting in Congress: about 95% as of 2016, "the highest level in over a century".
- 15 March 2020: over $2 trillion of stimulus, rates to 0%, $700 billion of QE.

**4. MP2 transmission 2008-20** (author's chain): central banks buy bonds → sellers buy other assets → asset prices rise → wealth gap widens → cheap funding pays for buybacks and acquisitions → stock prices and profits rise further. "This money did not trickle down proportionately."

## Key Takeaways
1. Check whether debt growth is self-funding. If it needs central bank subsidy, the author places the cycle at step d or later.
2. Separate debt in a printable currency from debt in a non-printable one before forecasting a crisis.
3. Expect the reflation announcement, and expect asset prices to rise in the depreciating currency.
4. Track the country income statement: export share, trade balance, reserves in days of imports, net international investment position.
5. Wealth and income gaps were "the largest since the 1930-45 period".
6. The author says reversing a declining power "is very difficult" but not fixed. It needs earning more than spending and a system that works for most people.

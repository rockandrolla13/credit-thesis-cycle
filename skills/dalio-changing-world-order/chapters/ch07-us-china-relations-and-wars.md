# Chapter 7: US-China Relations and Wars (published 25 September 2020)

## Core Idea
The US and China are in five interrelated "wars": trade/economic, technology, geopolitical, capital and military. The author adds two more: the culture war and "the war with ourselves". He treats them as one evolving conflict fought by tests of power, not by law. The chapter gives escalation markers for each. The author says he moves here "from just conveying facts to sharing opinions". All forward statements date from September 2020.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three principles** (opening section):
  1. **Relationships.** Two parties choose a win-win "cooperative-competitive" relationship or a lose-lose "mutually threatening" one. "Either side can force the second path on the other while it takes both sides to follow the first." In the second, power is "the relative abilities to endure pain as much as the relative abilities to inflict it".
  2. **Power.** "Have power, respect power, and use power wisely." Sequence of dispute resolution: (1) agree directly, (2) use agreed rules, (3) use power, (4) war, which is "the testing of relative power". Keep power like "a hidden knife". **Timing rule: "it pays to fight early if one's relative power is declining and fight later if it's rising."**
  3. **War.** When two entities have comparable power, including power to destroy each other, the risk of war to the death is high unless both have "extremely high trust". This is the prisoner's dilemma. The remedy is mutually assured protection plus "exchanges of benefits and dependencies that would be intolerable to lose".
- **The five types of war** (section "The Positions the Americans and Chinese Are In"): trade/economic, technology, geopolitical, capital, military. Read them as "interrelated conflicts that are extensions of one bigger evolving conflict". Ask of each side: is it trying to hasten or ease the conflict? Leaders must be clear on "red lines" and "trip wires".
- **Escalation markers** (the author's, per war):
  | War | Current state (Sept 2020) | Marker of major escalation |
  |---|---|---|
  | Trade/economic | Tariffs and import limits. A "very limited" Phase One deal. | Cutting the other off from **essential imports** (rare earths, semiconductors, oil, metals). "Much like the US cutting off oil to Japan was a short leading indicator." |
  | Technology | Bans and sanctions on Huawei, TikTok, WeChat. | The US shuts off Chinese access to essential technologies, most obviously advanced semiconductors (TSMC). |
  | Geopolitical | Sovereignty disputes. Countries choosing sides. | A "Fourth Taiwan Strait Crisis". |
  | Capital | About 8,000 US sanctions in place as of 2019. Early moves to curb US investment in China and delist Chinese firms. | Action against Chinese holdings of dollar assets. Cutting China from capital. |
  | Military | Both sides testing limits in the East and South China Seas. | Hot conflict over Taiwan, the China Seas or North Korea. |
- **The two capital war risks**: being shut off from capital ("a greater risk for China") and losing reserve currency status ("a greater risk for the US"). "No money = no power."
- **Four reasons dollar debt is less desirable** (section "The Capital War"):
  a. Foreign portfolios hold disproportionately large amounts of it. Footnote 8 gives four yardsticks: balanced portfolio weights, reserve needs for trade and capital flows, US debt market size against other markets, US economy size against others.
  b. The supply of dollar debt and money is rising at a pace that needs Fed monetization.
  c. Nominal yield is negligible and real yield is negative.
  d. Debt is a worse store of wealth in potential wartime.
  Plus: sanctions on one holder raise perceived risk for all holders.
- **The limit test.** The US is testing how much there can be at once of (a) enormous money and debt creation, (b) falling and negative real returns, (c) the dollar used as a weapon, (d) a fiat system. "We won't know what the limit is and we can't say it is here until it is reached. At that point it will be too late to fix."
- **What the loss of reserve status would look like** (the author's sequence):
  1. Holders sell dollar debt.
  2. Smart debtors borrow dollars cheaply.
  3. The Fed must choose between letting rates rise "unacceptably" and printing to buy debt.
  4. It prints. The currency falls. The fall is self-reinforcing because yields do not compensate.
  5. It continues until the currency and real rates set a new balance of payments, meaning forced selling and curtailed buying by Americans.
  No alternative reserve currency is needed for this to happen.
- **Why stupid wars happen** (four causes): the prisoner's dilemma, tit-for-tat escalation, the cost to a declining power of backing down ("a retreat is a defeat"), and misunderstanding under time pressure. Leaders who "rile people up" add to the risk.
- **Agreed principles the author proposes**: one's power determines one's position. "The only real rule in international relations is that there are no rules." Winning means getting what matters most without losing what matters most. The smartest way to win is to outcompete and negotiate from strength.

## Key Concepts
- **Decoupling**: both countries reduce dependence. Footnote 6: a "compartmented rather than a broad-based" decoupling with "significantly reduced efficiency".
- **Dual circulation**: China's self-sufficiency plan (footnote 2).
- **Sovereignty**: the author says it is "probably China's biggest issue" and the one it "would fight to the death" over.
- **Tributary-like relationships**: what the author thinks China wants with most non-rival countries.
- **Moral high ground principle**: "when the fighting gets tough, it becomes easier to justify doing that which was previously considered immoral".
- **The 100-point exercise** (footnote 11): each side lists its top 10 wants and allocates 100 points. Trade low-value items for high-value ones.

## Mental Models
- "Whoever wins the technology war will probably also win the economic and military wars."
- "Big data + big AI + big computing = superior decision making."
- "Throughout history the most powerful country is typically taken down by alliances of less powerful countries that are collectively stronger." Among China, Russia and the US since 1945, two have allied against the third.
- Countries choosing sides say: on economics, China. On military, the US, if it will show up.
- Reserve currency use, like language use, "lags the fundamental reasons for using it by many years".
- "The implausible happens more often than one would expect." Negative nominal and real long rates without capital controls were called implausible a few years earlier.
- Internal disorder and leadership transitions are when opponents move.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading each dispute as separate.**
- **Complaining that the other side plays unfairly.** The author asks leaders to "stop whining" and play smartly.
- **Making a show of a commitment you may not keep.** "The more of a show the US makes of defending Taiwan the greater the humiliation of a lost war or a retreat."
- **Using capital sanctions without counting the cost.** They are "double-edged swords" that weaken US investors and exchanges.
- **Expecting the other culture to convert.** "One can't expect the Chinese not to be Chinese and Americans not to be Americans."

## Worked Example
**1. Reserve currencies assessed by fundamentals** (the author's verdicts)
| Asset | Author's assessment |
|---|---|
| Dollar | Dominant by use. Four weakening influences above. |
| Euro | "Weakly structured", fragmented union, economically and militarily weak. |
| Yen | Little international use. Too much debt, monetized, unattractive rates. |
| Pound | "Anachronistically held", weak fundamentals. |
| Gold | "Worked the best for the longest time". Limited market size. |
| RMB | The only one held "because of its fundamentals". Drawbacks: little use, no free capital flow, no free float, undeveloped markets and clearing, trust not yet built. A lot of domestic debt to restructure. |

**2. Underinvestment in China** (author's numbers): China and the US each about 13% of world trade. RMB about 2% of trade finance against over 50% for the dollar. China about 19% of world GDP (PPP) and 15% of equity capitalization, but about 5% of MSCI indices and 2% of foreign assets in portfolios. The US: about 20% of GDP, over 50% of MSCI, about 48% of non-American money.

**3. Technology position**: US tech market capitalization about twice China's. Alibaba and Tencent ranked fifth and seventh in the world. 40% of the largest civilian supercomputers in China. STEM graduates entering tech "about eight times" the US number. The US leads in advanced AI and trails in 5G.

**4. Taiwan logic**: the author thinks China is militarily stronger in the region and the US "will probably lose that fight". Not fighting would signal US decline "in much the same way as the British loss of the Suez Canal". He gives an illustrative 70% chance of the US losing and still expects pressure to fight, because retreat costs standing with allies.

**5. Other numbers**: US bases in more than 70 countries. China holds about $1 trillion of about $27 trillion of US debt, around 4%. A 2019 CNBC survey: 1 in 5 North American firms said China had stolen their IP in the past year. A 2020 Pew survey: 73% of Americans viewed China unfavourably.

**6. Dated forecasts** (as stated in September 2020): the trade deal is about the best there will be. Both countries will be more independent in five years. China will be "much more independent and in a much stronger position" in technology in 5-10 years. Chinese currency and capital markets will develop at "a shockingly fast pace" over 5-10 years. Some think China could reach broad military superiority in 5-10 years. If there is war, later suits China and sooner suits the US.

## Key Takeaways
1. Watch for cut-offs of essential imports and essential technology. The author treats them as the short leading indicator of worse conflict.
2. Treat sanctions as capital war. Each use adds to other holders' reasons to diversify.
3. Do not wait for an alternative reserve currency before considering dollar risk.
4. The author judges the internal conflicts in both countries "more important and bigger" than the external ones.
5. A rising power wants delay. A declining power is tempted to act early.
6. The author's summary of US standing: "a slight lead in power that is shrinking".

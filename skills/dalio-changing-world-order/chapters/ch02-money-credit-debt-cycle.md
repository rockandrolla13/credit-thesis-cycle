# Chapter 2: The Big Cycle of Money, Credit, Debt, and Economic Activity (published 23 April 2020)

## Core Idea
Money and credit are "the biggest single influence on how wealth and power rise and decline". Credit is stimulative when given and depressing when paid back, so the system is cyclical. The long-term debt cycle runs from low debt with much room to stimulate, to high debt with none. It ends in a restructuring or devaluation. The author says central banks "always print money and devalue" when the debt is in their own currency.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The income statement and balance sheet test** (section "The Timeless and Universal Fundamentals"). Every person, company, nonprofit and government has income, expenses, assets and liabilities.
  - How: project income against expenses plus debt service. Compare assets with liabilities. Ask how long savings last if income stops and assets halve. Do it for yourself, then for others, then add them up.
  - Tell: entities that "look rich even while they are in the process of going broke". They spend a lot and hold cash, but income is below expenses or liabilities exceed assets.
- **"Debt eats equity, money feeds the hunger of debt, and central banks can produce money."** Debt is senior. When debt cannot be serviced the asset is sold or taken. The creditor is paid ahead of the owner.
- **Real economy versus financial economy.** Each has its own supply and demand. Watch both.
  - Real: demand above capacity raises inflation.
  - Financial: money and credit created relative to the demand for it sets the value of money.
  - **Monetary inflation**: money and credit flow into other currencies and inflation-hedge assets, not into lending. Currency falls. This can happen while the real economy deflates. "That is how inflationary depressions come about."
- **The stimulant bottle.** The central bank has a limited bottle of stimulant. The long-term cycle starts with a full bottle and ends with an empty one.
- **End-of-cycle test** (three conditions together): debt levels are high, interest rates "can't be adequately lowered", and money creation "increases financial asset prices more than it increases actual economic activity". Then holders of debt want other stores of wealth.
- **The long-term debt cycle in six stages** (section "The Long-Term Debt Cycle"):
  1. No or low debt and "hard money" (gold, silver).
  2. Claims on hard money ("notes" or paper). A **linked currency system**.
  3. Increased debt. Claims grow relative to hard money and to goods and services.
  4. Debt crises, defaults and devaluations. A run occurs.
  5. Fiat money. The constraint is removed. The risk moves to over-creation.
  6. Flight back into hard money. Governments restrict gold, foreign currency and capital outflows. Debt is "largely wiped out, usually by making the money to pay it back plentiful and cheap". Then a link to hard money is restored.
- **Three types of monetary system**: Type 1 hard money, Type 2 paper claims on hard money, Type 3 fiat. Countries move 1 → 2 → 3 to gain room to print, then back to a constrained type after the fiat currency fails. Cited examples: Song Dynasty, Weimar Germany.
- **Monetary Policy 1, 2, 3** (section "The Monetary System That We Are in"):
  - **MP1**: lower interest rates. First choice.
  - **MP2**: print money and buy financial assets, mostly government bonds. Used when rates hit 0%. Called "quantitative easing" because it "sounds less threatening" than debt monetization. It lifts asset prices but is "relatively inefficient" at reaching those who need money.
  - **MP3**: the central government borrows and directs spending and lending. The central bank creates money and buys the debt. Includes helicopter money.
- **The central bank's choice in a run**: let money leave the debt asset (rates rise, the economy worsens) or print and buy the bonds. If claims are too large relative to money and goods, the choice becomes default or devalue. "They inevitably devalue."

## Key Concepts
- **Money**: "a medium of exchange that can also be used as a storehold of wealth".
- **Money versus debt**: money settles claims. Debt is a promise to deliver money.
- **Short-term debt cycle**: "about eight years, give or take a few". Ends when demand reaches the real economy's capacity.
- **Long-term debt cycle**: "about 50 to 75 years". Footnote 2: judge position by conditions, not elapsed time.
- **Big debt crisis inside a regime**: two to four per currency regime, with write-downs or devaluations "of 30% or more", not big enough to break the system.
- **Crisis speed**: the abrupt part lasts "months to up to three years".
- **Reserve currency shares (2020)**: dollar about 55% of international transactions, euro about 25%, yen under 10%, renminbi about 2%.
- **Calculated wealth versus actual wealth**: a higher house price does not give you more house.

## Mental Models
- Holding debt late in the cycle is "like holding a ticking time bomb that rewards you while it's still ticking".
- "The closer people are to the blowup the safer they tend to feel." Memory of the last one fades as the risk rises.
- Three ratios the author says to watch: (1) debt due relative to hard money available to pay it, (2) debt payments relative to debtors' cash flow, (3) the interest reward for lending.
- Reflation has two outcomes. Early in the cycle it funds a new credit expansion, "good for stocks". Late in the cycle it produces monetary inflation with a weak economy, "good for inflation-hedge assets such as gold".
- No policy maker owns the whole cycle. Each acts in their own interest at their point in it. Expect governments to "abuse their privileged positions".
- Monetization is "vastly more politically palatable" than taxes as a way to shift wealth.

## Anti-patterns
- **Confusing money with wealth.** Creating money does not create goods and services.
- **Assuming short-term cycles will "go on working this way forever".**
- **Assuming an alternative reserve currency must exist before a currency can fail.** The author cites China and Rome as runs with no alternative currency.
- **Trusting governments to protect savers.**
- **Printing without checking productive use.** The two risks of money over credit: nobody analyses the use, and nothing must be repaid.

## Worked Example
**Bretton Woods to MP3** (the author's chain, with his numbers):
1. 1944-45: Type 2 system. Other central banks could convert dollars to gold at $35 an ounce. The US held about two-thirds of government-held gold and produced about 50% of world output. There was $50 of paper money per ounce of US gold.
2. Later years: "guns and butter" deficits. Claims on gold grew. Gold in the vault fell.
3. 15 August 1971: Nixon ended convertibility, as Roosevelt had on 5 March 1933. Move to Type 3.
4. 1970s: flight from dollar debt into goods and gold. Gold went from $35 to a peak of $670 in 1980. Borrowers worldwide took dollar loans through US banks.
5. 1979-82: Volcker tightened. Rates rose far above inflation. Debtors were squeezed. The dollar was strong.
6. 1980s: emerging-market debtors had a "decade-long depression and debt-restructuring period". The Fed funded US banks and accounting rules let them avoid loss recognition. Closed in 1991 with the Brady Bond agreement.
7. 1990s to 2008: two "fairly typical" debt cycles, the dot-com bubble (burst 2000) and the larger 2007 bubble (burst 2008).
8. 2008: short rates hit 0%. MP2 began. Sellers of bonds bought other assets. Expected returns fell. Investors borrowed to buy assets, "the classic bubble process".
9. 9 April 2020: the Fed and the US government announced programs with "all the classic MP3 techniques". The author calls it "essentially the same announcement that Roosevelt made on March 5, 1933".

**Dollar reach in 2020**: the US is about 20% of world GDP but the dollar is about 60% of reserves. Dollar debt owed by non-Americans is "about $20 trillion", about 50% above 2008, with a little under half short-term. The author compares the dollar shortage with 1982-91, "except interest rates can't be cut significantly this time".

## Key Takeaways
1. Run the income and balance sheet test on any entity before trusting its appearance.
2. Place the economy in the six stages by debt level and by how much stimulant remains.
3. Debt in a currency the borrower can print gets devalued. Debt in a currency the borrower cannot print gets defaulted.
4. MP2 raises asset prices and the wealth gap. MP3 is what the author says follows when MP2 stops working.
5. Non-reserve borrowers with reserve-currency debt, low reserves and falling export income are the most exposed.
6. The reserve issuer runs policy for its own citizens. Others may not get the currency they need.

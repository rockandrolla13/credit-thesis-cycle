# Chapter 5, Part 1: The Big Cycles of the United States and the Dollar, 1930-1945 (published 17 July 2020)

## Core Idea
"Before there is a shooting war there is usually an economic war." The 1929 bust led to depression, populism of left and right, tariffs, sanctions and then war about ten years later. The author studies this period because he says the US and China are "now in an economic war that could conceivably evolve into a shooting war". Mostly a CASE chapter, with two reusable checklists.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three opening principles** (section "1930 to 1939/41: The Economic War"):
  1. Before a shooting war there is usually an economic war.
  2. "Severe economic downturns with large wealth gaps, large debts, and ineffective monetary policies make a combustible combination" that leads to conflict and revolutionary change inside countries.
  3. In great conflict there is "a strong tendency to move to more autocratic leadership".
- **Deflationary depression principle.** Deflationary depressions are debt crises where debtors lack money to service debt. "They inevitably lead to the printing of money, debt restructurings, and government spending programs". "The only question is how long it takes." In the US case: October 1929 to March 1933.
- **Redistribution principle.** Severe distress plus large wealth gaps produce "revolutionarily large redistributions of wealth". Peaceful form: large tax increases on the rich and money creation that devalues creditors' claims. Violent form: forced confiscation.
- **Three governance choices** (the author's definition grid):
  | System | Decision-making | Ownership | Priority |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | US, UK | Democratic (bottom-up) | Capitalist | Individualistic |
  | Fascism (Germany, Japan) | Autocratic (top-down) | Capitalist | Collectivist |
  | Soviet Union | Autocratic | Communist | Collectivist |
- **Economic warfare techniques** (the author's list of the "most common"):
  1. **Asset freezes or seizures.** From targeted freezes to debt repudiation.
  2. **Blocking capital market access.** Example: Germany banned purchase of Russian securities in 1887.
  3. **Embargoes and blockades.** Example: the US oil embargo of Japan.
- **Classic wartime economic policies inside a country.** The government controls: (a) what is produced, (b) rationing, (c) imports and exports, (d) prices, wages and profits, (e) access to one's own financial assets, (f) moving money abroad. It also (g) issues debt that is monetized, (h) uses gold for international payment because credit is not accepted, (i) governs more autocratically, (j) sanctions enemies, (k) is sanctioned.
- **The corner principle.** Powers fight only when roughly comparable. "Smart leaders typically only go into hot wars if there is no choice because the other side pushes them into the position of either fighting or losing by backing down."
- **Wealth in wartime principle.** "Protecting one's wealth in times of war is difficult." Safe investments are not safe, capital mobility is limited, taxes are high. Protecting the wealthy "is not a priority".

## Key Concepts
- **Tariff effect (author's view)**: lower efficiency, tariff wars, lost exports for the tariff-raising country. It benefits protected firms and can build political support for the leader.
- **US power today (author's reading of the lost chart)**: education, competitiveness, trade and production have declined over 100 years to "around the 50-60th percentile" against other leading powers. Innovation and technology, reserve status, financial center and military remain at or near the top. Peak was the 1950s.
- **Lend-Lease (March 1941)**: the US lent or leased supplies and postponed payment. It "ended the United States' neutrality".
- **Weakness invites opportunism**: "when countries are weak, opposing countries take advantage".

## Mental Models
- Date the war from the start of the economic war, not from the first shot. "The economic wars started about 10 years before the hot wars."
- See events "through the relevant parties' eyes, including those of enemies". The author says he aims to be "mechanical", not ideological.
- Borrowing in one's own currency "can be highly productive if the money borrowed is put into investments that raise productivity" enough to service the debt. The author uses 1930s Germany as his example.
- In wartime, markets that stay open trade on battle odds. Losing a war "typically leads to a total wipeout of wealth".
- Central bank tightening into a fragile recovery: the Fed tightened in 1936-37 and caused the 1937-38 recession.

## Anti-patterns
- **Demonizing the rival.** The author calls it counterproductive to understanding.
- **Strangling a rival into a fight-or-lose position.** The 1941 embargo left Japan a choice between backing down and attacking.
- **Assuming stock markets stay open.** German and Japanese markets closed at the end of the war and did not reopen for about five years.

## Worked Example
**1. US: bust, reflation, redistribution**
| Item | Number in the text |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Fed tightened in 1929 to curb speculation. Smoot-Hawley tariffs in 1930. |
| Time to policy response | October 1929 peak to March 1933 |
| Roosevelt's steps, March/April 1933 | Ended gold convertibility. Funded all banks. Ordered gold over $100 turned in at $20.67 an ounce. Devalued the dollar. |
| Result to end-1936 | Stocks returned over 200%. Real growth averaged about 9%. |
| Top personal tax rate | 25% (1930) → 75% (1935 "Soak the Rich Tax") → 81% (1941) |
| Top corporate tax rate | 12% (1930) → 31% (1941) |
| Budget deficit | About 1% → about 4% of GDP |

**2. Germany and Japan**
- Germany: 25% unemployment when Hitler took power in 1933. Nil by 1938. Income per head up 22% in five years. Real growth over 8% a year in 1934-38. Equities up nearly 70% from 1933 to 1938. Financed by forcing banks to buy government bonds and by Reichsbank monetization.
- Japan: debt crisis in 1927. Exports fell about 50% from 1929 to 1931. It left gold in 1931. The military took control. Manchuria was invaded in 1931.

**3. The sanctions ladder against Japan**
| Date | US step |
|---|---|
| 1940 | Export Control Act. Pacific Fleet moved to Hawaii. |
| October 1940 | Embargo on iron and steel outside Britain and the Western Hemisphere. |
| March 1941 | Lend-Lease to Britain, the Soviet Union and China. |
| July-August 1941 | All Japanese assets in the US frozen. Panama Canal closed to Japan. Oil and gas exports embargoed. |
| Effect | Cut three-fourths of Japan's trade and 80% of its oil. Japan estimated two years of oil left. |
| 7-8 December 1941 | Attacks on the Philippines and Pearl Harbor. |

**4. War costs**: 40-75 million dead, 3% of world population. Russia about 25 million, China about 20 million. Financial cost "in the vicinity of $4-7 trillion in current dollars". After the Battle of Midway in 1942, Allied equities rallied almost continuously. Axis equities were flat or down.

## Key Takeaways
1. Count the three economic warfare techniques in use between two powers. They mark the stage of conflict.
2. Expect policy makers to print in a deflationary depression. Estimate the delay, not the direction.
3. Expect large tax rises on the rich when distress and wealth gaps combine.
4. Wartime controls reach prices, profits, access to one's own assets and capital outflows.
5. An embargo that removes a rival's essential resource can force the hot war it was meant to prevent.
6. The relative winner of 1939-45 was the US. It sold and lent, and its territory was undamaged.

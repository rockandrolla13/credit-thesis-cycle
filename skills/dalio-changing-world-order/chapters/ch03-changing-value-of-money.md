# Chapter 3: The Changing Value of Money (published 7 May 2020)

## Core Idea
This chapter is the author's appendix to Chapter 2. "All currencies devalue or die", and cash and bonds fall with them. Printing is "the most expedient, least well-understood, and most common big way of restructuring debts". The author says the dollar, euro and yen are in the late stage of their long-term debt cycles.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The four levers to cut debt burdens** (section "Printing and Devaluing Money Is the Easiest Way out"). The author credits *Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises*.
  | Lever | Effect | Author's verdict |
  |---|---|---|
  | Austerity | Deflationary | "doesn't last long because it's too painful" |
  | Defaults and restructurings | Deflationary | Painful for debtor and creditor |
  | Transfers from haves to have-nots (taxes) | Not stated | "politically challenging but more tolerable", usually part of the resolution |
  | Printing money and devaluing | Lowers the value of money | Most expedient and most common |
- **Why printing wins politically**: it relieves the squeeze, the harmed parties are hard to identify (they are holders of money and debt), and asset prices rise in the falling currency so people "appear" richer.
- **What a currency devalues against.** First against **debt**: that is the purpose. Then the flow of new money decides the rest:
  - It flows into productivity and profits → real stock prices rise.
  - It hurts returns on cash and debt enough to push holders into inflation hedges and other currencies → "a self-reinforcing decline in the value of money".
- **The late-cycle breakdown test** (three conditions):
  a. Debt and money are too large to be turned into real value.
  b. The real rate low enough to save debtors is below the real rate creditors need to hold the debt.
  c. MP1 and MP2 no longer work, so monetary policy becomes "a facilitator of the political system that allocates resources in an uneconomic way".
- **Beneficial versus destructive devaluations.** *Systemically beneficial*: relieves debt and restarts credit. *Systemically destructive*: damages the capital allocation system but is needed to wipe out debt and build a new monetary order. The author says to tell them apart. He gives the three-condition test above as the guide.
- **Common pattern of reserve-currency devaluations** (section "The Patterns of Countries Devaluing"):
  1. A classic run. Claims on the central bank exceed the hard currency behind them.
  2. Net central bank reserves fall first, "in some cases starting years ahead".
  3. Convertibility is sometimes suspended before the exchange rate moves (UK 1947 before 1949, US 1971).
  4. Large debt problems are present, often from war spending.
  5. The central bank first lets short rates rise to defend. That is too painful. It capitulates and devalues. Then it cuts rates.
  6. Outcomes afterward depend on how much economic and military power the country still has.
- **Three preconditions for losing reserve status**:
  1. Loss of economic and political primacy to a rising rival.
  2. Large, growing debts monetized by the central bank.
  3. A self-reinforcing run that "can't be stopped because the fiscal and balance of payments deficits are too great for cutbacks to close".

## Key Concepts
- **Currency risk**: the author says most people watch asset prices and ignore the currency they are measured in.
- **Episodic, not evolutionary**: big devaluations come as abrupt drops in debt crises, with stable periods between.
- **Devaluation versus loss of reserve status**: not the same. Loss of status "comes from chronic and large devaluations".
- **Total return of cash**: spot currency move plus interest earned.
- **Jubilee**: the Old Testament debt-forgiveness year. The author compares printing with a Monopoly banker who makes more money when players go broke.

## Mental Models
- Treat cash as an investment and ask how it performed.
- Use two yardsticks for a currency: gold ("the timeless and universal alternative currency") and a CPI basket (buying power).
- The central bank facing a choice between higher real rates and printing "will choose the second path".
- In wartime, credit between countries stops. "Gold, or in some cases silver or barter, is the coin of the realm."
- Under a free-floating fiat system, expect "a more gradual and continuous devaluation" in place of abrupt breaks.

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing cash is the safe asset.**
- **Judging a devaluation only by the exchange rate.** Check gold, goods, stocks and other currencies.
- **Assuming the winner of a war keeps its currency.** Britain won and lost reserve status.

## Worked Example
**The six big devaluation periods since 1850** (the author's list):
| Period | What happened |
|---|---|
| 1860s | US Civil War. Gold convertibility suspended. "Greenbacks" printed to monetize war debt. |
| World War I, 1914-18 | Deficits funded by central bank printing. Gold was international money. |
| 1919-22 | European currencies devalued. German mark and mark debt totally extinguished in 1920-23. |
| 1930-45 | Debt bubble burst, then war debts. Competitive devaluations in "virtually all countries". After the war the currencies and debts of Germany, Japan, Italy and China were destroyed. Britain and France were severely devalued. |
| 1968-73 | Claims on gold exceeded gold. Link broken on 15 August 1971. |
| Since 2000 | Gradual fall against gold from money creation and low rates relative to inflation. |

**Scorecard numbers given in the text**:
- About 750 currencies have existed since 1700. About 20% remain. All survivors were devalued.
- 1850-1913: holding interest-earning currency was "generally profitable" against gold. Most currencies stayed fixed.
- By the mid-1950s only the dollar and the Swiss franc were worth even half of their 1850s value.
- Since 1850: average annual return on interest-earning cash 1.2%. The text gives gold's average real return as 1.3%.
- Since 1912: real return on bills -0.2%. Gold 2.2%. Cash lost over 2% a year in France, Italy and Japan, and over 15% a year in Germany.
- About half of countries gave a positive real return on bills since 1850. Germany wiped holders out twice.
- Swings around the averages were "roughly 10-year-long".
- Gold after 1971: rose in real terms to 1980-81, fell until 2000 when rates were well above inflation, then rose again as rates fell and central banks printed.

**The three reserve currencies compared**:
| | Dutch guilder | British pound | US dollar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speed | Massive, under a decade | Gradual, two devaluations | Two abrupt (1933, 1971), gradual since 2000 |
| Trigger | Fourth Anglo-Dutch War | World wars | Vietnam under Bretton Woods |
| Result | Status lost | Status lost | Status kept so far |

## Key Takeaways
1. Expect printing. It is the lever with the least political cost.
2. Watch net reserves and convertibility rules. Both move before the exchange rate.
3. A rate defence that hurts the economy does not last. The author says central banks "quickly capitulate".
4. Most of the real return on cash came when countries held gold standards in prosperous, low-debt periods.
5. Holding interest-earning cash carries "very large risks" late in debt cycles.
6. Reserve status needs a rival, monetized debt and an unstoppable run to end.

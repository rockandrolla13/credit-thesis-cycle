# Chapter 8: The Archetypical Cycle of Internal Order and Disorder (published 3 December 2020)

## Core Idea
The internal cycle of order and disorder is, in the author's words, "the most important cycle". Four forces rooted in human nature drive it: class struggle over wealth and power, the balance of power dynamic, the preference for short-term enjoyment, and the failure to learn from history. The author says the US is "at a tipping point" between manageable tension and "revolution and/or civil war". He does not call that outcome inevitable. This is a METHOD chapter.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Force 1: The wealth and power class struggle dynamic.**
  - A small share of the population, "the elites", holds most wealth and power. Wealth-holders and rule-setters work together to keep the order.
  - The class cycle: peace and productivity raise wealth unevenly → a small group holds very large shares → overextension → bad times hit the least wealthy hardest → conflict → revolution or civil war → a new order.
  - Change comes "gradually at first and then abruptly". Peaceful reforms come earlier in the cycle. Violent ones come later.
  - **Eight class lines**: rich/poor, right/left/moderate, race, ethnicity, religion, gender, lifestyle, location (urban/suburban/rural). Classes matter more under stress.
  - How to use: ask which classes rule, which are revolutionary, which are rising and which are declining. The author's example of a rising class: AI and information technology developers.
- **Force 2: The balance of power dynamic.** Five steps (section "The Balance of Power Dynamic"):
  - A. **Alliances form** until the two sides have roughly equal power. "The enemy of my enemy is my friend." Allies are not aligned.
  - B. **The struggle** decides winners and losers. Big fights need roughly equal power and existential differences. Mutually assured destruction produces stand-off. It stays non-violent only if both keep rules of engagement.
  - C. **The winners fight among themselves.** The author calls this the "**purge**" state. Watch whether extremes or moderates win inside each side.
  - D. **Peace and prosperity** under a clear power, which produces wealth gaps and excess.
  - E. **Increasing conflict** when prosperity fades.
- **Conditions for peace over war** (three): willingness to abide by the rules, willingness to adapt the rules by mutual agreement, and the threat of mutually assured destruction.
- **Force 3: Short-term enjoyment over long-term health.** The government debt path (author's chain):
  1. Politicians prefer the near term, dislike trade-offs and fear taxing.
  2. Lenders lend freely to a government with a printing press.
  3. Government borrows and spends until it cannot.
  4. **If money is hard or the debt is in foreign currency**: spending falls to income. Budgets are cut or taxes rise. Those with money leave. A **hollowing-out** spiral follows: revenue falls as tax rates rise, until "a revolutionary restructuring".
  5. **If money can be printed**: "more than enough money to service the debt will be printed and the value of the money will go down". It is a "hidden tax" that draws no complaint.
  - "In either case people suffer—they just suffer differently."
- **Force 4: Failure to learn from history.** The cycle is as long as a lifetime. The two ends of it are "more opposite than similar". So what people meet later in life is likely the opposite of what they learned from.
- **System plus people.** Outcomes depend on the existing order and on the people operating it now. The same constitution with different people gives different results.
- **Power principle, domestic version**: "Have power, respect power, and use power wisely, or leave rather than fight." And: "When in doubt get out. You can always get back in, but you might not be able to get out."

## Key Concepts
- **Internal order**: a country's system for governing how people behave with each other.
- **Elites / ruling classes**: those who own the means of wealth production and those who set and enforce the rules.
- **Ancien régime**: clergy and nobility were 2% of the French population with as many or more votes than the 98%. It ended on 5 May 1789.
- **Magna Carta**: the author's example of a law that "didn't matter much relative to power".
- **Meritocracy principle**: societies that draw on the widest range of people and assign responsibility by merit "are the most sustainably successful".
- **Top 1% wealth share** (footnote 2): US from near 50% in the 1920s to a bit over 20% in the late 1970s. UK from over 70% in 1900 to about 15% in the 1980s, about 35% now.

## Mental Models
- Expect the opposite. "The futures we encounter are more likely to be more opposite than similar to those that we had and expect."
- It is "a very rare country in a very rare century" that has no boom and no depression, civil war or revolution.
- Rules hold until they do not. The author cites Germany 1933-45, China 1949 and Cuba 1959 as cases where people who trusted rules lost freedom or life quickly.
- After any change of regime, look for the purge.
- "There is no free lunch." The author rejects the claim that debt and money can be produced without adverse consequences.
- Pay more attention to the forces of change than to present conditions.

## Anti-patterns
- **Thinking only of individual struggle.** The author says Americans under-weight class struggle.
- **Assuming allies are aligned.**
- **Assuming civility and rules will continue.**
- **Borrowing more because money is soft.** He says debt-financed booms "have historically led to depressions and civil wars".
- **Ignoring how today's people differ from past generations under the same system.**

## Worked Example
**England, 1200s**: King John wanted more tax. Nobles wanted to pay less and to have a say. Civil war followed. The nobles won, formed a council that became parliament, and wrote the Magna Carta. Power still decided the outcome. War resumed. A new Magna Carta came in 1225. Decades later the nobles cut off tax payments and Henry III gave in. Lesson the author draws: orders evolve through repeated tests of power over money.

**The author's father's generation**: people formed by the Depression and war would not borrow or buy stocks, and missed the post-war boom. People formed by debt-financed booms borrow heavily and consider depression and war implausible.

**The US after the 2020 election** (applying steps A to C): two parties gained roughly equal power (A). They fought for control under constitutional rules (B). The author says to watch the factions inside each party next (C). If extremists win, the parties move apart and a larger conflict is set up. If moderates win, the parties move together.

**Where the US stood** (the author's reading of lost charts): debt and money problem the greatest since the 1930s. Wealth and income gaps the greatest since the 1930s. Political gaps the greatest since about 1900. The US still leads and is declining. China is a rising comparable power.

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify the classes in conflict and whether the conflict is in the reform phase or the abrupt phase.
2. Place domestic politics on steps A to E.
3. For government debt, first ask whether the money can be printed. That decides austerity and hollowing-out against devaluation.
4. Monitor hollowing-out directly: who leaves, tax revenue against tax rates, service cuts.
5. The author's two pieces of advice: understand your real circumstances and the lessons of history, and "put deferred gratification ahead of immediate gratification".

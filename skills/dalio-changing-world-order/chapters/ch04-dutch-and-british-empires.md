# Chapter 4: The Big Cycles of the Dutch and British Empires and Their Currencies (published 26 May 2020)

## Core Idea
The Dutch and British cases follow the Chapter 1 archetype. Each rose on education, invention and capitalism, became the reserve issuer, over-borrowed, and lost reserve status after a run. The guilder collapsed in under a decade. The pound declined over more than 20 years through two devaluations. This is a CASE chapter.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The empire life cycle** (section "The Big Cycle of the Life of an Empire"). The author's sequence:
  1. A new world order is set by the winners of a war.
  2. A peaceful, prosperous period follows.
  3. People bet on prosperity with borrowed money. A bubble forms. The wealth gap grows.
  4. The debt bubble bursts. Money is printed. Internal conflict rises.
  5. A wealth redistribution "revolution" occurs, peaceful or violent.
  6. The leading empire is weaker relative to rivals. War of some type follows.
  7. New winners write the next order.
- **The pre-war decade.** "Typically before all-out war is declared there is about a decade of... economic, technological, geopolitical, and capital wars" as powers "approach comparability". The author applies it to the Anglo-Dutch case and to the 1930s.
- **Comparability raises war risk.** The author's charts show the US and China "approaching comparability". He says this is when "the risks of wars of one type or another are greater".
- **The guilder run as archetype** (section "The Dutch Empire and the Dutch Guilder"). A Type 1 system (coin, 1:1 backing) drifted to Type 2 (claims above metal). The shift "usually happens at times of financial stress and military conflict". It lowers trust and adds run risk.
- **Regional reserve currency.** After 1949 sterling was held by the Sterling Area under political pressure and pegs. The author says the UK is "best understood as a regional economic power and sterling as a regional reserve currency" in the 1950s and early 1960s.
- **Rising-empire assets outperform declining-empire assets.** Returns on British East India Company shares and English government bonds far exceeded the Dutch ones. The author says this reflected "virtually all investments in these two countries".

## Key Concepts
- **Capitalism (author's definition)**: "the ability of large numbers of people to collectively lend money and buy ownership in money-making endeavors". He dates it to the Dutch East India Company and the first stock exchange in 1602.
- **Guns and butter**: financing military and domestic spending together with the reserve currency. Applied to the Dutch in the 1780s and the US in the 1960s.
- **Too systemically important to fail**: the Dutch East India Company in 1780-84.
- **Sterling Area**: the UK and Commonwealth countries with pegs to the pound and trade in pounds.
- **Convertibility**: the right to change the currency into dollars or gold, on current and capital account.
- **Sterling Agreement of 1968**: members kept a floor on pound reserves when the UK guaranteed 90% of the dollar value.

## Mental Models
- Read an empire like a person's life. Markers show the phase, and behaviour should fit the phase.
- A reserve currency is like a common language. It outlasts the strength that created it.
- A reserve held under guarantee is not a vote of confidence. After 1968 holders of pounds "were holding de facto dollars with the British bearing the risk".
- A bubble company looks best early. Good fundamentals attract investors "even as its fundamentals started to weaken".

## Anti-patterns
- **Stop-gap support for a currency with a competitiveness problem.** The author calls the 1950s-60s swap lines and loans "essentially futile stop-gap measures".
- **Cutting the military to pay for domestic spending while holding an empire.** The author's main reason for the Dutch defeat.
- **Burdening the losers with debt.** Versailles debts led to the German inflationary depression of 1920-23. After 1945 the US gave Marshall Plan aid instead.
- **Restoring convertibility at an uncompetitive rate.** The 1947 attempt lasted weeks.

## Worked Example
**1. The fall of the guilder, 1780-1795** (author's numbers. He cites Quinn and Roberds, "Death of a Reserve Currency"):
| Step | Fact |
|---|---|
| Peak | Dutch produced about 25% of world inventions in the early 17th century. About one-third of world trade. Guilder over a third of international transactions. Population 1-2 million. |
| Seeds | Late 1600s: lost competitiveness, overextension. Debt service rose. Wealthy savers moved cash to British assets. |
| Lag | The guilder held up through most of the 1700s "solely because of the functionality of and faith in it". |
| Trigger | Fourth Anglo-Dutch War, 1780-84. The Dutch East India Company lost half its ships and its trade routes. |
| Monetization | The Bank of Amsterdam lent newly created guilders to the company and to the government. About 20 million bank guilder were outstanding at the start of the war. |
| Run | Coin-backed accounts fell from 17 million guilder in March 1780 to 300,000 in January 1783. |
| Defence | The bank sold its foreign reserves to buy guilders. Reserves were too small. Interest rates rose. The bank devalued. |
| End | 1791: City of Amsterdam took over the bank. 1795: French overthrew the Republic. Bank deposits traded at about a 25% discount to coin by 1795. 1796: company nationalized, stock worthless. Depositors recovered roughly 20% of metal backing, an approximately 80% total devaluation (footnote 41). |

**2. The decline of the pound, 1945-1968**:
| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1945 | War debts financed about a third of the war effort. Former colonies could not sell their sterling bonds. Convertibility suspended. |
| After the war | US loan of $3.75 billion, about 10% of UK GDP, in exchange for swift convertibility. |
| July 1947 | Partial convertibility. Heavy selling. Austerity and reserve sales. |
| 6 August 1947 | Attlee's "battle for Britain" speech. The run accelerated. $175 million of reserves spent in five days. Convertibility suspended by end of August. |
| September 1949 | Pound devalued 30% against the dollar. Competitiveness returned. Full convertibility by the mid-to-late 1950s. |
| 1960 | About half of world trade still in sterling. |
| 1961-64, 1966-71 | Central bank support through the BIS. Australia held 90% of reserves in sterling as late as 1965. |
| 1960s | UK sold about half its FX reserves and kept rates above peers with a weak economy. Runs in 1961 and 1964. |
| Mid-1960s | Average share of reserves in pounds about 20%. |
| 1967 | Devalued 14%. Central banks then actively sold sterling for dollars, deutschmarks and yen. |
| 1968 | Sterling Agreement with a 90% dollar-value guarantee. |

Why the 1949 devaluation caused little panic: the US government and Sterling Area states held most of the debt, and the Commonwealth devalued alongside.

**British peak**: 2.5% of world population produced 20% of world income. Over 40% of global exports. Over 20% of world land and 25% of world population before World War I.

## Key Takeaways
1. A central bank that lends new money to a failing systemically important borrower invites a run on itself.
2. Reserve status can survive decades of decline, then go in a few years.
3. Watch net reserves, capital controls and who holds the debt. Captive holders delay the exit and do not prevent it.
4. A devaluation that restores competitiveness can work for a time. The UK got 18 stable years from 1949.
5. The author's 2020 placement: the US is "the most powerful empire by not much" and "in relative decline". China is rising fast. No other power comes close.
6. Three post-1971 US debt waves are listed: 1982-2000 (dot-com), 2002-07 (real estate), 2009-19 (the "investment bubble" before COVID-19). Each pushed debt and rates to new extremes.

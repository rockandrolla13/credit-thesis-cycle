# Chapter 6: The Big Cycles of China and Its Currency (published 14 September 2020)

## Core Idea
China was a leading power for most of 1,400 years. It declined from about 1840 to 1949 and has risen since, first slowly and then fast after 1978. The author says China is now comparable to the US in trade, military and technology and lags in reserve currency and financial center. He stresses that Chinese leaders plan on arcs of 100 years or more. CASE chapter. The author states his sympathy for Chinese policy makers openly. Treat it as his view.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The capital controls principle** (section "China's Lessons and Its Ways of Operating"). A currency is used (1) domestically, where government "can get away with" fiat, and (2) internationally, where it must have real value. The test of a domestic currency is whether it trades internationally at the same rate as at home. The author's rule: "when you see capital controls being put on a currency, especially when there is a big domestic debt problem, run out of that currency."
- **The fiat-in-war principle.** "Get out of fiat currencies during debt crises and wars because they will be printed a lot to fund debt payments."
- **Average versus total.** China's average (education level, income) is below the US average. China's total is larger because the population is over four times as big. The author says many power comparisons turn on this.
- **Three-phase development arc** (the author's reading of Chinese planning):
  1. **Mao, 1949-1976**: win control, consolidate power, build institutions.
  2. **Deng and successors, 1978-2013**: build wealth and power "without threatening the leading world power".
  3. **Xi, 2012/13 onward**: move toward the 2049 goal. Near-term plans: Made in China 2025, China Standards 2035, five-year plans.
- **The symbiosis rule.** In a peace-and-prosperity phase the emerging power learns from, trades with and borrows capital markets from the leading power "until that becomes disadvantageous". The relationship changes when the emerging power is strong enough to threaten.
- **Three Chinese philosophies** (the author's short forms):
  | School | Aim | Method |
  |---|---|---|
  | Confucianism | Harmony | Everyone knows their role in a hierarchy. Benevolence above, obedience below. Education and meritocracy. |
  | Legalism | Rapid unification under an autocrat | Strong central state, strict obedience. The author's Western analogue: fascism. |
  | Taoism | Harmony with the laws of nature | Balance of opposites, yin and yang. |
  Marxism joined the mix in the 20th century.
- **Dialectical materialism (the author's summary of Marx).** Watch "contradictions" of "opposites" that produce "struggles". Resolution produces progress. The author says this resembles his own three forces and his "5-Step Process". He adds that he prefers what capitalism delivered (footnote 15).
- **Mao's geopolitical principle**: identify the main enemy, neutralize its allies, draw them away. It led to the 1971-72 opening to the US against the Soviet Union.
- **Transition vulnerability principle.** "When there is weak and divided leadership, especially during leadership transitions, enemies see this as a time of vulnerability."
- **Sound currency policy (author's definition)**: no big rises or falls against other leading currencies or against goods and services prices. He says China has managed to this since about 1985.

## Key Concepts
- **Mandate of Heaven**: a dynasty survives when its mix of philosophies works.
- **Dynastic cycle**: "typically lasted about 300 years".
- **Century of Humiliation**: about 1840 to 1949.
- **Tribute system**: weaker states gave gifts and received peace, recognition and trade. China rarely occupied distant states.
- **Iron rice bowl**: guaranteed basic pay and benefits with no merit pay. Reformers aimed to "break" it.
- **Reform and opening up**: Deng's two-part policy from 1978.
- **Triangular debt**: five state banks lent to unprofitable state enterprises under an implicit central guarantee. Resolved under Zhu Rongji with "bad banks".
- **Socialist market economy**: "socialism with Chinese characteristics".
- **Country = "state" + "family"**: the two characters of the Chinese word, as told to the author.

## Mental Models
- Americans see 300 years as long. Chinese leaders see it as recent. The author says the Chinese are "more strategic" and Americans "more tactical".
- Traditional Chinese war aim: build power quietly until the opponent capitulates without a fight.
- Opening a closed economy is "unconstrained water naturally seeking the same level". The author predicted skyscrapers in place of hutongs in 1984.
- Rich borrowing from poor: the Chinese earned "about one-fortieth" of American incomes and lent to Americans.
- Do not read Chinese policy through "what communists do". The author says seeming contradictions (more market at micro level, more state at macro level) are consistent to the policy makers.

## Anti-patterns
- **Stereotyping "communist China".** The author asks readers to triangulate with people who worked there.
- **Treating the 1800-1950 weakness as normal.** He calls it "an anomaly rather than a norm".
- **Relying on the index for precision.** The eight measures are "broadly indicative rather than precise".
- **Holding domestic fiat under capital controls with a debt problem.**

## Worked Example
**1. Monetary history** (author's dates)
| Period | System and result |
|---|---|
| Most of history | Metal coin, mostly copper. Silver for international use. Paper money invented in the 9th century. |
| After the Qing fell | Provinces issued metal money by weight. Type 1 held. Inflation stayed moderate. |
| 1920s-1930s | Debt grew. Defaults. Metal standard abandoned. Private silver outlawed. |
| Mid-1930s to 1948 | Domestic fiat printed heavily by a government losing territory. Hyperinflation. |
| December 1948 | First RMB issued in limited supply to end hyperinflation. |
| 1955-1971 | Fixed at 2.46 per dollar. |
| 1980-1994 | Devaluations totalling 83%, roughly 12% a year. Average inflation 7.8%. |
| 1996 | Current account convertible. Capital account not. |
| 1997-2005 | Pegged at 8.28 per dollar. |
| July 2005 | De-pegged. Managed against a basket from 8.11. |
| Since 2014 | Down about 2% a year against the dollar and 8% a year against gold. |

**2. Results by phase**
- Mao phase: growth about 6% a year, inflation 1-2%, reserves about $4 billion. 1952-57: industrial output +19% a year. 1958-62 (Great Leap Forward, drought, loss of Soviet support): economy -25%, an estimated 16-40 million famine deaths.
- Deng phase, 1978-1997: growth about 10% a year. Economy over six times larger. Inflation about 8%. Reserves from $4 billion to nearly $150 billion. Import cover from 60% to over 125% by 1998. Extreme poverty from about 90% to about 40%, then under 2% by 2013. Income per head was under $200 a year in 1979. GDP per head quadrupled by 1995, five years early.
- 40-year totals: output per person up 25 times. Poverty from 96% to under 1%. Life expectancy up 10 years.
- Trade: in 2001 the US was the larger partner for 80% of countries. Now China is larger for about 70%.
- 2009-12: debt growth "significantly faster than economic growth" after stimulus.
- Belt and Road: over $1 trillion, about 70 countries.
- Education: STEM graduates about three times the US number. One Chinese university in the world top 50 against 29 American.

**3. Opium Wars to 1949**: Britain paid silver for tea and silk, ran short, and sold opium. First Opium War 1839-42. Treaty ports ceded. Boxer indemnity of 17,000 tons of silver equivalent over about 40 years, secured on tariff income. Five causes the author lists: weak leadership, unsound finances, internal rebellions, foreign wars, acts of nature.

**4. Third Taiwan Strait Crisis, 1995-96**: missile tests, two US carrier groups in March 1996, no referendum. Each side believed it had prevailed. China then built regional military capacity. The author says he "would worry a lot" about a fourth crisis.

**5. 2018 changes under Xi**: power consolidated around "the core", Party control written into the constitution, presidential term limits removed, a supervisory commission created, "Xi Jinping Thought" enshrined.

## Key Takeaways
1. Check capital account convertibility before treating a currency as a reserve candidate. The RMB is 2% of reserves.
2. Expect long-horizon planning with stated metrics from China. The 2049 goal implies an economy about twice the US size (footnote 4).
3. The author judges Chinese economic policy makers "world-class" on debt restructuring. This is his opinion from direct contact.
4. Taiwan is, in the author's words, the "most consistently contentious" US-China issue.
5. China's debt is internal. The author notes this when he mentions over-indebtedness.
6. Globalization ran, by his dating, from 1995 (WTO) to 2016.

# Chapter 9: Delving into the Six Stages of the Internal Cycle with a Particular Focus on the US Now (published 4 December 2020)

## Core Idea
Internal orders move through six stages "like how a disease progresses". Symptoms identify the stage. The stage sets the range of outcomes and the kind of leader needed. The author places the US in late Stage 5 in December 2020 and says whether it crosses into Stage 6 is "too close to call". His "single most reliable leading indicator" of civil war or revolution is **bankrupt government finances**. This is a METHOD chapter with a dated US application.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The six stages** (full cycle "generally takes 100 years, give or take a lot"):
  | Stage | Name | Conditions | Best leader type |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | New order begins, leadership consolidates power | Post-war fighting and **purges** | "Consolidator of power" |
  | 2 | Resource-allocation systems and bureaucracies built ("early prosperity") | Institution building. Must deliver for the middle class. | "Civil engineer" |
  | 3 | Peace and prosperity ("mid-prosperity") | Debt growth funds productivity. Incomes exceed expenses. Broad optimism. | "Inspirational visionary" |
  | 4 | Excesses ("bubble prosperity") | Debt outruns cash flow. Luxury over investment. High military spending. Weak balance of payments. Wide gaps. | "Well-grounded, disciplined leader" |
  | 5 | Bad financial conditions and intense conflict | Classic toxic mix plus the markers below | "Strong peacemaker", or failing that a "strong revolutionary" |
  | 6 | Civil war or revolution | The system for resolving disputes stops working. Fights to the death. | "Inspirational general" |
- **The Classic Toxic Mix** (section of that name). Three ingredients:
  1. The entity is in bad financial shape (large debt and non-debt obligations such as pensions and healthcare).
  2. Large income, wealth and values gaps.
  3. A severe negative economic shock (burst bubble, act of nature, war).
  - Financial condition is the **shock absorber**. The size of the gaps is the **fragility**.
  - Problems hit the private sector first, then the public sector. "It is the government's financial condition that matters most."
- **The Stage 5 debt marker.** "The government has large deficits that are creating more debt to be sold than buyers other than the government's own central bank are willing to buy."
  - If the government **can print**: it prints and buys its own debt. Investors run from the money and debt.
  - If it **cannot print** (US states and cities): it raises taxes and cuts spending. Those with money leave. With large wealth gaps this "typically" leads to some form of civil war or revolution.
- **The tax-and-cut rule.** "Raising taxes and cutting spending when there are large wealth gaps and bad economic conditions has, more than anything else, been a leading indicator of civil wars or revolutions of some type." They need not be violent.
- **The red-flag index** (footnote 4: nine great powers, about 2,200 years). Flags include high inequality, high debt and deficits, inflation and bad growth.
  | Share of red flags present | Estimated chance of severe internal conflict |
  |---|---|
  | 60-80% | about 1 in 6 |
  | over 80% | about 1 in 3 |
  The author places the US in the 60-80% bucket.
- **Ten markers the author watches** (section "The US Now"):
  1. The classic toxic mix
  2. Decadent spending of money and time
  3. Bureaucracy
  4. Populism and extremism
  5. Polarization and loss of moderates
  6. Class warfare and demonization
  7. Polarized and distorted media
  8. Rule-following fading, power-grabbing rising
  9. Legal and political systems used as personal political weapons
  10. Fighting with fatalities
  - **Line into Stage 6**: "People dying in the fighting is the marker that almost certainly signifies the progression."
  - A further marker "that hasn't yet arisen in the US": conflict between states and the central government over their powers.
- **Populism conditions** (three): wealth and opportunity gaps, perceived cultural threats, and establishment elites not working for most people. "In Stage 5, moderates become the minority. In Stage 6, they cease to exist."
- **Revolution versus civil war.** A revolution changes how the system works and can occur inside the existing order (Roosevelt in the 1930s, Reagan and Thatcher in the 1980s). A civil war is a violent fight to replace the order. Footnote 15: historians require more than 1,000 deaths a year.
- **Polarization outcome rule.** Greater polarization means either (a) gridlock, which lowers the chance of the changes that would fix the problems, or (b) some form of civil war.
- **The policy scorecard** (section "What Would Good Look Like?"). Government economic policy is fiscal and monetary, each easy or tight. Easy creates debt and money that loses value unless productivity rises by more. Then ask what the borrowing is spent on. "What matters most is what the system puts the credit and money into."
- **Productive debt rule.** Lending and spending that produce broad productivity gains and a return above borrowing cost are good policy, even if the central bank prints as lender of last resort. Education, infrastructure and research "have paid off nearly all the time". Money given away without productivity gains is devalued.
- **Four things "good" would need**: (a) debts and non-debt obligations restructured or devalued, (b) productivity raised so incomes rise against expenses, (c) financial, educational and health disparities reduced, (d) education, infrastructure and health fundamentals improved.

## Key Concepts
- **Health index**: the average of the determinants, shown as green to red. The chapter says 18 factors and 108 readings (18 x 6). Chapter 1 said 17. The list is in a lost table.
- **Purges**: the second half of a revolution. Examples: Reign of Terror, Red Terror, Anti-Rightist Campaign.
- **Decadence**: spending on luxuries, often debt-financed. Fashionable in Stage 4, "grotesque" in Stage 5.
- **Scapegoat classes**: minorities, the rich or the poor blamed for the problems.
- **Capital controls in Stage 6**: controls on movement to other jurisdictions, currencies or hard-to-tax assets such as gold.
- **Diversification across countries**: countries are in different stages, so the world path is smoother than any one country's.
- **"When in doubt get out."** Doors close for people and for money.

## Mental Models
- A stage is a diagnosis, not a sentence. Being in Stage 5 makes Stage 6 more likely than from Stage 4, not inevitable.
- "Averages don't matter as much as the number of people who are suffering and their power."
- Revolutions inside the system can move as much wealth as civil wars. US 1860-1950 "almost completely changed who had wealth and power".
- Leaders of revolutions are usually well-educated and middle-class (Danton, Robespierre, Lenin, Mao).
- Internal and external wars cluster. Internal disorder invites foreign challenge, and leaders use external threats to unify.
- No system is always best. "If a society doesn't adapt it will die."
- The brutality reached in Stage 6 "will have been considered implausible in Stage 5".

## Anti-patterns
- **"It won't happen here."**
- **Judging policy by size of spending, not use.**
- **Favouring what is good for the whole without checking the division.** The author names free trade, globalization and labour-replacing technology.
- **Assuming a new president changes the cycle.** "The people have not changed."
- **Dating civil wars precisely.** Nobody at the Bastille knew a revolution had begun.

## Worked Example
**1. The redistribution of 1914-1944** (the author's chain)
1. 1865-1900: Second Industrial Revolution. "Robber barons" accumulate fortunes. Gilded Age spending.
2. 1880s strikes. 1890 Sherman Antitrust Act. 1893 depression with hard money. 1896 Bryan campaigns to break the gold link.
3. 1901 Theodore Roosevelt. Muckrakers. Progressive movement.
4. 1913: 16th Amendment allows federal income tax. Top marginal rates on income and estates rise to around 70-80%. In the US and UK the top rate "went from 0% to over 90% in 30 years".
5. 1914-1944: two world wars and a depression. Interest rates on debt legally capped. Currencies delinked from gold. Gold ownership outlawed. Capital outflows blocked. Rent and price controls. Money printed. Inflation cut the real value of fixed-income and equity assets. Businesses expropriated or nationalized, mostly in Europe.
6. Result: "almost all wealth was redistributed in the 30 years between 1914 and 1944".

**2. US polarization numbers cited**: 2019 Pew: 55% of Republicans and 47% of Democrats see the other side as more immoral than average. 79% of Democrats and 83% of Republicans feel cold or very cold toward the other party. Nearly half of Republican parents and a third of Democratic parents would be displeased by a cross-party marriage, against about 5% in 1960. 15% of Republicans and 20% of Democrats thought the country would be better if many on the other side "just died". Gallup 2019: 13% have "a great deal" of trust in media. 41% have a fair or great deal, against 72% in 1976.

**3. US states and cities**: the richest tend to be the most indebted with the widest gaps. The author names New York City, Chicago, San Francisco, Connecticut, Illinois, Massachusetts, New York State and New Jersey. Connecticut has the highest income per head, the largest wealth and income gaps and among the largest per-capita debt and unfunded pensions. 22% of its high school students are "disengaged" or "disconnected" (footnote 6).

**4. Two full internal cycles**: Soviet order 1917-1991, 74 years, with a failed in-system reform (perestroika) in its Stage 5. Japan's Meiji order 1869-1945, 76 years.

**5. The US reading, December 2020**: Stage 5, split near 50/50. A moderate president and a likely Republican Senate make gridlock or compromise most likely. Extremists outnumber moderates in each party, which pulls further apart. The author's "straw in the wind" would be a senior moderate losing a primary to a more extreme challenger.

## Key Takeaways
1. Check government solvency first. Ask who buys the debt other than the central bank.
2. Separate governments that can print from those that cannot. The second group faces taxes, cuts and exit.
3. Count the ten markers. Fatalities in political fighting mark the line.
4. Score fiscal and monetary policy as easy or tight, then judge the use of the money.
5. Expect capital controls and closed exits late in Stage 5 and in Stage 6.
6. Match the leader type to the stage. The author says disciplined Stage 4 leaders are rare because restraint is unpopular.
7. Stage 4 bubble logic: people treat assets that rose a lot as "good rather than expensive" and borrow to buy them. Footnote 22 names Japan 1988-90, the US 1929, the US 2006-07 and Latin American commodity producers 1977-79.

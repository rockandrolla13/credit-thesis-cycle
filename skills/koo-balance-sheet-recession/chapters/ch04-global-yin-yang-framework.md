# Chapter 4 (Section 4 of the excerpt): Preparing the global economy for both Yin and Yang phases

Source location: book pp. 250–252 (PDF pp. 30–32), full_text.txt lines 332–359. This file also covers the chapter endnotes. The section is short (about 500 words), so this file is thin by design.

## Core Idea
The cross-border fallacy of composition from Section 3 applies to the whole world. Countries in simultaneous balance sheet recessions will be tempted to devalue and export their way out, which produces a global deflationary spiral. The author wants an agreed code of behaviour, administered by the IMF, under which each country absorbs its own excess private saving with fiscal stimulus during a yin phase only.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Global fallacy of composition through competitive devaluation**
  - When to use: assessing the risk when several large economies are in balance sheet recessions at once.
  - How the mechanism runs:
    1. Each affected country has a strong incentive to balance its budget by devaluing its currency to raise exports (the source's wording).
    2. Economists will recommend this. The author's evidence: many academics, "from Krugman to Eggertsson", argued for a lower yen when Japan was in its balance sheet recession.
    3. If all do it, no one gains demand. The result is a global fallacy of composition and deflationary spiral.
  - Failure mode it describes: the 1930s. No framework existed, and the balance sheet recession that began in the U.S. pulled the world into depression and political turmoil.

- **Code of behaviour for yin phases** (the author's proposal)
  - When to use: as a benchmark for judging international policy coordination after a bubble.
  - How:
    1. Nations agree to do as Japan did: take responsibility for absorbing their own excess private-sector savings through fiscal stimulus when in this type of recession.
    2. Japan leads the initiative. Reasons given: it has the most experience and kept its problems inside its borders through the fifteen-year ordeal.
    3. The IMF administers the framework.
    4. The framework must distinguish balance sheet recessions from ordinary recessions, so that fiscal policy is mobilised only in yin phases and not in yang phases.
  - Why it matters now: there is no world government able to deliver stimulus for the global economy, and no such framework exists today.

- **Faster contagion under liberalised capital flows**
  - When to use: judging how quickly one bubble's collapse reaches other countries.
  - How: the author states that liberalised cross-border capital flows spread the impact much faster than in the 1930s. His two examples are the Asian currency crisis of 1997 and the subprime crisis that started in 2007.

## Key Concepts
- **Yin phase**: the balance sheet recession phase; fiscal stimulus is appropriate.
- **Yang phase**: the ordinary phase; fiscal stimulus should not be mobilised.
- **Global fallacy of composition**: every country trying to recover through devaluation and exports, which fails in aggregate.
- **Code of behaviour**: the proposed agreed rule set for individual countries in a yin phase.
- **Excess private-sector savings**: the saving and debt repayment the private sector generates in a yin phase, which the government must borrow and spend.
- **Volte-face**: the Financial Times' word, quoted in Section 1, for the IMF's shift from fiscal consolidation to stimulus on 27 January 2008.

## Mental Models
- Think of the world economy as a currency union without a federal budget. The eurozone problem of Section 3 scales up unchanged.
- Use the "who absorbs the saving" question for each country in a yin phase. If the answer is "foreigners, through a weaker currency", the adjustment is being exported.
- Treat policy advice to weaken the currency as a tell that the adviser views the recession as ordinary and national, not as a shared balance sheet problem.

## Anti-patterns
- **Devaluing out of a balance sheet recession when others are in one too**: it produces a deflationary spiral.
- **Applying fiscal stimulus without telling yin from yang**: the author wants stimulus only in yin phases.
- **Assuming other countries will do what Japan did**: the author says there is no guarantee.

## Worked Example

**The author's historical argument for an IMF role, step by step.**

1. 1930s: a U.S. balance sheet recession spreads worldwide with no framework to contain it.
2. After the Depression, Keynes recommends creating the IMF as a means of preventing global fallacies of composition.
3. Harry Dexter White's "much milder proposal" is adopted instead.
4. The author's explanation: Keynes lacked a full understanding of how balance sheet recessions are generated (argued in Chapter 5 of the book, not in this excerpt).
5. 1990s–2000s: Japan contains its own balance sheet recession with fiscal stimulus for fifteen years. Others recommend devaluation instead.
6. 2007–08: the subprime crisis is "fully capable" of becoming a global meltdown if mishandled.
7. 27 January 2008: the IMF Managing Director calls for a global fiscal effort. The author reads this as encouraging.
8. Remaining gap: bring in the balance sheet recession concept so that the IMF distinguishes it from ordinary recessions.

## Key Takeaways
1. Simultaneous balance sheet recessions create an incentive for competitive devaluation. Watch for it.
2. The author's rule: each country absorbs its own excess private saving with its own fiscal stimulus.
3. Fiscal stimulus is phase-dependent in this framework. It belongs to yin phases only.
4. No institution currently enforces this. The author nominates the IMF.
5. Expect more bubbles and more balance sheet recessions. The author states this as a forecast.
6. Open capital accounts shorten the time between one country's bubble collapse and its effect elsewhere.

# Chapter 1 (Section 1 of the excerpt): America's situation: the subprime fiasco

Source location: book pp. 221–239 (PDF pp. 1–19), full_text.txt lines 9–204. Written in early 2008; the "present" in the text is roughly February–March 2008.

## Core Idea
The subprime crisis is a bubble collapse that can drag the U.S. into a balance sheet recession, but one that starts in household and bank balance sheets rather than corporate ones. It therefore shows up first as a bank-capital credit crunch that only government capital injection can end, while rate cuts lose traction and fiscal spending has to carry demand.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Causal chain from the IT bubble to subprime** (Koo's reading of Greenspan's plan)
  - When to use: tracing how a policy response to one bubble seeds the next.
  - How:
    1. IT bubble bursts (2000). Aggregate demand falls. The Fed cuts from more than 6 percent to 1 percent, the lowest level since 1957.
    2. Housing, calm during the IT bubble, takes off because lower rates raise the mortgage a household can afford.
    3. Koo's inference of the plan: let housing carry demand while companies repair balance sheets; when repair ends, corporate borrowing returns, rates rise, and the housing bubble ends.
    4. It worked for three years. Most U.S. companies finished repair by end-2003. The Fed then raised rates seventeen times from June 2004, to 5.25 percent.
    5. The break: repaired companies still refused to borrow ("debt rejection syndrome", stage 4 of the yin-yang cycle). Long rates stayed at 4–5 percent while short rates reached 5.25 percent.
    6. Housing responds to long rates, so the bubble ran two more years.
    7. Ordinary households had already bought in 2001–03. With no corporate or prime household borrowers, Wall Street in 2004–06 searched for borrowers who would pay more than rising money-market rates and found subprime. Nearly $1 trillion went in.
    8. The inflow overwhelmed subprime infrastructure. Lending standards dropped at all levels, including the rating agencies. Securitization let originators favour quantity over quality.
    9. House prices peaked in spring 2006. Borrowers who counted on appreciation to refinance into prime fixed-rate loans were stuck. Teaser-rate resets produced a jump in defaults.
  - Why it works: the flat long end is read as a symptom of absent private loan demand, not of tight or loose policy.
  - Failure mode the author names: the plan assumed borrowing demand returns once balance sheets are clean. It did not.

- **Four-stage post-bubble process** (from Japan after the Heisei bubble)
  - When to use: locating an economy in the aftermath of a burst bubble.
  - How:

| Stage | What happens | Japan | U.S. (author's mapping) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Denial | Weakness assumed short-lived and local. Bubbles burst when the economy is most prosperous. | 1990–1992 | Summer and autumn 2007. Tell: the Fed declared a return to neutral bias after cutting in October 2007. |
| 2 Defensive turn | Losses reach balance sheets. Individuals save more, banks foreclose and auction. Fallacy of composition begins. Demand and asset prices snowball down. | From about 1993 ("balance sheet scare syndrome") | By end-2007 (rising bank loss announcements). Tell: Bernanke's 10 January 2008 statement that the Fed stands ready to take substantive additional action. |
| 3 Bank deleveraging | Each bank cuts lending to lift its capital ratio. In aggregate this worsens the credit crunch and the economy, so the target recedes. | — | Expected next: most of the 8,560 U.S. banks have no access to outside capital. |
| 4 Bailout tolerance | Pain rises until the public accepts capital injection into undercapitalised banks. Fiscal stimulus borrows and spends private excess savings. | Pain threshold late 1997. Injections 1998 and 1999. Fiscal stimulus from 1993 but always behind the curve. | Not yet reached. |

  - After stage 4 the economy stabilises. A self-sustaining recovery still waits for private balance sheet repair and the end of debt rejection syndrome. The text says Japan still struggled with it seventeen years after the bubble burst.

- **Four types of banking crises and four ways of dealing with them** (Exhibit 7-1; the endnote says the section is taken from Koo 2003a, pp. 170–1)
  - When to use: deciding how fast bad loans (NPLs) should be disposed of and which tool recapitalises banks.
  - How: classify on two axes. Is the crisis localized or systemic? Is there private demand for funds (yang) or not (yin)? See Reference Tables.
  - Diagnostic for "systemic": no private buyer appears for a failed institution. Northern Rock was nationalised in February 2008 after a months-long search. Long-Term Credit Bank failed in mid-1998 with no domestic or foreign bidder. Foreign buyers appeared only after capital injection and public works stabilised Japan.
  - Why it works: selling NPLs when there are hardly any buyers pushes asset prices down, weakens the economy and creates more NPLs. Rushing "destroys value" (the author credits the phrase to Stefan Ingves).

- **Capital injection without conflicting conditionality**
  - When to use: designing a type (IV) bank recapitalisation.
  - How: keep conditions to a reasonable level. Leave bank reform to regulators. Use the injection only to end the credit crunch.
  - Why: reform and ending the crunch contradict each other. Banks prefer to cut lending over taking money with strings. In early 1998 Japan offered capital with strict conditions and not one bank applied. The government dropped the conditions.

- **DCF anchor for post-bubble asset prices**
  - When to use: judging whether rate cuts will lift asset prices after a bubble.
  - How: burned investors refuse to buy until prices fall back to the discounted present value of future cash flows. Until then prices do not respond to rate cuts. Japan's real estate did not respond while the Bank of Japan cut short rates from 8 percent to zero.

- **Fiscal design in a balance sheet recession**
  - Spending beats tax cuts: a significant part of a tax cut is saved or used to pay down debt.
  - Seamless beats temporary: the headwind lasts until household and bank balance sheets are repaired. One pump-priming action will not do it.

## Key Concepts
- **Balance sheet recession**: as used in this excerpt, the recession that follows a burst asset-price bubble, when damaged balance sheets make borrowers pay down debt and refuse new borrowing. The full definition is in earlier chapters of the book, not in this excerpt.
- **Debt rejection syndrome**: companies stay unwilling to borrow even after their balance sheets are repaired (referred back to Chapter 2 of the book, not in this excerpt).
- **Fallacy of composition**: actions that are right for each household or bank (save more, cut lending) worsen the aggregate.
- **Credit crunch (bank-capital driven)**: banks cut lending to meet capital adequacy ratios after NPLs rise; called a special characteristic of a balance sheet recession driven by weak bank balance sheets.
- **Fat spread**: the central bank lowers the rate at which it funds banks while banks keep lending rates high, so banks rebuild capital from margin.
- **Speed limits**: Volcker's 2001 proposal (Toyo Keizai interview, 23 June 2001) to cap the pace of Japanese banks' NPL write-offs.
- **Teaser rate / interest-only / negative-amortization loans**: products with low initial payments and sharply higher later ones; they pull demand forward.
- **Behind the curve**: stimulus applied only after the previous one expired and deflationary pressure had already weakened the economy.

## Mental Models
- Think of household-led balance sheet recessions as slower than corporate ones. A technically insolvent firm must pay down debt at once. A household with negative equity has no immediate problem while it can pay the mortgage and income holds.
- Think of the transmission as two channels. Households that can pay: gradual negative wealth effect. Households that cannot pay (teaser resets, nearly two million people): default, foreclosure, NPLs, lower bank capital ratios, credit crunch, and foreclosed-house sales that drag neighbourhood prices.
- Use the price at which top banks raise capital as a severity gauge. Citibank paid 11 percent and UBS 9 percent on sovereign wealth fund capital, more than subprime borrowers pay.
- Liquidity is not capital. A central bank liquidity infusion cannot end a credit squeeze. Only government capital can.
- Treat a futures-implied price decline as partly self-fulfilling: it tells buyers to wait and stretched borrowers to default now and rebuy later.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rushing NPL disposal in a systemic crisis**: fire sales with no buyers lower asset prices and create more NPLs.
- **"Market solution" for a systemic crisis**: private buyers do not exist in type II and IV crises. Market solutions fit only types I and III.
- **Two goals with one tool**: loading bank reform onto capital injection makes banks refuse the capital.
- **Proposing a bank bailout too early**: Miyazawa's 1992 proposal met a public outcry so strong that no politician could raise bank rescues for five years.
- **Relying on monetary easing**: Bernanke in 2008 has no interest-sensitive sector left to stimulate. Greenspan in 2000–01 had housing.
- **Tax-cut-heavy, explicitly temporary stimulus**: it is saved, and it stops before balance sheets are repaired.
- **On-off stimulus**: Japan's intermittent approach raised the cumulative deficit by at least ¥100 trillion and lengthened the recession by as much as five years (cross-referenced to Chapter 4 of the book).

Notes on the tools:
- Fat spread is unfair (income moves from depositors to banks) but politically expedient because it needs no public funds. It needs loan demand, so it fails in type (IV).
- Type (III): no snowball risk, but NPLs are not the main brake on growth. No case for taxpayer money to speed disposal.
- Capital injection precedents cited: U.S. 1933; Japan March 1998 (stopped the crunch worsening) and March 1999 (let banks resume intermediation). The author says the 1933 and 1999 injections cost taxpayers nothing.

**Subprime versus the Latin American debt crisis**

| Dimension | Latin American debt crisis (1982) | Subprime (2007–) |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Hundreds of U.S. banks made huge loans to Latin American dictators | Many U.S. institutions lent recklessly |

| Public funds | Could not even be discussed publicly | Public vehemently opposed |

## Worked Example

**Greenspan 2000–01 versus Bernanke 2008: the same shock with different tools left.**

1. 2000: IT bubble collapse. Greenspan reverses position and supports the Bush tax cuts.
2. After 9/11: he urges fiscal stimulus worth 1.0 to 1.5 percent of GDP. The administration complies.
3. He also cuts rates. Housing, the most rate-sensitive sector, had not overheated and responds.
4. Result: fiscal stimulus plus a housing boom offsets deflationary pressure.
5. 2008: housing itself is the burst bubble. No rate-sensitive sector remains. The position resembles the Bank of Japan after both stocks and real estate burst in the early 1990s.
6. Expected result of aggressive cuts: little effect on house prices until they reach DCF value. The one positive channel is a weaker dollar lifting exports, which will not offset the banking and housing drag.
7. Prescription: a seamless fiscal package centred on government spending over the next couple of years, plus a bank capital injection programme prepared for the moment it becomes politically feasible.
8. Supporting data points: Congress and the White House enacted a $168 billion tax cut in February 2008. IMF Managing Director Strauss-Kahn argued for global fiscal stimulus at Davos on 27 January 2008.

**Housing futures as a forward read (Exhibit 7-2).** Chicago Mercantile Exchange housing futures, trading since 2006, implied a fall of more than 20 percent by 2010. The same exhibit overlays Tokyo and Osaka condo prices. The author notes the rise in Tokyo prices before 1991 matches the U.S. rise before 2006.

## Key Takeaways
1. Read a flat long end during a tightening cycle as possible debt rejection: repaired borrowers still not borrowing.
2. Classify a banking crisis on two axes before prescribing: localized or systemic, loan demand present or absent.
3. Only type (I) rewards fast NPL disposal. Systemic crises need slow disposal, plus fat spreads (II) or capital injection (IV).
4. Expect the capital injection to arrive late. It waits for public pain to exceed bailout aversion.
5. After a bubble, test asset prices against DCF value before expecting rate cuts to work.
6. In a balance sheet recession, prefer sustained government spending to temporary tax cuts.
7. New financial products that lower initial payments borrow demand from the future. The author draws the same parallel to the years before the Great Depression. A third of recent homebuyers used such products, by estimates the author cites without a source.

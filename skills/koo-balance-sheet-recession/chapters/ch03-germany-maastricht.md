# Chapter 3 (Section 3 of the excerpt): Germany's choice under Maastricht

Source location: book pp. 247–250 (PDF pp. 27–30), full_text.txt lines 302–330. Includes the sub-section "A new treaty needed for Eurozone".

## Core Idea
A country in a balance sheet recession that is barred from fiscal stimulus exits through exports, which pushes its adjustment onto its neighbours. Germany did this inside the eurozone because the Maastricht deficit cap makes no provision for a yin phase. The author's fix is a treaty that requires stimulus in certified balance sheet recessions and forbids fiscal laxity at all other times.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The fiscal-cap-to-export-surplus mechanism** (Germany after 2000)
  - When to use: explaining a large trade surplus in an economy with weak domestic demand and a binding fiscal rule.
  - How:
    1. German companies took on debt in the 2000 telecoms bubble, then rushed to pay it down after the collapse.
    2. German households raised their saving at the same time. The author calls this more serious than Japan's case in that respect.
    3. Corporate debt repayment plus higher household saving squeezed demand from both sides. Performance stayed anemic until early 2006.
    4. The Maastricht Treaty caps budget deficits at 3 percent of GDP regardless of the size of household saving and corporate debt repayment. Fiscal policy could not absorb the excess saving.
    5. Companies used the single market and common currency to raise exports to stronger regions. With no currency risk and no tariffs, a competitively priced firm can raise intra-region exports virtually without limit.
    6. Domestic labour costs were kept low.
    7. Result: the world's largest trade surplus, larger than Japan's or China's at the time of writing.
  - Why it works for the exporter: neighbours' demand replaces missing domestic demand.
  - Failure mode for the system: the author calls it exporting unemployment to surrounding nations. He says it is not strictly beggar-thy-neighbour because the destination markets share the currency.

- **Yin-phase exception to a fiscal rule** (the author's proposed treaty design)
  - When to use: designing or assessing a fiscal rule for a currency union.
  - How:
    1. An authoritative group of experts certifies that a member is in a balance sheet recession.
    2. Certified members are exempt from the deficit cap.
    3. Go further: require them to administer the necessary fiscal stimulus, so that government spending absorbs excess private-sector savings at home.
    4. Prohibit profligate fiscal policy at all other times. Deficits at will would damage the credibility of the common currency.
  - Why it works: if each affected economy contains its recession inside its borders, a eurozone-wide fallacy of composition is averted. The provision matters more in the eurozone because it has no federal government to do the job.

- **Currency union versus federation test**
  - When to use: judging the claim that a member state is "just like California".
  - How: check two conditions the author says the U.S. meets and the eurozone does not. (1) Labour mobility: people move easily between California and Oregon, but language divides France and Germany. (2) A federal fiscal authority: Washington would provide stimulus if many states fell into a balance sheet recession. Europe has no federal government.

## Key Concepts
- **Maastricht Treaty deficit cap**: budget deficits limited to 3 percent of GDP for member nations, with no allowance for private-sector saving or debt repayment.
- **Yin phase**: the phase in which private demand for funds is weak or absent because balance sheets are being repaired.
- **Yang phase**: the ordinary phase with normal private demand for funds.
- **Exporting unemployment**: the author's unflattering description of Germany's export-led exit.
- **Beggar-thy-neighbour policy**: gaining demand at neighbours' expense, normally by devaluation; the author says Germany's case is not strictly this.
- **Eurozone-wide fallacy of composition**: each member acting to protect itself in a way that worsens the union as a whole.
- **Brüning's balanced budget**: the 1930s German policy the author blames for the economic collapse that let Hitler take power (cross-referenced to Chapter 5 of the book).

## Mental Models
- Think of a balance sheet recession as excess private saving that must go somewhere. The options are a government deficit at home or a trade surplus that takes demand from abroad.
- Use the origin test for who should pay. The author's position: German companies joined the bubble, so the resulting recession should be handled inside German borders with German fiscal stimulus.
- Think of a fiscal rule as incomplete unless it specifies behaviour in both phases: restraint in yang, mandatory stimulus in yin.
- Treat institutional self-defence as a forecast input. The European Central Bank was established under Maastricht, so calling the treaty defective is taken as doubting the bank's legitimacy and meets strong resistance.

## Anti-patterns
- **A deficit cap blind to private-sector saving**: the author calls Maastricht fundamentally defective to the extent it makes no provision for a yin phase.
- **Forcing a balanced budget in a balance sheet recession**: it deepens the downturn and can put democratic structures at risk. Germany under Brüning is the cited precedent.
- **The California analogy**: it ignores low cross-border labour mobility and the lack of a federal budget.
- **Unconditional fiscal freedom inside a currency zone**: regions running higher deficits at will damage the currency's credibility.

## Worked Example

**Tracing Germany from bubble to surplus to a wage dispute.**

1. Shock: 2000 telecoms bubble bursts. Companies switch from borrowing to repayment.
2. Amplifier: households save more. Demand shrinks from two sides.
3. Constraint: the 3 percent deficit cap blocks a fiscal offset.
4. Adaptation: firms sell into stronger eurozone economies, helped by the common currency and tariff-free market, and hold down domestic labour costs.
5. Outcome: Germany reaches the world's largest trade surplus and is "finally coming out" of its balance sheet recession at the time of writing.
6. Second-round effect: a heated eurozone debate about whether German wages are too low. The author notes this is probably the first time France and Italy have criticised Germany for low wages.
7. Author's counterfactual: German fiscal stimulus under a Maastricht exception would have resolved the recession without the spillover.

## Key Takeaways
1. A binding deficit cap during a balance sheet recession redirects the adjustment into the trade balance.
2. Inside a currency union the exporter faces no exchange-rate brake, so the surplus can grow virtually without limit.
3. Read a large surplus plus wage restraint plus weak domestic demand as a possible sign of a balance sheet recession resolved through neighbours.
4. A sound union rule exempts, and even mandates stimulus for, certified balance sheet recessions and stays strict otherwise.
5. Expect the central bank created by the treaty to resist revising it.
6. The author rates the risk of repeating the 1930s balanced-budget mistake as no smaller today, given the orthodox preference for balanced budgets.

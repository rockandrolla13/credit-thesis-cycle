# Chapter 2 (Section 2 of the excerpt): The Chinese bubble

Source location: book pp. 239–247 (PDF pp. 19–27), full_text.txt lines 206–300.

## Core Idea
Whether a burst bubble becomes a balance sheet recession depends on who financed it. Money supply relative to GDP will not warn you of an asset bubble; deposit turnover will. China's bubble sits between the cash-financed Taiwanese case and the leveraged Japanese case, and its leaders respond slowly for reasons of legitimacy.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Deposit turnover as the asset-price pressure gauge**
  - When to use: monitoring for an asset bubble when money supply, velocity and consumer prices all look stable.
  - How:
    1. Compute deposit turnover = total withdrawals from the banking system / average demand deposits. The exhibits label it "Total Debits / Demand Deposits".
    2. Compute velocity of money = nominal GDP / demand deposits (the definition used in Exhibit 7-3).
    3. Compare growth rates. If deposit turnover rises much faster than GDP or velocity, asset trading must be rising. The reverse also holds.
  - Data sources named: for the U.S., the Federal Reserve publishes the series directly. For Japan, divide the "payments" entries of demand deposits by the amount of demand deposits, both from the Bank of Japan's "Amounts Outstanding of Deposits by Depositor".
  - Why it works: asset trades are not in GDP. Money supply to GDP measures pressure on general prices only. A rise in asset prices, especially financial assets, comes with a large rise in trading volume (churning), and the same deposit changes hands faster.
  - Failure mode it corrects: stable money-to-GDP and stable general prices gave central banks a false sense of security before both the U.S. Great Depression and Japan's Great Recession.

- **Bubble financing source decides the aftermath** (Taiwan versus Japan)
  - When to use: estimating damage from a bubble that has burst or will burst.
  - How: ask how much of the money came from financial institutions and corporations. A bubble financed entirely by cash is unlikely to produce a serious balance sheet recession or credit crunch.
  - Criteria the author applies to Taiwan: (1) the money was largely personal savings; (2) all major banks were government-owned and run very conservatively, so little bank money reached the market directly or through companies.

- **Transaction tax against churning**
  - When to use: restraining a market with high churn rates.
  - How: raise the cost of speculative transactions. Churning gives participants a false belief that assets are liquid and that they can always exit. A higher tax reduces turnover and forces a second thought.
  - Limit the author states: the tax does not fix the underlying imbalances driving the bubbles.

- **Damage-minimisation rule for bubbles**
  - Bubbles are hard to prevent. Minimise the damage by (a) raising the cost of speculative transactions and (b) keeping financial institutions and corporations out.

- **Legitimacy-constrained policymaking** (China)
  - When to use: forecasting the speed of Chinese policy shifts.
  - How: leaders who were not elected, did not take part in the revolution and lack major economic successes cannot afford a visible mistake. They act only when a policy is shown to be better than all alternatives. That requires academic justification and models, which adds delay.
  - Example: renminbi revaluation competes with import targets from the U.S. and a lower VAT rebate on exports. Revaluation happens only if it is judged better than both.

- **Savings-driven trade imbalance** (China)
  - Causal chain: state-owned enterprises closed or privatised → workers lose pensions and health insurance → they must save for themselves → household savings reach nearly 40 percent of national income → domestic demand falls short of domestic production → companies rely on exports → trade gap widens.
  - Remedy: a proper social security system. Slow to build for more than 1.2 billion people, and slower still to change behaviour because people must first trust it.

## Key Concepts
- **Deposit turnover**: total withdrawals from the banking system divided by the average value of demand deposits.
- **Velocity of money**: nominal GDP divided by demand deposits, as used in Exhibit 7-3.
- **Churning**: rapid rise in asset trading volume that accompanies asset-price bubbles.
- **Tobin tax**: the author's label for China's June 2007 transaction tax on share trading.
- **Hesokuri**: Japanese word for savings kept secret from spouses or family; losses on it go unspoken.
- **Corporate hesokuri**: undisclosed corporate funds played in the stock market, enabled by poor corporate governance.
- **Micro-level tightening**: targeted measures such as a substantial required downpayment on mortgages.
- **Fourth-generation leaders**: the Chinese leadership at the time of writing, which the author says faces constant questions about legitimacy.

## Mental Models
- Think of money-to-GDP as a thermometer for goods prices and deposit turnover as a thermometer for asset prices.
- Use the "who lost the money" test. Secret personal savings lost quietly leave bank and corporate balance sheets intact.
- Think of fast growth with a fixed exchange rate as a setting where land prices rise sharply without that alone proving a destructive bubble. Japan 1955–70 is the author's comparison.
- Think of Chinese policy speed as inversely related to the size of the decision. Bigger shifts need more justification.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging monetary looseness by money supply to GDP alone**: the ratio stayed remarkably stable through both the 1920s U.S. and 1980s Japan bubbles.
- **Letting banks and corporations fund a bubble**: this converts a price collapse into millions of damaged balance sheets.
- **Treating a transaction tax as a cure**: it slows speculation but leaves the imbalances.
- **Expecting quick, trial-and-error Chinese policy moves**: the author says a leader with Deng Xiaoping's standing could move a rate 10 percent and reverse 5 percent, but the current leaders cannot.

## Worked Example

**Applying the deposit turnover test to Japan's late-1980s bubble.**

1. Check the conventional gauge. Velocity (nominal GDP / demand deposits) stayed stable through the bubble (Exhibit 7-3). General prices were stable too. Conclusion on that gauge: no excess.
2. Check deposit turnover. The same bank deposit supported double the transactions in 1990 compared with 1985 (Exhibit 7-4).
3. Compare the two. Turnover doubled while velocity stayed flat. The extra transactions were not GDP transactions, so they were asset trades.
4. Confirm against prices. The exhibit plots turnover against the Nikkei and the author says the rise coincided with the stock bubble.
5. Watch the reversal. After the burst, turnover fell to one-sixth of the peak.
6. Repeat for the U.S.: turnover 80 percent higher in 1929 than in 1925, then down to one-third of the peak (Exhibit 7-5).
7. Policy read-through: because turnover correlates closely with asset prices, China's June 2007 share transaction tax is one way to damp speculation.

**China's political arithmetic.** About 400 million people on the coast have benefited from reform and about 800 million in the interior have not. The author argues that an election now would let the 800 million overturn the reforms, so elections wait until the "8:4 ratio" reverses. The first 400 million took twenty-five years, so he expects another twenty to thirty. External imbalances already need immediate action. His single most important question for China's outlook is whether global trade imbalances will wait for China.

## Key Takeaways
1. Track deposit turnover against velocity to detect asset-price pressure that money aggregates hide.
2. Before sizing the fallout from a bubble, find out whether banks and companies or private cash financed it.
3. A cash-financed crash can be large (down 80 percent) with almost no GDP effect.
4. Rapid property appreciation under fast growth and a fixed exchange rate has precedents in Japan and Taiwan.
5. China's high saving, and so its trade surplus, traces to the loss of the state-enterprise safety net. It will not reverse quickly.
6. Expect Chinese policy to move slowly on major shifts because legitimacy constraints rule out visible errors.

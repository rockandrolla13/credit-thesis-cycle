## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Four types of banking crises and four ways of dealing with them (Exhibit 7-1, ch01)
Classify on two axes before prescribing. Is the crisis localized or systemic? Is private demand for funds normal (yang) or weak to absent (yin)?

| | Yang: normal demand for funds | Yin: weak or no demand for funds |
|---|---|---|
| **Ordinary (localized)** | (I) Quick NPL disposal, pursue accountability | (III) Normal NPL disposal, pursue accountability |
| **Systemic** | (II) Slow NPL disposal, fat spread | (IV) Slow NPL disposal, capital injection |

- Author's examples. (I): 1989 S&L crisis. (II): Latin American debt crisis 1982, U.S. credit crunch 1991–93, Nordic crisis early 1990s. (III): Japan before 1995. (IV): Japan since 1996, Taiwan since 2000, U.S. 1930s, U.S. and U.K. subprime since 2007.
- Only type (I) rewards fast disposal of nonperforming loans (NPLs). In systemic crises, selling with hardly any buyers pushes prices down and creates more NPLs.
- **Fat spread** (type II): cheap central bank funding with high lending rates. It needs borrowers, so it fails in type IV.
- **Capital injection** (type IV): precedents are the U.S. in 1933 and Japan in 1998 and 1999.
- Systemic tell: no private buyer for a failed bank (Northern Rock, February 2008; Long-Term Credit Bank, mid-1998). "Market solutions" work only for types I and III.

### 2. Four-stage post-bubble process (ch01)
Use it to locate an economy after a bubble bursts. Japan is the template.
1. **Denial.** Weakness is assumed brief and local (Japan 1990–92; U.S. summer–autumn 2007).
2. **Defensive turn.** Losses hit balance sheets. Saving and foreclosures rise. A fallacy of composition starts (Japan from about 1993; U.S. by end-2007).
3. **Bank deleveraging.** Each bank cuts lending to lift its capital ratio. Together they deepen the credit crunch.
4. **Bailout tolerance.** Pain rises until the public accepts capital injection (Japan: late 1997, injections 1998–99). Fiscal stimulus absorbs private excess savings.

Stabilisation is not recovery. Self-sustaining growth waits for balance sheet repair and the end of **debt rejection syndrome**.

### 3. From one bubble to the next: the IT-to-subprime chain (ch01)
Rate cuts after the IT bubble (more than 6 percent to 1 percent) started the housing boom. Companies repaired balance sheets by end-2003 but still would not borrow. Long rates stayed at 4–5 percent while the Fed hiked seventeen times to 5.25 percent. Housing ran two more years. Lenders with no prime borrowers left pushed nearly $1 trillion into subprime. Use the pattern "tightening with a flat long end" as a sign of absent private loan demand.

### 4. Household-led versus corporate-led balance sheet recessions (ch01)
- Corporate: technical insolvency forces immediate debt paydown. Forecasting is relatively easy.
- Household: negative equity is tolerable while payments continue. The wealth effect is gradual.
- Those who cannot pay default. The loss moves to banks as NPLs, cuts capital ratios and produces a credit crunch. Foreclosure sales depress nearby prices.
- A central bank liquidity infusion cannot end this credit crunch. Only government capital can.

### 5. Capital injection rules (ch01)
- Expect it late. Miyazawa's 1992 proposal caused an outcry that silenced the topic for five years.
- Keep conditionality reasonable. With strict terms in early 1998, no Japanese bank applied.
- Do not chase two contradictory goals with one tool. Regulators reform banks. The injection ends the crunch.

### 6. Why monetary easing stalls, and what fiscal policy must look like (ch01)
- **DCF anchor.** After a bubble, buyers wait for prices to reach discounted cash flow value. Until then asset prices ignore rate cuts. Japan's real estate ignored a move from 8 percent to zero.
- Greenspan in 2000–01 had housing to stimulate. Bernanke in 2008 has no rate-sensitive sector left. The weaker dollar is the one positive channel.
- Prefer government spending to tax cuts. Tax cuts are saved or repay debt.
- Prefer seamless to temporary. Japan's on-off stimulus added at least ¥100 trillion to the cumulative deficit and up to five years to the recession.

### 7. Deposit turnover as the asset-bubble gauge (ch02)
- Money supply to GDP measures pressure on general prices. It stayed stable through both the 1920s U.S. and 1980s Japan bubbles.
- **Deposit turnover** = total withdrawals from the banking system / average demand deposits. It captures asset trading, which GDP excludes.
- Signal: turnover rising much faster than GDP or velocity.
- Readings: Japan doubled from 1985 to 1990, then fell to one-sixth of the peak. U.S. rose 80 percent from 1925 to 1929, then fell to one-third.

### 8. Who financed the bubble decides the aftermath (ch02)
- Taiwan 1987–90: shares rose elevenfold and fell 80 percent. GDP kept growing about 6 percent. The money was personal savings (hesokuri) and banks were state-owned and conservative.
- Japan late 1980s: banks and corporations were deeply involved. Fifteen years of balance sheet recession followed.
- China: in between (personal savings plus undisclosed corporate funds).
- Rule: bubbles are hard to prevent. Minimise damage by raising the cost of speculative trades and keeping financial institutions and corporations out.

### 9. China's constraints (ch02)
- Closing state-owned enterprises removed pensions and health cover. Households now save nearly 40 percent of national income. That gap drives the trade surplus.
- Leaders with weak legitimacy cannot risk visible mistakes. Major policy shifts need proof they beat all alternatives, so they come slowly.

### 10. Fiscal caps export the adjustment (ch03)
- Germany after the 2000 telecoms bubble: corporate debt repayment plus higher household saving, with deficits capped at 3 percent of GDP by Maastricht.
- Exit route: exports within the eurozone and low domestic labour costs. Outcome: the world's largest trade surplus. The author calls this exporting unemployment.
- Proposed fix: certify balance sheet recessions, exempt those members from the cap, require them to stimulate, and stay strict at all other times.
- The "California" defence fails on two counts: low labour mobility across languages and no federal fiscal authority.

### 11. Global code of behaviour for yin phases (ch04)
- Simultaneous balance sheet recessions tempt every country to devalue. The result is a global fallacy of composition and deflationary spiral, as in the 1930s.
- Rule proposed: each country absorbs its own excess private saving through fiscal stimulus. The IMF administers the framework. Stimulus applies in yin phases only, not in yang phases.

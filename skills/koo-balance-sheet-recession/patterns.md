# Patterns

Concrete techniques from the excerpt. Each names its chapter file.

## Banking crisis typing (Exhibit 7-1)
**When to use**: a banking problem appears and you must judge the right pace of bad-loan disposal and the recapitalisation tool. (Ch 1)
**How**: (1) Localized or systemic? Systemic tell: no private buyer for a failed institution. (2) Is there private demand for funds (yang) or not (yin)? (3) Read off: I quick disposal and accountability; II slow disposal and fat spread; III normal disposal and accountability; IV slow disposal and capital injection.
**Trade-offs**: slow disposal invites charges of forbearance. Fat spread taxes depositors. Capital injection needs public consent that arrives late.

## Four-stage post-bubble staging
**When to use**: locating an economy after a bubble bursts. (Ch 1)
**How**: stage 1 denial; stage 2 defensive saving and foreclosure; stage 3 bank lending cuts that worsen the crunch; stage 4 public accepts capital injection, plus fiscal stimulus. Use official statements as tells: a "neutral bias" marks stage 1, "stand ready to take substantive additional action" marks stage 2.
**Trade-offs**: the template is one country (Japan). Japan's stage dates span 1990 to 1999; the author expects the U.S. to move faster.

## Debt rejection read from the yield curve
**When to use**: the central bank tightens but long rates do not follow. (Ch 1)
**How**: check whether repaired corporates are borrowing. If not, low long rates reflect absent loan demand. Expect rate-sensitive assets priced off long rates to keep inflating, and lenders to reach for lower-quality borrowers.
**Trade-offs**: the excerpt offers this as the explanation for 2004–06 and does not weigh alternatives.

## Household versus corporate balance sheet transmission
**When to use**: the damaged sector is households or banks, not companies. (Ch 1)
**How**: split households into those who can still pay (slow wealth effect) and those who cannot (default → foreclosure → NPLs → lower bank capital → credit crunch → forced house sales → lower neighbourhood prices).
**Trade-offs**: forecasting is harder than in a corporate case. Outcome hinges on employment and income.

## DCF floor test for rate-cut effectiveness
**When to use**: after a bubble, before assuming easing will support asset prices. (Ch 1)
**How**: compare prices with the discounted value of cash flows. If prices are still well above it, expect no response to cuts and a possible hard landing.
**Trade-offs**: the excerpt gives no method for computing DCF value for housing.

## Capital injection design
**When to use**: a type (IV) crisis once injection is politically possible. (Ch 1)
**How**: keep conditions reasonable. Separate bank reform (regulators) from ending the crunch (injection). Prepare the programme in advance.
**Trade-offs**: light conditions look like rewarding failed bankers. Heavy conditions mean no bank applies, as in Japan in early 1998.

## Seamless spending-led fiscal stimulus
**When to use**: a balance sheet recession with rate cuts losing traction. (Ch 1)
**How**: centre the package on government spending. Sustain it until household and bank balance sheets are repaired. Avoid gaps between doses.
**Trade-offs**: larger near-term deficits. The author argues on-off stimulus cost Japan at least ¥100 trillion more and up to five extra years.

## Deposit turnover monitor
**When to use**: screening for asset bubbles when money-to-GDP and consumer prices look stable. (Ch 2)
**How**: compute total debits or withdrawals over demand deposits. Compare its growth with velocity (nominal GDP over demand deposits). A wide gap means asset trading is rising.
**Trade-offs**: shown for two episodes only (Japan 1985–90, U.S. 1925–29). The excerpt gives no threshold beyond those two readings.

## Bubble financing audit
**When to use**: sizing the aftermath of a bubble. (Ch 2)
**How**: establish the share funded by personal cash versus banks and corporations. Check bank ownership and conservatism. Cash-funded bubbles leave bank and corporate balance sheets intact.
**Trade-offs**: hidden corporate money (corporate hesokuri) is hard to observe.

## Transaction tax on churn
**When to use**: a market with very high turnover. (Ch 2)
**How**: raise the cost of each trade to cut turnover and the illusion of liquidity.
**Trade-offs**: does not address the imbalances that feed the bubble.

## Fiscal cap to export surplus diagnosis
**When to use**: a surplus economy with weak domestic demand under a deficit rule. (Ch 3)
**How**: look for corporate debt repayment plus household saving, a binding deficit cap, wage restraint, and rising exports to partners in the same currency area.
**Trade-offs**: the excerpt gives no figures for Germany, so the pattern is qualitative.

## Yin-phase exception rule
**When to use**: assessing a fiscal rule for a currency union or an international framework. (Ch 3, Ch 4)
**How**: expert certification of a balance sheet recession → exemption from the cap → mandatory stimulus to absorb domestic excess saving → strict discipline at all other times. Globally, the IMF administers it.
**Trade-offs**: depends on credible certification. Institutions founded on the existing rule resist change.

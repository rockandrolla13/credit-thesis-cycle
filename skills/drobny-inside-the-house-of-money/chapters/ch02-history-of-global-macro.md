# Chapter 2: The History of Global Macro Hedge Funds (Steven Drobny)

Type: CASE (a narrated history of past episodes).

## Core Idea
Each macro crisis taught the next generation a risk lesson. Keynes had 1929. Today's managers had 1987, 1992, 1994, 1997, 1998 and 2000. Drobny's repeated reading is that policy error creates the dislocation and speculators are "a mere symptom".

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two streams of macro managers**:
  - *Equity stream*: grew from the A.W. Jones long/short model (1949). After currencies floated in 1971, Soros, Rogers, Steinhardt and Robertson treated FX as a new asset class. Jones hedged macro out. They traded it.
  - *Commodity stream*: grew from physical commodity and futures trading. Commodities Corporation (Princeton), founded by Helmut Weymar, incubated or seeded Kovner, Tudor Jones, Bacon, Marcus and others. Its "generalists" were "indifferent to the underlying asset" so long as there was volatility.
- **Policy-error reading of crises**: in 1971 (Nixon), 1992 (UK), 1997 (Mahathir), politicians blamed speculators. Drobny blames the policy. He quotes Andres Drobny: speculators sometimes amplify and sometimes dampen volatility, but "they don't influence the trend".
- **The Greenspan put**: "an implicit option that the Fed writes anytime equity markets stumble". Drobny counts three writings: after the 1987 crash, the 1998 LTCM bailout and cuts, and the 2000 to 2003 cut from 6.5% to 1%.
- **The peg-break sequence** (same pattern in 1992 and 1997):
  1. A fixed or banded exchange rate sits at a level many think too strong.
  2. The peg forces a rate policy that fights the domestic cycle (UK), or invites "riskless" foreign-currency borrowing (Southeast Asia).
  3. The rate nears the band edge. Locals and companies hedge. That selling adds to the pressure.
  4. The central bank defends with reserves or rate hikes. Hikes in a recession "embolden" traders, because the policy is unsustainable.
  5. The authority gives up. The move is large and fast.
- **The LTCM lessons**: (a) history-based risk systems fail on fat tails, (b) positions correlate "for no other reason than the fact that they were in LTCM's portfolio", the "LTCM liquidity premium", (c) "there is no such thing as a risk-free arbitrage", (d) "The problem with liquidity is that it is never there when really needed."

## Key Concepts
- **Fat tails**: extreme outcomes more frequent than a normal distribution implies. Drobny ties them to herding and over- and under-reaction.
- **Convergence trade**: a bet that currencies and bonds converge toward a single currency and rate (Europe, early 1990s).
- **Flight to quality**: capital moving into on-the-run U.S. government bonds, widening every other spread (1998).
- **Portfolio insurance**: 1987 hedges that failed when futures and options "became unhinged from the cash market".
- **Diseconomies of scale**: the mega-funds had to trade only the deepest markets.

## Mental Models
- Think of a defended peg as a one-way option once the defense fights the domestic economy.
- Treat hedges written by local counterparties as credit exposure to those counterparties.
- Read "spreads blow out" as the normal result of a flight to quality, not as a model error. Figure 2.13 plots AAA and BAA corporate spreads to Treasuries for 1994 to 1999. The chart did not survive extraction.
- Robertson was right on value and closed at the top. Being right after the redemptions arrive does not help.

## Anti-patterns
- **Leverage scaled up as the spread shrinks**: LTCM's path.
- **Risk limits built on historical prices alone**: Soros said in April 2000 that "historical measures of value at risk no longer apply".
- **Judging funds by dollar gains and losses**: Drobny asks for net percentage returns. Soros reported over 30% a year for 31.5 years.

## Worked Example: Black Wednesday as a sequence
1. Parity 2.95 marks, band 6%. Many believed the rate too strong.
2. Germany runs high rates for its own inflation. The UK, in recession, must match them.
3. September 1992: sterling nears the floor. The Bank of England buys pounds as the rules require. UK firms hedge by selling pounds.
4. Lamont raises rates from 10% to 12%. Traders read it as unsustainable and sell more.
5. Evening of 16 September: the UK withdraws. Sterling falls about 15% against the mark over the next weeks.
6. Bessent's view: the officials "knew to fold their hand quickly".

## Key Takeaways
1. Each episode in this chapter ended with a forced seller. Find the forced seller.
2. Leverage plus a crowded position plus a rate surprise produced 1994.
3. The Greenspan quote on LTCM is the chapter's test: "abnormal returns are sustained only through abnormal exposure to risk."

# Chapter 4: The Family Office Manager - Jim Leitner (Falcon Management)

Type: CASE for the trades. The portfolio method is Leitner's own stated process and is reported as his.
Interviewee: Jim Leitner. Former head risk taker at Bankers Trust (1986 to 1991). Founded Falcon in 1997. Drobny reports about 30% compound annual return since then.

## Core Idea
Leitner combines the real-money habit of always being invested to earn risk premia with hedge fund risk control and leverage. He adds a few large option-like bets. He starts from an admission: "we are ignorant."

## Frameworks Introduced (all Leitner's)
- **The five-bucket portfolio plus absolute return**:
  1. Start with 20% of risk in each of five asset classes: equities, fixed income, foreign exchange, commodities, real estate. He does not fine-tune weights as Harvard and Yale do.
  2. In each bucket, earn the risk premium systematically and apply stop-losses.
  3. Lever each bucket in the way that suits it.
  4. Add an **absolute return** bucket for rare mispricings. He bets "up to 10 percent of our fund on one idea" that he thinks can double or triple. He found six or seven such ideas in eight years.
  - He says the fund is not yet fully invested this way. "This isn't an exact science."
- **Three differences between hedge funds and real money**:
  1. *Risk management*. Endowments diversify and rebalance, with no stops. Rebalancing into a falling market raises risk. A hedge fund cuts.
  2. *Leverage*. Real money takes none.
  3. *Choice of assets*. Hedge funds look for risk premia in more places.
  - A fourth point follows later: real money has no redemptions, so it can hold for years. Hedge funds manage to one to three months.
- **"Doubly right" rule**: long positions earn a risk premium. A short pays it away. "To fight risk premia, you have to be doubly right."
- **Three currency risk premia** he says no real money manager earns systematically:
  1. High-yield against low-yield currencies. The market overcompensates for yield on average (the forward rate bias).
  2. Short-dated volatility is too high because it carries an insurance premium. Selling it is like "running a casino".
  3. Long-dated options are expensive against future daily volatility but cheap against the drift in spot. The seller delta-hedges and earns the volatility premium. The buyer does not hedge and earns the trend.
- **Signal-to-noise in FX**: the euro went from about 0.83 (July 2001) to 1.28 (January 2004). That is 45 big figures over 900 days, or 5 pips a day. One-month volatility near 10% implies a daily range of 75 pips. Ratio 1 to 15. He concludes short-term noise mean-reverts, so run daily and weekly mean-reverting strategies.
- **The weekly straddle program**: every Friday buy one-year straddles. Allocation 6.5% of NAV. About 12.5 bp of premium a week across five currency pairs, choosing the cheapest that week. Sizing test: how much he loses if every spot ends the year unchanged. His study of FX options from 1992 found one-year straddles made money although implied always exceeded realized.
- **Numbers before story**: screen for cheapness with hard measures (price to book, earnings, cash flow). Then require a story, because "appeal is what drives markets". Cheap with no story can stay cheap forever.
- **Good country, bad country**: twin surplus is good, twin deficit is bad. Then ask what is changing and how it will express itself. A bad country does not require a short in every market.
- **Disconfirming evidence**: he trains himself to ask why a thing should go down, not why it should go up.
- **Cut off tail risk completely**: "things can happen for the first time that aren't in your distribution so they can't be priced."
- **The quant screen as research tool**: models scan 10,000 prices and give him 20 to 30 items a day that look nonrandom. He uses it to force attention, not to trade.

## Key Concepts
- **Forward rate bias**: forwards overestimate later spot moves. Discount forwards earned positive returns on average (boxed text).
- **RAROC**: Bankers Trust's risk-adjusted return on capital, "the first version of value at risk".
- **Real money**: unlevered long-only managers such as endowments and pension funds.
- **Commodity roll premium**: buy the second contract, ride it up the curve, sell. He cites about 6% real a year from an NBER study by a Yale professor.
- **Short-duration leverage**: the best Sharpe ratios sit at two years and under. Five times leverage in the two-year gives a 10-year bond's risk with a higher payout.

## Anti-patterns (Leitner's)
- **Standing in front of a devaluation for carry**: "let the truck go by", then resume. Central banks gave clear signals.
- **Changing position on news**: "News is random."
- **Expertise in one area**: "Expertise is overrated."
- **Reaching for yield by selling optionality** in the direction you think the market will not go.
- **Trading as if smarter than others**: it "eventually leads to disaster".
- **Monthly liquidity terms**: they force managers into crowded short-term timing. "That's not at all the way to wealth."

## Worked Example: Icelandic index-linked housing bonds (presented March 2003)
1. Screen: budget and trade surplus, central bank independent since 2001, inflation and rates falling.
2. Yield: about 5% real, against under 4% for the next highest (New Zealand). With inflation just over 2%, total yield over 7% against a short rate of 5.8%.
3. Holders: domestic funds in healthy positions. Government guarantee. $4bn outstanding. Low risk of forced selling.
4. Catalyst: the bonds were not yet euro-clearable. A change would bring European buyers.
5. Funding: half domestic (carry 1.5%), half dollars (carry 6%, with currency risk).
The pattern is his general one: positive carry while waiting, a named catalyst, and holders who will not be forced to sell.

## Key Takeaways
1. Earn the documented premia systematically. Save judgment for a few large asymmetric bets.
2. Buy long-dated options when you cannot time the event. Cheap volatility plus a "wicked" outlook was his 1998 setup.
3. He wanted a trigger before paying time decay. In 2005 he saw "all kinds of bad things lurking" and no trigger.
4. His 2005 worry: "how cheap credit risk has become", the growth of CDS, and yield-reaching through derivatives.
5. Go to the country before a 10% bet. Often a spread is out of line only because nobody challenged it.

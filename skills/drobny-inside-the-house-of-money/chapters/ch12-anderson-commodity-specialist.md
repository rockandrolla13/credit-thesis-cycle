# Chapter 12: The Commodity Specialist - Dwight Anderson (Ospraie Management)

Type: CASE for the trades. The research and sizing method is Anderson's own stated process and is reported as his. This is the most method-rich interview for supply and demand work.
Interviewee: Dwight Anderson. Manufacturing consultant and project manager at a printing company. Ran commodities at Tiger Management from January 1995. Founded Ospraie in 1999 with Tudor's backing. About $2bn at the interview.

## Core Idea
Anderson calls his style "global micro". It is "pure Microeconomics 101": supply and demand, the cost curve, who is low cost and who has cash. He makes macro assumptions against his own position, so the trade works even if the economy disappoints. He tries to invest in things that are "microeconomically inevitable".

## Frameworks Introduced (all Anderson's, some credited to Julian Robertson)
- **Macro assumptions against yourself**:
  - When short, assume great growth. When long, assume very low growth.
  - A classic macro buyer of base metals bets the economy beats expectations. Anderson buys only if prices rise even with a worse economy. A strong economy is then "an extra bonus".
  - Robertson would then layer his macro view on top and size up: if the short works even with good growth and he expects Asia to implode, "we should be short five times your amount".
- **Margins mean-revert, not prices**: "People often get it wrong and think it's price that reverts to the mean, but that is not the case in commodities. It is margins that revert to the mean." So you must be willing to pay up when the cost curve has moved.
- **The supply and demand checklist** he keeps running for each industry:
  1. How is the composition of demand changing?
  2. How is the cost curve changing?
  3. What are currencies doing to the cost curve?
  4. Who are the competitive players, who is low cost, who has cash?
- **Robertson's questions**:
  - "What are the economics here?" Prices "eventually have to go to the economics".
  - "Are we right in this situation? If so, we should be bigger. It doesn't matter that prices moved against us."
  - The conclusion must be simple, even if months of data support it.
  - "How much can I lose on that?" If he would lose twice that, double the position.
  - Reject "the market is telling me". "How come it's never talked to me?"
- **Sizing at Ospraie**:
  1. Size each position on "How much money am I willing to risk? How much could I lose?"
  2. Add a subjective exit: "If this happens, we get out."
  3. Aggregate positions on worst-case moves, ignoring correlation.
  4. Read VAR only as a floor. "If there's a very high VAR I know I have much larger risk on because VAR is dampening."
  - Example: long $100 of aluminum and short $100 of copper. VAR might show net risk of $20. He counts $200 at risk.
  - Reason: "in any one month, anything can happen—that's why we disregard correlations".
- **Risk by volatility, not leverage**: $100m of gold at 8% to 16% volatility is a different position from $100m of natural gas at 35% to 135%.
- **Low-cost assets only**: "you can't buy a high-cost asset cheap enough". With a high-cost asset you must get price and timing right. With a low-cost one you need not get timing right.
- **Management test**: management must have "at least mediocre competence" in operations, costs and shareholder returns. These industries throw off large cash flows. Ask whether management reinvests for returns or buys "a shiny new toy".
- **Pay up when fundamentals confirm** (Robertson's strength, his own stated weakness): Robertson bought Citibank at 10 in 1990 to 1991 and doubled at 20, because the real estate problems were resolved and it "wasn't going bankrupt". It went to 100, split adjusted.
- **Capacity discipline**: he planned to close at $750m and closed at $550m. At Tiger's $25bn, $250m was 1% of the fund and his investable universe fell to about 20 stocks.
- **Capacity scales with prices**: a weaker dollar, larger market caps and doubled oil prices raise the capital he can run. "When your inventory doubles, you need twice as much capital to carry it."
- **Lock-ups match duration**: his investment horizon is one to two years. Monthly redemptions force buying and selling at a cost.

## Key Concepts
- **Insecure confidence**: he spends 90% of his time asking what he has wrong.
- **Purchase price versus cost**: a firm that loses a three-year worker over 25 cents an hour thinks the swap is free. It lost the experience.
- **Cost of putting money to work**: breakage, commissions and market impact. Ospraie lost 1% on its first day with outside money.
- **CTA effect**: short-duration systematic money raises short-term volatility and has "zero net effect" over three months. He runs slightly smaller positions, more of them, for shorter periods. He cuts size in a position crowded by CTAs.
- **Materials-intensive phase**: at about $3,000 of per capita GDP or household income, people move up Maslow's hierarchy and raw material use explodes. The order he gives: dairy, then electricity, TVs, appliances, then cars.
- **Demand must be priced out**: if production cannot grow, price rises until demand stops growing.

## Mental Models
- "The cure for high oil prices is higher oil prices."
- An early supply shock helps less than a late one. Five years gives demand and substitutes time to adjust.
- Industry people give the best information. Consultants and sell-side come after. "We are here to plagiarize the best ideas we can."
- People cannot see "well beyond two years".
- A price move against him with no change in fundamentals is painful and not a reason to act.
- Be sceptical of headlines that make no sense: "Corn Prices Plummet on Rumors of Chinese Buying."

## Anti-patterns (Anderson's)
- **Managing a concentrated book off VAR**: fine for a bank with a thousand traders, "incredibly dangerous" for a commodity portfolio.
- **Trading what you do not understand**: an oil desk where nobody knew what "spud" meant.
- **Shorting valuation with no timing**: his personal tech shorts in the late 1990s, with short calls on top.
- **Buying the high-cost asset because it is cheap**: the refinery.
- **Acting on tips**: the man in the orange shirt who told him not to be long gold. Gold kept rising.
- **Buying junk credit when spreads have collapsed**: "it's difficult to get paid for the risk assumed".

## Worked Example: palladium at Tiger
1. Thesis: a supply deficit. In a market producing six million ounces, the deficit was over two million and growing.
2. Demand check: prices could more than double before users substitute.
3. Primary research: conferences, user surveys, supplier meetings, and visits to mines in Siberia and South Africa. Norilsk supplied 60% of world output.
4. Path: Tiger lost money being long "for three years in a row".
5. The only question asked each time: "Are we right that there is a deficit?" If yes, be bigger.
6. Trigger: Norilsk, the export company, the finance ministry and the central bank fought over export control. Sales stopped.
7. Result: palladium went from $120 to over $1,000 an ounce.
The research found the deficit. It did not predict the trigger. Staying power came from Tiger's structure.

## His 2005 Reading (dated view)
- Two drivers of world growth: the U.S. consumer and Asian industrial and capital investment. Both have to slow. He doubts it will be smooth over 6 to 24 months.
- Little spare capacity in oil. Losing a refinery for long would bring "$100 crude very quickly".
- Credit: "Junk spreads have collapsed." He buys corporate debt only in industries he knows and only at equity-like returns. In 2005 he found little.
- China: heavy research, almost no direct investment, for reasons of property law, governance and accounting.
- He expects "a couple more moves" like 2003 to 2005 in commodities over the next decade, with "massive booms and busts".

## Key Takeaways
1. Stress the thesis with the macro case that hurts it.
2. Ask where the asset sits on the cost curve before asking whether it is cheap.
3. Add up worst cases. Do not net them by correlation.
4. Check whether margins, not prices, are far from normal.
5. Match the capital's lock-up to the thesis horizon.

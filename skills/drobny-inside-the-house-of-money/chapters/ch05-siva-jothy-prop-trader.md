# Chapter 5: The Prop Trader - Christian Siva-Jothy (SemperMacro, former head of proprietary trading at Goldman Sachs)

Type: CASE for the trades. The trading rules are Siva-Jothy's own stated process and are reported as his.
Interviewee: Christian Siva-Jothy. Citibank from October 1987. Goldman Sachs prop from September 1991. Ran Goldman's European prop group from 1995. Interviewed after leaving Goldman and before launching SemperMacro.

## Core Idea
His rules come from one loss. In February 1994 a sterling/yen position of over £1bn, with short puts on top, lost $100m to $200m. He rebuilt his method around three points: never be short gamma, treat liquidity as the risk, and scale risk to the year's profit.

## Frameworks Introduced (all Siva-Jothy's)
- **Never be short gamma**: "Never, ever be short gamma." He includes carry trades, which he calls "essentially synthetic short gamma trades". Macro portfolios "should always be long gamma".
  - Why: a stop does not help when the market gaps. With a long put, the problem sits "entirely on the shoulders of the counterparty who had sold it to me".
  - Corollary: do not sell short-dated options to offset time decay. In 1994 he lost "many, many multiples" of the premium taken in.
  - Corollary: "actively look for options to purchase in times of stress or dislocation". Implied volatilities "are always massively undervalued during big events".
- **Liquidity rule**: "liquidity comes at a cost, and a lack of liquidity comes at an even greater cost!" "To me, risk is about liquidity." This is why he moved to the major G7 markets.
- **Trade your P&L**:
  1. Start each period near zero with small positions.
  2. Build a profit cushion first.
  3. "Trade up when things are going well and you have a high-confidence view."
  4. Never lever up a losing position.
  - His asymmetry: "There's a big difference between going from 30 percent to 20 percent, and going from zero to –10 percent."
- **Six-month reset**: the fund pays the performance fee every six months (year-end September, second payout March). The trader's P&L goes back to zero. Traders with a strong run cut risk. He uses it to copy a bank prop desk's profile inside a fund.
- **Three or four opportunities a year**: catch one and the return is high single digits. Two gives 10% to 20%. Three or four is "doing incredibly well". In between, do not trade. "Overtrading is as bad as running losing positions for too long."
- **The go-to dislocation trade**: "When I see dislocation in the direction of pain to the U.S. economy, I buy Eurodollars." He wants "the most obvious trade in the most liquid market".
  - Reason given: more than 90% of the time a major dislocation to the economy rallies fixed income. Long fixed income is "a synthetic long gamma trade".
  - He made more money long fixed income than short. Bond bear markets are short with powerful rallies and need perfect timing.
- **Daily diary discipline**: each morning ask why each position is on and what changed. He keeps a rolling one-year view only.
  - Exit when reasons or data change.
  - Exit a trade that makes money for the wrong reason. It equals copying a smart person's position: "you don't know why you got in or where to get out".
- **Portfolio structure**: 50% to 60% options. Medium-term options run three to six months. He adds overnight options near targets. Horizon zero to three or four months.
- **The bank's view of prop (why a bank pays for it)**: Goldman saw the prop group "as a call option and hedge" for the franchise, which is long inventory. The franchise loses in "tightening cycles and credit events". Prop must make outsize returns then and must not lose in other years.

## Key Concepts
- **Gamma**: the rate of change of delta. Owning an option is long gamma. Writing one is short gamma (boxed text).
- **Glass ceiling**: traders pushed to take more risk than they want "become totally overwhelmed". He knows nobody who made a quantum leap quickly.
- **Fixed payout problem**: a known percentage payout makes traders bank certainties. Goldman kept payouts discretionary to make traders push in dislocations.
- **Anchor**: rates always have an anchor. Nobody can say whether the euro's fundamental value "is one or two".

## Mental Models
- "The market found me." A large known position attracts the move. Leeson and LTCM are his other examples.
- Markets are "unbelievably slow to react to big events that there is no script for".
- History helps only in context: where in the cycle, inflation or deflation. He rejects the habit of repeating the 1987 trade because a market looks like 1987.
- "Once you've had a good run, you're at your most dangerous."
- Central banks "can buy time but little else". He expected the real problems after Greenspan left.

## Anti-patterns (Siva-Jothy's)
- **Selling puts on your own long**: he sold sterling/yen puts on dips. When it fell he "was selling just to stand still".
- **Everyone a prop trader**: Goldman had 500-plus prop traders in 1990 to 1994, "and they all had on the same position".
- **Blaming the market or the firm**: "they cut you out of the position because you were down X".
- **Giving investors a volatility limit or 1% a month**: "why on earth would you want to invest in macro?"
- **Travelling for ideas** unless you know the place well. A central banker will not tell you his next move.
- **Allocating a trader more capital than he is used to**: "that is when accidents tend to happen".

Five days later UK inflation data hit sterling. About 10% in weeks. Lost about $40m on day eight and liquidated. The cited Goldman book says over $80m. He accepts $100m to $200m. |

| Aftermath | The cross dipped the next year, then went from 130 to 240 over five years. "My timing was out by a year." |

| Fall 1998 | Saw the risk arbitrage group repeat his 1994 mistakes. A colleague up $50m would be down $50m if he cut, for lack of liquidity. Siva-Jothy bought Treasuries and Eurodollars. Best year since 1994. |

## Worked Example: what the 1994 loss changed
1. Before: no limit structure, no VAR, a record 1993 of well over $100m, and the largest position of his career.
2. The error he names: confidence after a good run, plus short options to pay for time decay.
3. The trigger: two unrelated shocks in one week. A stop would not have worked because the price gapped.
4. The decision: liquidate everything, then report. His boss said he would have been fired had he not liquidated first.
5. After: lower limits, a prop group cut from over 20 traders to 3 by mid 1995, and no down year from then on.

## Key Takeaways
1. Count carry as short optionality when you add up a book's exposures.
2. In a dislocation, the liquid and obvious expression beats the diversified one.
3. Size by cushion. Volatility belongs in the years you are ahead.
4. Ask of a known large holder: can it get out, and who knows it must?
5. In 2005 he expected "a lot of pain" because "we still have to pay for the 1990s".

# Chapter 13: The Stock Operator - Scott Bessent (Bessent Capital)

Type: CASE. An interview about past trades at Soros Fund Management and after. His rules are his own, or are credited by him to Druckenmiller and Soros, and are reported that way.
Interviewee: Scott Bessent. Intern for Jim Rogers. Worked for a Middle Eastern family office from 1985, then for short seller Jim Chanos. Joined Soros Fund Management in November 1991 and ran its London office for seven years. Launched his own fund with $1bn in fall 2000. He later returned outside capital.

## Core Idea
Bessent expresses macro views largely through equities. He learned from Soros and Druckenmiller that micro research reveals the macro picture, that entry point matters more than the stop, and that the size of a winning position decides the result. In 2005 he sees leverage "at every single level" and names corporate spreads as the place he would be short in an unwind.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Micro reveals macro** (Soros and Druckenmiller, per Bessent): "if you can find out what's going on at a micro level, you can figure out the big picture."
  - His example: about 90% of UK mortgages were linked to the overnight rate. A rate rise on Wednesday hit mortgages on Friday. So a rate defense of sterling squeezed every mortgage holder and could not last.
- **Trade the year's profit at the boundary** (Druckenmiller's plan in 1992):
  1. Find a defended level where the authority must take the other side in unlimited size.
  2. Lever up at that boundary.
  3. Size so that a move to the far side of the band costs the year's profit "but not more". The fund was up about 12% at the time.
  - Credit: "shorting the pound was Stan Druckenmiller's idea. Soros's contribution was pushing him to take a gigantic position."
- **Be there when it ends** (lesson from Chanos): "it's more important to be there when a mania ends, than spotting it early." Chanos was usually right and seldom found the catalyst.
- **Short on the way down**: if a stock goes to zero, the entry does not matter. Shorting $100m of Enron at $25 made as much as at $50. "It's better to have more conviction and do twice as much."
- **Asymmetry of shorts**: a long that falls 10% to 20% invites buying more. A short that rises 10% to 20% forces an exit. A losing short grows as a share of the book. A winning one shrinks.
- **Entry over stops** (from Druckenmiller): he does not usually use stop-losses. He aims for an entry good enough that he rarely has to stop out. He plans scenarios in advance: what keeps him in, what makes him add, what gets him out.
- **Never get backed into a corner** (Druckenmiller): after a shock, cut the positions with least conviction to keep the power to add to the best ones.
- **Keep powder dry** (the family office): they used leverage and kept "a segregated pile of money". In the 1987 crash they "were never forced sellers" and bought more.
- **Locals test**: "as long as the locals maintain faith, the speculators usually can't break a system." Capital flight by locals signals a real problem. Russia is his example.
- **Paradigm shift in what drives a market** (Soros): "Don't try to play the game better, try to figure out when the game has changed." His example: the dollar traded off the current account in the late 1970s, then off rate differentials under Volcker. He thinks it is moving back to the current account.
- **Soros's manager-monitoring rules**: pull the money if a manager who is down late in the year makes his trades bigger, or if his trading volume jumps. "The worst thing you can do when you're having a hard time is flail."
- **Return decomposition of a stock**: "50 percent of a stock's move is the overall market, 30 percent is the industry group, and then maybe 20 percent is the extra alpha from stock picking." He gives no source.
- **Screen as attention tool**: a system screens about 1,400 stocks and commodities weekly. He never trades on it. If ten Indonesian stocks appear, he looks at Indonesia. He keeps about 20 idea files and waits for entry points.

## Key Concepts
- **Whale watching**: the risk is not the whale. "Everyone sees a whale, runs to that side, and tips the boat." "Your biggest risk can be how your competitor is positioned."
- **Pain trade**: the market "is always looking for" it.
- **Technical analysis**: he uses it to see "what the crowd is thinking".
- **Batting average**: Soros's is below 50%, perhaps below 30%, with very large wins. Soros: "If you're right in a position, you can never be big enough."
- **Robert Wilson's rule**: "To go up 100 percent, you've got to be willing to go down 20, and you can't go down 20 with other people's money."
- **Embedded macro**: an equity manager who buys airlines makes a call on oil. An endowment with venture capital, oil, gas and timber makes macro calls.
- **Zero-probability event**: Russia's default on local-currency debt in 1998. "Economics 101 will tell you that a country will never default" on such debt.

## His 2005 Reading on Leverage and Credit (dated view)
- "In a low volatility, low return environment, people mistake low volatility for low risk."
- "We're setting up for another perfect storm." Everyone is leveraging to get returns, "at every single level".
- Layers he names: hedge funds are leveraged. Funds of funds, their biggest investors, are leveraged too, many "five to one". Prime brokers compete on leverage and offer to "repo out these Russian corporates for 95 percent".
- Pressure point: "I'm not sure whether it's in corporate spreads, which is where everyone is, or whether it's in emerging markets, but it's somewhere." Figure 13.2 plots emerging market and BAA corporate spreads to Treasuries for 1990 to 2005. The chart did not survive extraction.
- If every fund-of-funds investor redeemed tomorrow: "I'd be short corporate spreads. They'll blow out."
- Why he was not short yet: "you get killed while waiting".
- The flow mechanism: large hedge fund complexes take in money each quarter "and they just keep putting on the same trades, bigger and bigger". He compares it to the Janus 20 fund, which took in $50m a day and bought the same 20 stocks.
- Possible triggers he lists: a clearing broker problem, JP Morgan, Fannie Mae, a finance subsidiary of a major corporate, or a large continental European hedge fund with no clear regulator.
- Incentives: a $10bn fund earns $200m from a 2% fee. "Big managers are disincentivized to make money."
- Long-range: "At some point, we will have had The Big One." He does not know if it is "a financial asset depression or a real depression". Savers earn 1% while leveraged home buyers gain. "That's not sustainable."

## Anti-patterns (Bessent's)
- **Giving investors what they want**: at launch he ran no macro, wide diversification and many analysts. He calls this his biggest mistake.
- **Dedicated shorting**: "the house usually wins". Being required to be short in 1991 meant shorting Citibank at 12.
- **Adding to a losing trade**: his line about Meriwether. "When a trade goes against John, he adds. When a trade goes against me, I cut."
- **Taking profits quickly** to protect a year that is up 10%.
- **Long/short funds that are 150% long and 85% short** and have never been net short.

## Worked Example: sterling 1992 from the London office
1. Micro evidence: mortgages float off the overnight rate. He was already short UK housing stocks.
2. Macro inference: the economy was weak. Rate rises to defend the pound would fall straight on households.
3. Structure: at the band edge the Bank of England had to buy unlimited pounds.
4. Sizing: risk the year's 12% profit and no more.
5. Signal: rates were raised with the stated aim of defending the pound. "We knew it was unsustainable."
6. Aftermath: UK firms refused to meet him for a time. He credits UK officials, who "knew to fold their hand quickly".

## Key Takeaways
1. Find the household or company balance sheet that a policy defense will hurt.
2. Size from profit in hand, and decide the largest loss first.
3. For a mania, wait for the catalyst. Entry on the way down is enough.
4. Map where the leveraged money is crowded, and who must sell if it redeems.
5. His credit view is a 2005 judgment. He held no position on it at the time.

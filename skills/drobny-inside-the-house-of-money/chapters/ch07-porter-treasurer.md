# Chapter 7: The Treasurer - Dr. John Porter (Barclays Capital)

Type: CASE for the trades. The portfolio rules are Porter's own stated process and are reported as his.
Interviewee: Dr. John Porter. PhD in psychology (Sorbonne). Ten years at the World Bank treasury. Moore Capital in Paris until 1996, with his own capital to trade from 1993. Principal risk taker for Barclays' bank portfolio from June 1998. Drobny reports 30% compound annual returns gross since then.

## Core Idea
Porter runs a bank's interest rate mismatch book. It is not marked to market daily, so he can hold through distress. He calls his edge "time horizon arbitrage". His single macro variable is the central bank against what the market prices for it.

## Frameworks Introduced (all Porter's)
- **Time horizon arbitrage**: every other investor runs the same short-term style and cannot bear earnings volatility. He takes "the other side of market distress when people are blowing out of positions".
  - Small-door analogy: at a crowded party someone yells "Fire!". Everyone runs for the large door. Use the small door at the back, even though it is slower in pure physics. "When things get crowded, you need to adapt your strategy in light of everybody else."
  - Cost: "Most of the time, I'm not going to be invested." Returns come at the moment of distress.
- **Maximize current income per unit of VAR**:
  1. Favor the front end. Rolling one billion one-year notes for ten years beats buying 100 million 10-year bonds. Same VAR at equal volatility, "much more current income".
  2. Do a qualitative review of what makes each position right or wrong.
  3. Add positions that offset the rest if all go wrong, even if current correlations give no reason (his New Zealand example).
  4. Check the VAR effect, knowing VAR is backward-looking and assumes stable correlations.
- **The one variable**: "the probability that a central bank will move interest rates in line with the market." With risk in the front end, "the only thing that can really make me right or wrong is a central bank". "Everything else is just noise."
- **One-year, one-year forwards**: his core area. It is the most volatile point of the curve and "the one area where the potential for large overshoots still exist".
  - Rule of thumb: most rate cycles last 16 to 24 months. When a central bank starts moving, the market prices an acceleration. The back Eurodollar months extrapolate "more than what will actually occur".
  - Benchmark: with a perfect central bank the 10-year would sit at 4% while base rates moved between 3% and 5%.
- **Hold-to-maturity discipline**: he trades only instruments that return principal at maturity (government bonds, swaps, bank paper). No corporate credit. UK rules weight all credit at 100% for bank portfolios, so he cannot lever it.
- **Stimulus, response, reward, punishment** (from conditioning experiments):
  1. A stimulus draws a response. Each repeat draws a smaller response.
  2. The longer a response has been rewarded, the longer it takes to stop after the reward stops.
  3. Punishment extinguishes the response faster.
  - He says this explains markets and "doesn't necessarily predict where things are going".
- **Concept exhaustion**: markets "can't start trending until there's a new concept". A concept is exhausted when it reaches the paper or magazine covers. He tracks which concepts hold positions in place.
- **Curve trades are directional**: "The yield curve is 90 percent explained by the direction of interest rates." A steepener is a bet on a rally. He enters a relative value trade only when each leg could profit alone.
- **Concentration**: if one instrument captures 80% to 90% of the thesis, use it. "The more positions, the more potential for losses."
- **Stop-loss as the first rule**: "if you have a proper stop-loss, you'll never blow up". Cut when a trade goes badly for reasons you do not understand, or for reasons other than your entry reasons.

## Key Concepts
- **Accrual accounting**: a UK bank books current income (carry) on these positions, not mark-to-market.
- **DV01**: value of one basis point. His Bund/Gilt trade had a DV01 of £800,000, about £650m notional.
- **Gap risk**: lend long, borrow short. His group's mandate.
- **3-6-3 rule**: pay 3% on deposits, lend at 6%, golf by 3 o'clock (boxed text).
- **Minimum Funding Requirement**: UK pension rule after the Maxwell scandal that measured liabilities off long Gilt yields. Pension funds bought long Gilts and pushed yields down.
- **Central banks as counterindicators**: "When central banks engage in market-based activities, they are great counterindicators."

## Mental Models
- Two adages he says he respects: "One man's staying power is another man's foolish lack of discipline." "The road to ruin is paved with positive carry."
- "Once you start to rationalize, you're wrong."
- Bacon's line: "You're not here to be right, you're here to make money."
- "Things can always get worse than you can possibly imagine."
- Ask central bankers only "what they are looking at". They need not slip up for him to profit.
- The pit trader who made $10m on an inflation release and then asked "what was the number?" is his picture of markets as psychology.

## His 2005 Reading on Credit and Hedge Funds (dated view)
- "Relative value hedge funds and hedge funds in general are all about leveraged selling of volatility." He does the opposite.
- Evidence of crowding: "how tight high yield spreads are". Managers must earn 50 bp a month or lose investors. Credit spreads are one way, "but the risk is tremendous".
- "Low levels of volatility in the past have preceded major market disruptions—1998 being the classic example." Figure 7.2 plots high yield spreads to Treasuries for 1991 to 2005. The chart did not survive extraction.
- He calls 2005 a cyclical hedge fund bubble. The 2% fee makes launching too easy.
- He would go short volatility only against a long front-end position, because front-end rates should rally in a blowup.
- He expects a trading market, not a trending one, with a downward bias in stocks.

## Anti-patterns (Porter's)
- **Jumping on the trend under pressure to perform**: his 1993 long bond position after his short failed.
- **Tripling size after one good year**: 1994.
- **Adding a hedge to satisfy a VAR limit** when you expect the hedge to lose.
- **Working a distressed exit through friends**: use a broker you have never spoken to, who wants only the commission.
- **Trusting numbers because they are numbers**: his reading of LTCM. They "lost sight of ... social psychology".
- **Trading outside your area of expertise.**

## Worked Example: Bund against Gilt, 1998 to 2004
1. Anomaly: 30-year Gilts traded expensive to Bunds. He knew the cause: pension buying forced by the Minimum Funding Requirement.
2. Carry: about 250 bp positive.
3. Risks he listed: the trade was crowded. UK base rates could fall below European rates, which had no precedent.
4. Free option: UK entry to the euro would collapse the spread.
5. Size: DV01 £800,000, about a quarter of portfolio risk.
6. What happened: LTCM and others unwound the same trade. He was down £60m mark-to-market at one point.
7. Response: during the blowout he locked in 350 bp of carry for two years. The net present value of the book never went negative.
8. Result: most of the position came off in mid 2004 for about £180m.
What made it possible: accrual accounting, a known cause for the adverse move, and a holder who could not be forced out.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask who can hold a position through distress. That holder earns the premium the forced sellers pay.
2. Compare forwards with the likely central bank path. Overshoot sits about one year forward.
3. Read tight high yield spreads plus low volatility as a crowding sign, per Porter in 2005.
4. Treat every curve trade as a directional view.
5. Keep VAR, and overrule it with judgment when it asks for a losing trade.

# Chapter 6: The Researcher - Dr. Andres Drobny (Drobny Global Advisors)

Type: METHOD in the main (how to turn a view into a trade), with CASE material on his own trades.
Interviewee: Dr. Andres Drobny. Academic economist at Cambridge and London. Joined Bankers Trust in August 1987, head of research from May 1989. Credit Suisse First Boston from summer 1992. Retired in spring 1998. He is the author's business partner and no relation. He does not manage outside money.

## Core Idea
"Talk is cheap." A view is worth nothing until it becomes a trade with good risk/reward. He finds trades by comparing his fundamental view of a process with the market's perception and positioning. The best ones sit where few are looking and the market leans the other way.

## Frameworks Introduced (all Andres Drobny's)
- **View versus trade**:
  1. Build a fundamental view of the process.
  2. Compare it with what the market believes and talks about.
  3. Look for what is "missed or misread". The market looks at the wrong variable, or it misses one.
  4. Express the gap as a specific trade with defined boundaries.
  - Rule: "If your views are a little confused, you really want to back off on your trades."
  - Rule: be confident in a trade (its risk/reward), never in a view. A view "gets proven in the future".
- **Biases and trades (his newsletter discipline)**: a bias is the talk. A trade is a risk/reward bet "not inconsistent" with the bias.
  - With a bias, he allows only no position or a position in line with it.
  - To trade against a bias he must first move the bias to neutral. Neutral allows long or short.
  - Purpose: it forces thought about the view and avoids "market whiplash".
- **Positioning from flows and talk**:
  - He gauges positioning from "what people are talking about", not short-term overbought and oversold measures. He looks back two, three or five years.
  - Flow identity for currencies: a currency with a rising current account or basic balance deficit "must mean that there's a long building up". A surplus currency that is falling means "a big short building".
  - If everyone talks his view, he pauses. He missed that year's dollar rally because many talked about it.
- **Three catalysts for a trend change**: after a big trend with the market leaning one way, look for (1) a central bank event, (2) an economic event, or (3) "pure market exhaustion". He finds the first two easier. He calls hunting exhaustion "very tough".
- **Carry risk/reward test (the ERM lesson)**: a banded regime shifts adjustment onto the forwards. High yielders at the bottom of the band are a buy while policy holds and carry is large. When rate gaps narrow, carry shrinks while system risk builds. Leave when "the reward to the carry trade became almost insignificant" and holders are leveraged.
- **Expected-return stop arithmetic**:
  1. Each trade is an expected return set by its boundaries (target and stop) and the probability attached.
  2. Minimum risk/return of 4 to 1. "The great traders think in terms of 8 to 1 or 10 to 1."
  3. After a stop, re-enter only "with a very tight stop". Add the loss already taken.
  4. After hitting the 1 a couple of times, a 4 to 1 trade is a 4 to 3 trade. Move on, "even if you don't want to".
- **80/20 portfolio**: 80% of the money in carry or lower-risk income that rarely moves. 20% hunts "those 20 to 30 percent events".
- **Carry trader plus gamma trader**: diversify a desk or a fund-of-funds book by how managers trade, not by market or region. One earns income in quiet times. One earns on the big move. "The great trades in global macro are when you combine carry with gamma", such as a cheap high-yielding currency.
- **"Brain damage" rule**: after a run that stops out all positions, leave markets for about a month. Take three to four weeks away once a year in any case.
- **Favorite trade question**: "If you had to do one trade to get rich today, what would it be?" A trade that can make 2% shows a small thinker. One that can make 20% to 30% shows someone who sees the big move.

## Key Concepts
- **Equilibrium shift**: "The biggest moves occur when we move from one equilibrium to the next." Most analysts describe the current one.
- **Pressures, not horizon**: he thinks "in terms of pressures rather than time horizon". Time matters only through carry.
- **Speculators**: they amplify or dampen volatility but "don't influence the trend". "Underlying pressures combined with policy decisions drive market events."
- **Analytical distance**: he does not travel. "Domestics misunderstand their own economies." He asks an equity person about bonds.
- **Global macro versus relative value**: "intersecting sets". A spread with a macro reason is macro. A 3 bp bond mispricing is not.
- **Bifurcation**: capital inflow brings weaker managers. A few stars charge 3% to 4% fees. The rest become commoditized.

## Mental Models
- "If you're going to panic in markets, panic early."
- His ERM lesson: "never to underestimate how far a move can go". Cable went from 2 to 1.5 after sterling broke.
- The great traders "make a little money even when they're wrong". The worst sign is a manager who is right and makes no money.
- Lever up when hot. Cut size when cold. For him that means writing less.
- Write investor letters to yourself. Where the text does not make sense, you learn what you do not understand.

## Anti-patterns (Andres Drobny's)
- **Extrapolating a trend forever**: he did it with the dollar in 1988.
- **Staying in a trade because it has been great**: ERM carry holders in early 1992.
- **Historical rules of thumb** such as "The yield curve is flat, therefore it tells you it's a recession". He gives them "very little value".
- **Letters that say "the markets were irrational"**: irrationality is an opportunity, not an excuse.
- **Overconfidence in a view** in a monthly letter: "a warning sign".
- **Buying the currency that already devalued** as the safe one: his Finnish markka trade.

## Worked Example: timing the dollar fall (April 2002)
1. The view (dollar down) had been wrong through 2001. Equities fell and the Fed cut, but the dollar held.
2. He asked "Why now?" and looked for the reason the view had failed.
3. Hypothesis: the 2001 profit collapse squeezed U.S. company cash. Firms repatriated capital, which held the dollar up.
4. Market talk in early 2002: companies are flush with cash, good for recovery.
5. His translation: firms no longer need to repatriate. The support is gone.
6. Trade: long euro against the dollar. Figure 6.6 marks the presentation near the start of the move.
The lesson he draws: "The beauty of the trade was not the view... The beauty was the timing."

## His 2005 Reading (dated view, not method)
- Low rates and liquidity produced "generalized overvaluation of assets". It is well known, so it is harder to trade than 1991.
- Main hypothesis: "it all ends in tears". Either recovery lifts rates until assets fall and deflation returns, or assets burst alone. Inflating the debt away "seems hard to achieve".
- Paradox: those who expect house prices to fall are the optimists, because rates could then stay low. The optimistic growth case forces rates up more than priced.
- He calls his preferred path "a bumpy path to deflation" and admits he is confused.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask of every view: what is the trade, where is the stop, what is the ratio?
2. Count prior stopped-out losses against the same idea.
3. Check carry against system risk. Thin carry with leveraged holders is the exit sign.
4. Look for the reason a correct-looking view failed. Its removal is the timing signal.
5. State when you do not understand. Then trade and write less.

# Chapter 10: The Floor Trader - Yra Harris (Praxis Trading, Chicago Mercantile Exchange)

Type: CASE. An interview about pit trading and past trades. His rules are his own and are reported as his. The chapter is light on numbers.
Interviewee: Yra Harris. On the CME floor since 1977. Ran a currency futures arbitrage business until 1993. Trades only his own money at the time of the interview.

## Core Idea
Harris trades a mental "matrix" of where global money flows. He holds that "the market is right and you are wrong". His edge is taking losses fast and waiting for a move to develop.

## Frameworks Introduced (all Harris's)
- **The matrix**: "my mental picture of how money flows around the world, the flow of funds on a global basis seeking out the highest risk-adjusted return." Money is always moving somewhere, "from oil to currencies to metals or to stocks". He follows it.
  - Output: a directional bias. He does not set price targets. "That is what technicians do."
  - Built 25 years before the interview. He gives no formula.
- **Fear regime**: money stops flowing only when it is afraid, as in 1998 or after 11 September. Then it "stops thinking about return" and "just wants to save its principal". It heads to Switzerland.
  - "Money is fascist. It craves stability more than anything else. Nothing bothers money more than uncertainty."
  - The most interesting times in macro are when money "turns in on itself and heads for the exit". Asia 1997 is his case study.
- **Second-order devaluation effect** (the matrix at work): a devaluation raises local-currency income for exporters paid in dollars. They sell and plant more. Supply rises and the commodity price falls. See worked example.
- **Loss-taking rule** (from his mentor Lenny Feldman): "just do it, because you can always get back in". Never let ego stop you saying you are wrong.
  - "If you're right at the wrong time, you're wrong."
  - The cocoa story: a trader bought at 38 cents on a correct crop report, doubled at 36 and at 34, and was broke at 32. Cocoa traded at $1.50 a year and a half later.
- **The prayer trade**: holding a loser to get back to even. "If breaking even is your goal, you're not trading anymore."
  - His remedy: "Take a deep breath, step back, clear the books, and start over again." "There is always another train leaving."
- **Probe trades**: "80 percent of my day is being wrong." He sends out small positions "like a reconnaissance mission". The 20% must far outweigh the 80%.
- **Focus rule**: he trades best when focused on three or four areas where the matrix "is onto something", with small "incidentals" around them. Twelve positions means he is bored. "This is a terrible business to be bored in."
- **Too big for the playground**: anyone too big for a market goes down. His examples are Soros, Tiger, Rich Dennis and the Hunt brothers. In 2005 he asks how PIMCO moves its positions while announcing them.

## Key Concepts
- **Slower Fool Theory** (Richard Dennis, 1987, boxed text): investors hold paper they think is bad and plan to sell faster than the other fools. It works while everyone thinks the crisis is in the future and someone else's. Dennis: "I would hate to be caught with the right theory and the wrong position."
- **Geopolitics**: Harris says "global macro" is a new term for it.
- **Pure arbitrage**: his 1977 business matched currency futures against cash plus forward on exact dates. Spreads narrowed until the edge did not justify the risk of "gambler's ruin". He closed it in 1993.
- **Options timing** (Myron Scholes's reply to him): options are slow to pick up a trend because its start is a grind. Volatility is marked up at the end, when everyone is in.
- **Singles and doubles**: he does not swing for home runs. He calls his habit of taking positions off a weakness. "The big successes know how to push."

## His 2005 Reading on Credit and Systemic Risk (dated view)
- "Hedge funds have taken over the role of global financing." Banks are slow and methodical. Hedge funds are in or out at "a push of the button".
- He asks what banks now do, and answers: perhaps finance hedge funds. Then taxpayers "bear the brunt of any fallout".
- "LTCM was a drop in the bucket compared to some of the positions that exist out there today."
- "Global risk is way out of line right now." People hold positions they cannot exit when it turns, and "it always goes south at the absolute worst moment possible".
- On credit: "When you look at today's credit markets, that's exactly what they're predicated upon", meaning the Slower Fool Theory.
- He adds his own check: markets are always right, so bond prices tell him he is wrong, "and I respect that".
- Derivatives: "there's somebody out there sitting on a hell of a lot of risk. And it's socialized risk!" The repeal of Glass-Steagall left fewer and larger risk takers. There is not enough liquidity for a "low probability, high impact event".
- "One percent interest rates in the United States was such an anomaly, nobody really understands it."
- When the turn comes, "like in 1994, it's going to be uglier".

## Mental Models
- "The markets are like water. They will flow to the weakest point that they can push through."
- A fast move against your view tells you many others hold your position. Wait, then trade the other side at the extreme.
- Use a "stone cold" executor for stops. Thinking at the stop costs money.
- Speculators in most markets now fight each other. In currencies there are still central banks and corporates on the other side.
- Managing your own money teaches risk. He says bank-trained managers "manage positions, not risk".

## Anti-patterns (Harris's)
- **"Can you believe what the market did?"**: look at what you did.
- **Doubling down on a correct fundamental view.**
- **Muscling a trade when the market disagrees.**
- **The "last good trade" mind-set.**
- **Travelling for ideas**: anecdote can cost perspective. Flows matter more.
- **Rushing to be at the start of every move.** If something looks too good to be true, somebody knows more.

## Worked Example: real devaluation to soybeans (1999)
1. Event: Brazil devalues the real.
2. Link: soybeans are priced in dollars. Brazilian farmers' receipts in local currency jump.
3. Response: they sell all they can and plant "fence post to fence post".
4. Result: supply rises and soybean prices fall.
5. Trade: sell soybeans. Figure 10.1 plots both series for 1999.
The pattern: trace a currency shock to the producers it enriches, then to supply.

## His Regional Views in 2005 (dated)
- Europe: he doubts the union holds. France will not reform. Germany looks east.
- Eastern Europe: his first choice for equities and real estate over five years.
- United States: negative unless deficits fall. The largest debtor is also the reserve currency. "The only way out of deflation is through the printing press."
- China: "probably too good to be true".
- He expects more capital controls of the Malaysian kind.

## Key Takeaways
1. Follow where money is going before asking why.
2. A correct view with the wrong timing is a loss.
3. Count the holders who must exit together and the firms able to take the other side.
4. Keep the number of serious positions small.
5. His credit warning is a judgment with no numbers. He marks it as contradicted by prices at the time.

# Chapter 11: The Pioneer - Jim Rogers

Type: CASE. An interview about past trades and a set of dated 2005 calls. His rules of thumb are his own and are reported as his.
Interviewee: Jim Rogers. Cofounded the Quantum Fund with George Soros in 1969. Drobny reports a 4,200% return in a decade when the S&P 500 returned 47%. Retired about 1980. Launched the Rogers International Commodity Index in 1998.

## Core Idea
Rogers buys things that are cheap where "a fundamental change is taking place", and holds for years. He calls these secular trends. He says he is a poor trader and a poor timer. His central 2005 call is a commodity bull market lasting to between 2014 and 2022.

## Frameworks Introduced (all Rogers's)
- **Cheap plus change**: "Whatever success I have had has come from finding things that are cheap where a fundamental change is taking place and buying." He adds no formula and no portfolio technique.
- **Twenty-year reversal rule**: "Usually, when something has a 20-year move up or down, it is wrong and ready to reverse." When everybody is on one side, it "has nearly always proven to be wrong". Sell hysteria. Buy panic.
- **Commodity and stock cycles alternate**: long commodity bull markets go with stock bear markets, and the reverse. His stated lengths: commodity bull markets last "15 to 23 years". He started his index at the end of 1998 on the view that the 20-year bear market was ending. He says the bull market lasts until "sometime between 2014 and 2022". He adds: "This is not a prediction; history shows" it.
  - His mechanism, offered as a theory he does not vouch for: in a commodity bear market a company like Kellogg controls falling input costs and widens margins. In a commodity bull market costs rise and swing. Hedging departments raised in a bear market are "on the wrong foot". Margins are squeezed.
- **Industry bottom signal**: "when everybody in an industry is losing money and you have a few bankruptcies, the industry is usually making a bottom. Either it is going to disappear or it is going to turn around."
- **Front-page rule**: when something is "really pounded" or "skyrocketing" and is on the front page of the New York Times, reverse for a while, however much you agree long term. He stopped selling dollars when the dollar made the front page.
- **Retest pattern**: a new secular trend can retest its start years later. Treasuries bottomed at 15% yield in 1981 and were back at 14% in 1984. He says the bond bull market ran 1981 to 2003 and that the same retest is happening in reverse.
- **Bubble test by distance from the high**: he rejects "bubble" for commodities in 2005 because prices sit far below records. Sugar 80% below, cotton 60%, silver 80%, gold 60%, soybeans 60%, oil 50% below in real terms.
- **Bubble sameness**: bubbles "always look the same". People say a new technology will change the world and that doubters are old fogeys. "The smart money always loses money shorting them" because it cannot grasp how high prices go.
- **Staying power**: being right on the names is not enough. See worked example.
- **Institutional rules create opportunity**: a manager who "cannot buy stocks under $10" waited for Loral to rise fivefold and then bought.

## Key Concepts
- **Secular trend**: his 1970s term for what is now called macro. "Worldwide secular."
- **Reserve currency decline**: sterling fell 80% from top to bottom as it lost that role. The yen went from 500 to 100 per dollar after World War II.
- **Political currency**: his label for the euro.
- **Outlaw capitalism**: his label for Russia.
- **Marketing terms**: he calls "global macro" and "small cap" marketing gimmicks that limit an investor.
- **History through maps**: look at maps from 100 years back to see how fast the world changes.

## Mental Models
- "The most important thing in investing is to buy low and sell high."
- Markets are "almost always wrong" at extremes, yet "markets know more than you" on any given day. He holds both.
- Slack: he no longer runs fully leveraged. At Quantum, "the main thing that was wrong is that we always thought we were right".
- Know who was lucky. Those who knew it sold and left. Those who thought they were geniuses lost it.
- It is "always a mistake to bail people out". "You have to amputate the arm or the whole body dies."

## Anti-patterns (Rogers's)
- **Extrapolating five years of a bull market forever**: gold in the late 1970s, the NASDAQ in 1998 and 1999.
- **Shorting outright after options get expensive**: his 1970 wipeout.
- **Trading without homework**: short oil the Friday Iraq invaded Iran in 1980.
- **Buying by policy and habit**: he blames low Treasury yields on "the nuttiness and the inertia of Asian central banks".
- **Paying 2% to 3% and 30% to 50% fees**: his evidence that hedge funds are "overdone". He counts about 50,000 mutual funds and 8,000 hedge funds.

## Worked Example: right on the stocks, wiped out anyway (1970)
1. He put all his money in puts on the market. In May 1970 the market had its worst fall since 1937. He tripled his money.
2. He expected a rally, sold the puts and went flat. The rally came.
3. After the rally he wanted puts again. Premiums were high.
4. He went outright short with all his money instead.
5. The rally had a final run. "Two months later I was wiped out."
6. Most of the companies he had shorted later went bankrupt.
His lesson: "I didn't have the staying power." The view was right. The instrument and size left no room for timing error.

## His 2005 Calls (dated, with outcomes not in the book)
- The dollar falls another 40% to 50% over the next few years. The U.S. imposes exchange controls. Foreign debts rise by $1 trillion every 21 months.
- "Bonds are over."
- The euro does not survive 15 years.
- China has a hard landing. That is the next great buying point for commodities and for China.
- Oil reaches $150 a barrel within a decade. Cotton reaches $4.
- Gold rises, and sugar and cotton do better than gold over five years.
- Africa prospers on raw materials and suffers when the commodity bear market returns.
- Russia continues to "break up and disintegrate".
- He would not put "a nickel into India".
- An endowment should hold commodities, by index fund, and non-dollar assets.
- The label shakeout comes "within this decade".

## Key Takeaways
1. Check how far a price sits from its long-run high and how long the trend has run.
2. Look for an industry where everyone loses money and some firms have failed.
3. Choose instruments that survive being early.
4. Stand aside when your view is on the front page.
5. Treat his cycle lengths as his reading of history. The book gives no data table for them.

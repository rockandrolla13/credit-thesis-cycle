# Chapter 14: The Emerging Market Specialist - Marko Dimitrijević (Everest Capital)

Type: CASE. An interview about emerging market episodes from 1994 to 2005. His risk limits and rules are his own and are reported as his.
Interviewee: Marko Dimitrijević. First emerging market trade in 1978. Founded Everest Capital in 1990 with $8m. Over $1.5bn at the interview. He states 15% net a year for 15 years. His emerging markets fund lost over half its value in fall 1998 and later passed its high-water mark.

## Core Idea
A country in crisis behaves like one issuer. Every instrument tied to it falls together. So Dimitrijević caps exposure per country and treats the country "in aggregate like a company". His best trades came from contagion that hit countries whose economic position differed from the country in crisis.

## Frameworks Introduced (all Dimitrijević's)
- **Country as a company**: "when a country implodes, it acts like a company where management is discovered to have committed fraud." Owning Russia in 1998 was like owning Enron in 2001. Stock, bonds, receivables and pensions all suffered.
  - Before 1998 he concentrated at country level and diversified across instruments inside it. That gave no protection.
  - Limit after 1998: 10% of the macro fund and 15% of the emerging markets fund in any single emerging market country.
- **Contagion as opportunity**: after a shock in one country, similar-looking countries sell off "in sympathy". Check whether "the economic reality" differs. If it does, buy.
  - 1995: Venezuela, Argentina and Brazil sold off after Mexico.
  - 1998: Poland, the Czech Republic and Hungary did not default, sold off with Russia and "came back strong".
- **Why contagion has weakened**: markets are deeper and "much more domestically driven". Locals save, and invest at home first. Locals in Central Europe knew the Argentine default had no bearing on them: no trade, no bank exposure, no company links.
- **Stripping a Brady bond**: split it into (1) principal, backed by a U.S. Treasury zero, which is easy to price, and (2) the coupon stream, which is sovereign risk. Subtract the zero to see what the market pays for the sovereign part.
- **Capital-structure hedge inside a country**: when debt prices in the good outcome and equities do not, go long equities and short debt. If the country does well, debt tightens and equities do better. In a sell-off the short debt hedges. It fails where the bonds are already near investment grade and will not fall.
- **Position ladder**:
  1. Start with positions of low return and high probability.
  2. Add more aggressive positions, sized by how much each can lose.
  3. Look for a hedge, by options or by a correlated instrument.
  4. Judge each position alone. There is no portfolio return target.
- **Stop-loss rules**:
  - Use stops in continuous markets.
  - Do not use them in distressed debt or value positions where markets are not continuous. There they "can be dangerous".
  - Keep stops internal. "If you give stops to counterparties, they invariably get hit."
  - After an internal stop, wait one month before re-entering.
- **Clean sheet test**: forget where a price was. Ask "if we had a clean sheet of paper, would we or wouldn't we consider this investment at the current price?" If yes, hold it even after losing money in it.
- **Materiality screen for indirect exposure**: a company benefits from a theme only if the theme is a large enough share of its growth. Revenue share going from 0.5% to 1% is not a reason to invest.
- **Disaggregate the country**: "soft landing or hard landing" is the wrong question for China. Judge each sector and region.

## Key Concepts
- **Tequila effect**: fear of further devaluations after Mexico devalued at the end of 1994.
- **Tesobonos**: Mexican government securities denominated in dollars. With a crawling peg, the yield gap looked like free money.
- **Home country bias**: investors hold most assets at home (boxed text).
- **Lumpy returns**: he cites Buffett: "I'd rather have a lumpy 15 percent than a smooth 12 percent." He thinks the smooth alternative is now 6% to 8%.
- **Arbitrage crowding**: with 8,000 to 10,000 hedge funds there is not enough to arbitrage. Directional macro has no such limit if the trend is right.
- **Consumer credit take-off**: when rates fall in an emerging market, "pent-up demand for consumer loans explodes". Banks benefit.
- **Leapfrogging**: poorer countries skip wire lines for cellular.

## Mental Models
- "No matter how cheap you think something is, it can always get cheaper." The reverse holds for overvaluation.
- When people say "this has never traded above X" or "it's never moved more than three standard deviations", it often does just that.
- Low volatility "tends to be a temporary phenomenon". It was low from 1993 to 1995 and then shot up. He ties low volatility to the absence of an external shock.
- Markets underprice tail risk. His list of the unimaginable: 1987, Mexico 1994, Asia 1997, 11 September.
- "Actions speak much louder than words." He met Hugo Chavez and thought he would be good for Venezuela.
- Money can be made in long quiet spells as momentum carries prices from undervalued to overvalued.

## Anti-patterns (Dimitrijević's)
- **Concentration in one country across many instruments.**
- **Relying on the rule that a country devalues or defaults but never both**: Russia did both at once. He calls it unprecedented.
- **Going long high rates raised to defend a currency**: his Russian lesson kept him out of this in Argentina.
- **Stops left with counterparties.**
- **Meeting officials for information**: "pretty limited in value".
- **Selling smoothness**: when every investor wants 1% a month, it becomes half a percent.

## Worked Example: Brady bonds after the Mexican devaluation (1995 to 1997)
1. Before: money flowed into Mexico in 1994 because of the crawling peg. Many investors held Mexican and other Latin debt with leverage.
2. Shock: Mexico devalued at the end of 1994. It was "a complete surprise".
3. Contagion: Venezuelan, Argentine and Brazilian bonds sold off hard, though each country's position was different.
4. Analysis: strip out the Treasury zero that backs the principal. The remaining coupon stream priced sovereign risk "for next to nothing".
5. Result: the sovereign spread fell from over 1,900 bp in early 1995 to 400 bp by early 1997. He exited there.
The same steps appear in his reading of 1998: find the forced, leveraged holder, then find the issuers sold for resemblance and not for fundamentals.

## His 2005 Reading (dated view)
- Core theme: emerging market equities, which he calls "Emerging Markets Inc.". Supports: underinvestment by long-only funds, structural reform, demographics and cheap currencies.
- He would not expect emerging markets to rise with U.S. equities down 20%.
- A Treasury sell-off driven by growth with inflation under control would not end the theme.
- Two risks over three to five years: China taking Taiwan by force, and North Korea.
- Chinese autos: 17 manufacturers, heavy capacity, none making money. He expects a hard landing there. Tourism has "no landing". In some regions house prices rose 40% in seven years while incomes rose 70%.
- Long term he sees "a bipolar world between China and the United States".

## Key Takeaways
1. Add up all exposure to one sovereign, across equity, debt and currency.
2. After a crisis, sort the issuers that fell with it by their own trade, bank and company links.
3. Separate a secured instrument into its safe part and its risky part before judging the price.
4. Compare what debt and equity of the same issuer or country each imply.
5. Capacity buildout by many firms chasing market share with no profit is his marker for a sector hard landing.

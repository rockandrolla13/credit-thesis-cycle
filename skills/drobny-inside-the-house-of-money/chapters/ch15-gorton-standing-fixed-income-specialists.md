# Chapter 15: The Fixed Income Specialists - David Gorton and Rob Standing (London Diversified Fund Management)

Type: CASE for the trades. The portfolio construction method is their own stated process and is reported as theirs.
Interviewees: David Gorton and Rob Standing. Launched their fund inside JP Morgan in 1995. Drobny reports an average annual return over 15% with a standard deviation of 5.5% for the first 10 years. The 10-year U.S. Treasury Index returned just over 7% with 6.6%.

## Core Idea
They run fixed income relative value with a macro overlay. Traditionally two-thirds of risk is relative value and one-third macro. Their claim is that pure relative value is "very dangerous", because it is inherently short volatility and cannot be exited in a crisis. Liquid macro positions hedge it.

## Frameworks Introduced (attributed by speaker)
- **RV plus macro** (Gorton): "Getting out of RV trades during the LTCM unwind was near impossible, and the best hedges were classic macro trades like Eurodollar futures and out-of-the money options."
  - Their 1998: up 10% while RV funds failed. They were less leveraged in the RV trades that blew out. When things unravelled they bought as many short sterling and Eurodollar contracts as they were legally allowed. The Fed's 75 bp of cuts "bailed us out of our RV losses".
- **Definition of RV** (Gorton): "a trade that exploits an inefficiency, typically in the yield curve or the volatility surface". These are not pure arbitrages. So:
  1. Find the macro or structural reason the inefficiency exists.
  2. Then ask whether something simple, such as buying short sterling or selling Treasuries, exploits it better.
- **Definition of macro** (Gorton): finding imbalances within or between four asset classes: equities, commodities, fixed income, foreign exchange. Macro stands "at the top of the hill looking down, trying to find which of those four big chess pieces is about to move".
- **Portfolio build order** (Standing):
  1. Start with RV structures that are consistent and easy to put on.
  2. Form the macro view: the dollar, oil, U.S. rates, curve shape, the ECB.
  3. If the macro view is clear, allocate to micro strategies inside each theme so that being right gives excess return and being wrong gives "very small losses".
  4. If the macro view is not clear, build from relative value instead.
- **Volatility balance** (Standing): "a lot of RV trades are inherently short volatility". They track the net position and add long-volatility macro and uncorrelated RV trades. They are "very rarely net short volatility". They stress-test for a market accident and "generally we make money".
- **Risk limits**:
  - Diversified Fund: no more than 20% of capital to any one strategy. A strategy is one RV structure or one macro theme.
  - Stated loss limit to investors: never more than 5%. Worst drawdown to date: 3.5%.
  - Select Fund: fewer trades, more macro, up to half the risk in one trade.
  - Cost of the limits (Gorton): returns of over 15% would have been "upwards of 25 percent" with concentration.
- **Two-thirds, one-third expression** (Gorton): put two-thirds of a trade's risk in the best instrument and split the rest across two or more others.
- **Add to winners** (Gorton): "When you are up a percent or so on a good trade, that is when you really need to push." He calls early profit-taking "a limiting mentality".
- **VAR trap in low volatility** (Gorton): "as the volatility in markets declines, your risk declines. To keep VAR levels stable, you have to do more and more of the same trade so that, in essence, the position is actually getting more and more dangerous."
- **Disaster rule** (Gorton, from October 1987): "when a disaster is happening, just get out. Chop all ropes."
- **Risk transfer from banks to funds** (Standing): banks guarantee deposits at par and are "not well equipped to withstand shocks". Hedge funds and asset managers make no such guarantee and now hold more of the risk. He concludes: "That is why the system absorbs shocks quite effectively these days."
  - On credit: "Banks now have a lot less credit exposure on their balance sheet relative to their capital." Credit derivatives make this possible. He says it makes the system more resistant to shocks.
  - This is the opposite of the view Harris gives in Ch 10.

## Key Concepts
- **Conditional steepener**: buy a receiver swaption on the one-year rate one year forward and sell the same on the 10-year rate.
- **Agency convexity hedging**: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac hedging bid 10-year volatility "way too high".
- **Near arbitrage**: Gorton says they look for pure arbitrage every day and would love to find one.
- **First through the jungle**: the first trader hacks the path. Others follow easily. No-brainer trades go "from looking really good to looking extraordinarily good", so size matters.
- **Risk premium in bonds**: Gorton says for 20 years you were paid to own bonds. "Lend long, borrow short" worked. He wonders whether that premium is being priced out.
- **Hedge fund share of the market** (Standing): central bank assets grow faster than hedge fund assets, so hedge funds are a shrinking share of U.S. fixed income.

## Mental Models
- "You never know if the game is harder or if it has just changed."
- Every method has a macro component. RV models embed views on rates, volatility and curve shape.
- Stable core CPI means fewer opportunities. Core CPI stayed between 1% and 2% for eight years. In a year of 4.5% growth it moved from 1.3 to 1.6.
- "The really good trades last for a long, long time."
- A good trade can lose for months. Diversification is what lets them stay in.
- "Don't be a dick for a tick!" Pay to get the hedge done.

## Anti-patterns
- **Pure RV with leverage and no macro hedge** (Gorton).
- **Selling volatility or spread for 0.5% to 1% a month in a low-volatility market**: it "would probably work, but it has the danger of going horrifically wrong" (Gorton).
- **Allocating too fast into a cheapening market** without counting the positions built up in the prior rally (Standing, summer 2003).
- **Haggling over a hedge** (Gorton, Greece 1997).
- **Putting dealers in competition on every trade** (Gorton).
- **Taking profit as soon as a good trade works** (Gorton).

## Worked Example: TIPS as a mispriced real yield (1998 to 2003)
1. Observation: TIPS yielded well over 3%. Gorton: you were "paid what is perceived to be trend growth plus inflation while taking no risk".
2. Fair value view: about 1.5%, "in light of productivity growth".
3. Entry: 3.4% yield.
4. Adverse move: yields rose to 4.25%. They came close to the stop-loss and stayed in.
5. Learning period: they worked out hedges from the correlation of TIPS with nominal markets, with rate levels and with volatility.
6. Exit: July 2003 at the original 1.5% target.
The same pattern runs through the yen curve trade. The price was "completely wrong" at entry, the market took years to adjust, and carry plus diversification paid for the wait.

## Their 2005 Reading (dated view)
- No large clear themes. A "shortage of great ideas".
- Long rates are "extraordinarily stable" and volatility has fallen with them. A flatter curve lets volatility fall further.
- "We are at a point now where we should not be playing." Capital preservation first. They compare themselves to "a wily old leopard".
- They want to be short rates because rates are "clearly too low relative to nominal GDP". The trade has been losing, so they wait.
- They left long European bonds. Buying them at 3% requires a Japan-type view.
- Why ideas are short, in Gorton's order: better central banks, more market skill, and least of all the money in hedge funds.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask why a spread anomaly exists before trading it.
2. Net out a book's volatility exposure. Spread and carry trades usually sit on the short side.
3. Hold liquid macro hedges against illiquid spread positions.
4. Read a falling VAR in a calm market as a warning, per Gorton.
5. Note the disagreement inside the book: Standing says risk moved to funds makes the system safer. Harris, Porter and Bessent say the opposite.

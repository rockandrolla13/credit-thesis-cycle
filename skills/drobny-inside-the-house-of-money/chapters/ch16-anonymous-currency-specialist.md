# Chapter 16: The Currency Specialist - Anonymous

Type: Mixed. METHOD in the main (a stated framework for the price of money, the risk curve and liquidity), with CASE material on two trades.
Interviewee: Anonymous. Drobny says the manager is chief investment officer of a "several billion dollar" hedge fund inside a bank's asset management unit in London. The institution would not allow the name. Former money broker and Eurobond market maker. Mandate: currencies, interest rates and their derivatives, "with a strict focus on liquidity". No fund returns are given.

## Core Idea
"There is only one true macro trade and that's the price of money. Everything else is a function of the price of money." The manager makes a few directional calls on the Fed, holds them in liquid markets through owned options, and refuses every position with negative gamma. That includes relative value.

## Frameworks Introduced (all the anonymous manager's)
- **The price of money and the risk curve**:
  1. Central banks set the price of money with their policy rate.
  2. Monetary policy works "by encouraging people to move out along the risk curve".
  3. "The risk curve, in essence, is the credit curve."
  4. The order along it: the 18-month to two-year rate, then government bonds, corporate bonds, then equities. Foreign exchange "fans out" at the tail end.
  5. The effective price of money is not the overnight rate. It is "the interest rate that corporations use to evaluate investment opportunities", which he puts at 18 months to two years.
- **One central bank**: G10 monetary policy is highly correlated, Japan excepted. "There is really only one central bank and that's the U.S. Federal Reserve."
  - Japan is the exception because its monetary and fiscal policy are "maxed out", so the exchange rate is the one tool left.
  - The Fed created "the global business cycle", which raised cross-asset correlation and made its own job "like driving an oil tanker".
- **FX as beta on beta**: currencies are "beta on interest rate and credit sentiment". If the market decides Brazil is a great credit, buyers must buy the real. FX has "no real anchor". So a true macro fund should make more in fixed income than in FX.
- **Three or four trades**: at most three or four macro trades exist at once. Besides the cost of capital, they are what bonds do, what equities do and what the dollar does. The dollar trades against three blocs: Europe, Asia and Latin America.
- **75/15 rule of price movement**: "75 percent of price movement happens only 10 to 15 percent of the time. The rest is nothing but noise within a range." Data releases drive the moves.
- **Long gamma, long vega structure**:
  - 70% of the time he opens a position through options.
  - Leverage then follows the analysis: right calls lever up by themselves and wrong calls delever.
  - He is always in the market. "Being flat is a very, very difficult position to run."
  - A cheap or dead option goes "in the back of a drawer" until expiry.
  - He says CTAs work for the same reason. They add as a move goes their way, "giving them a payoff profile that is very similar to an option".
- **No relative value, two reasons**: (1) "Negative gamma is implicit in relative value trading because it works on regression to the mean." (2) It is labor intensive.
  - Spread trading expects "somebody to value a spread a bit tighter than you did. That works until someone decides to sell."
  - "All RV trades have directionality in them." In his 1998 trade the direction was that "the liquidity going in was a lot deeper than the liquidity getting out".
  - Winner's curse: once you hold the RV position, you know you made the wrong price, or the trade would not have happened.
- **Liquidity rule**: "Liquidity, liquidity, liquidity is key." "The hedge funds that blow up are always the ones who misestimate how much liquidity is in the marketplace." "It is liquidity where most traders misprice assets relative to the returns they expect."
- **Frozen ice theory**: see worked example.
- **Gestalt shift**: the large trades come when the market reprices a view or moves to "a whole different concept". The signal: it "usually comes after volatility gets pumped up because everyone is out there buying insurance". Buying options to protect positions shows nervous holders.
- **Timing of option purchases**: buy "when everyone is telling me that something is inevitable and the market is discounted to one extreme". He is less likely to enter if he shares the consensus view.
- **Basic laws**: "when you're bullish you're long or flat, and when you're bearish you're short or flat."
- **Five-month test**: he judges a view over five months. If the macro drivers have not shown by then, he is analyzing the market wrong and gets out.
- **Hold through a drawdown**: if the facts are unchanged and no stop is hit, keep the trade and the leverage. He cites a trader's line that a good trader recovers "with the same trade". His firm has a 5% monthly loss rule.
- **Curve trades**: he avoids them. For a bearish flattening he trades the end that moves most, the two-year note.

## Key Concepts
- **Global macro (his definition)**: asset classes driven top-down, where nobody can control an instrument "for more than a two- to three-hour period".
- **Owning the market**: "When you own 25 percent of a stock, you cease to be a macro trader and become an owner."
- **Wholesale view**: from a dealing room you see who buys and how much. From a screen the price is one-dimensional.
- **Cyclicality**: macro "has the least amount of cyclicality". Credit trading is "clearly linked to the underlying economy".
- **Forced seller**: "I don't ever want to be a forced seller." Long gamma lets him buy from forced sellers at low prices.
- **Late allocators**: institutions invest in a fund after its run, at the peak, and take the drawdown without the earlier gains.

## Mental Models
- "Price action never lies." The group's knowledge exceeds any expert's.
- Information changes slowly. Most messages are noise. Policy trends do not reverse over three meetings, because a first move is "the first step in a long journey".
- "All the crap rallies at the end of a bull market, which is how you know it's near the end."
- "The weaker credits always have the least liquidity and the so-called highest returns."
- Look back at why you put a trade on. If not, you do what economists do: raise the target after it is hit.
- In a bad spot, play defense. From deep rough at 250 yards, take the 9-iron.

## Anti-patterns (the manager's)
- **Any short option position.** His example: a salesman who financed Bund calls by selling puts. The position grew as the market fell.
- **Smooth 1% a month from RV**: it lasts "for years, until all of a sudden there's a massive drawdown".
- **Taking profit early, then buying a proxy.**
- **Deferring to the other side's confidence**: a dealer refused to buy at 86 what he had just priced at 96 to 97, because he assumed the seller knew something.
- **Trading on talk of an "inevitable" event**: his example is China revaluing.
- **Choosing a fund because it runs a few billion dollars.**

## Worked Example: the frozen ice theory (dollar/yen and Asia, 1995 to 1997)
A lake freezes thicker at the sides than in the middle.
1. 1995: dollar/yen is at 80. Everyone agrees it is the wrong price. Traders buy dollars against yen. This is the thick ice: a sound, liquid trade.
2. The rate rises. Traders take profit in the 90s. It goes on through 100 toward 120.
3. Traders find it hard to buy back above where they sold. They look for a proxy.
4. They buy the dollar against the baht, then the ringgit, the won, the Philippine peso, and on down.
5. Dollar/yen reaches 135. Nobody is long it. Everyone is long "dollar/Asia" and has forgotten why.
6. They have moved out along the spectrum of "liquidity and credit" to the thin ice in the middle.
7. The liquid leg reverses. Everyone asks for prices in size at once, and there are none.
His general claim: "Everybody runs a vicarious relative value book in how they trade."

## His 2005 Reading (dated view)
- "The globe is actually okay." Geopolitics is stable for now.
- His darker private view: what the world is built on is "clearly not sustainable". He gives no mechanism.
- The opportunity set is neither larger nor smaller than before.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start from the two-year rate and walk outward: governments, corporates, equities, currencies.
2. Read wide credit participation as policy working as designed. Read the last, weakest credits rallying as the late stage.
3. Ask how the liquidity on the way out compares with the liquidity on the way in.
4. Ask whether the crowd still holds the original liquid trade or only its proxies.
5. Own the option when the timing is unknown.

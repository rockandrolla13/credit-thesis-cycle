# Chapter 9: The Dot-Commer - Peter Thiel (Clarium Capital)

Type: CASE. An interview about one manager's post-bubble thesis and trades. His risk rules are his own and are reported as his.
Interviewee: Peter Thiel. Derivatives at Credit Suisse Financial Products. Cofounder and CEO of PayPal (founded 1998, listed February 2002 at $1bn, sold to eBay for $1.5bn). Launched Clarium in October 2002. Drobny reports net returns over 50% in 2003 and in 2005, and assets over $1bn.

## Core Idea
Thiel runs a few concentrated directional themes built on one thesis: the equity bubble and the policy response to it left "huge, long-lasting distortions" that must unwind through deleveraging and deflation. He argues that tight cross-market correlation makes broad diversification neither practical nor desirable.

## Frameworks Introduced (all Thiel's)
- **Two worlds test**: "Either we are living in a normal world or else something extraordinary is happening." In a normal world macro has little to do. In a world distorted by the largest bubble ever, "there are things that are likely to break in very asymmetric ways".
- **The post-bubble chain** (his causal story, 2004 to 2005):
  1. The equity bubble burst.
  2. Governments and central banks "pumped enormous amounts of extra liquidity into the system".
  3. That created "a consumer spending bubble and a housing bubble" and "an enormous overhang of capital".
  4. Leverage runs "from housing to venture capital".
  5. The financial system must delever. Baby boomers start saving and shift to fixed income.
  6. Real rates fall a lot. Nominal rates should fall too.
  - His claim on the exit: "Hyperinflation is not a choice." No country with a financial economy as large as the U.S. has inflated out of a debt problem. He says the 1970s do not count, because the economy was far less financialized.
  - His Japan comparison: Japan cut less and got mild deflation with "no consumer credit bubbles". If U.S. consumer credit unwinds, deflation is "likely to be much stronger".
- **Correlation argument for concentration**: "emerging market spreads are linked to junk bond and credit spreads, which are in turn correlated with the USD and high tech equities". The tightness is "historically high". So themes are few and scalable.
- **Relative value as a source of systemic risk** (Clarium philosophy box): market-neutral styles use "increasing amounts of leverage and risk to extract declining returns". They "further correlate markets and inject systemic risk". Clarium takes directional positions with moderate leverage instead.
- **Risk management**:
  1. Stress test all positions, drawing on LTCM.
  2. Assume all positions "correlate perfectly".
  3. Size the portfolio so the disaster loss is about 15% to 20%.
  4. Put a stop-loss on each trade, set from entry. It is not a trailing stop. A trade in profit is allowed to move more against him.
  5. After a stop, decide whether the analysis needs refining or was misconceived.
  - He calls strict stops "the toughest discipline" in fundamental macro, because an adverse move makes the trade "look better and better".
- **Nonprice indicators**: for each trade, name indicators that should move a certain way as it progresses. For equity shorts he tracks volatility and sentiment. "When price is used as the only indicator, things devolve into an ineffective charting exercise."
- **Options as information, not positions**: he reads derivative, swap and swaption markets for positioning. He calls trading options directly "usually an inefficient or lazy way to express a theme".
- **Timing rule**: a distortion "can get bigger before reversing course". Without a timing indicator, do not trade it. "Sometimes good trades are the ones you don't put on."
- **TIPS as a valuation cross-check**: the 30-year TIPS yield reached 1.6%, its lowest. He reads that as the 30-year risk-free real return. He argues equity valuations assume productivity growth "not compatible with 1.6 percent real yields".
- **Fee structure as an agency fix**: no management fee, 25% of profits, and almost all his liquid net worth in the fund.

## Key Concepts
- **Macro view in an operating company**: PayPal raised $100m, more than it seemed to need, and closed on 31 March 2000. He expected the window to shut.
- **Absent pushback**: by the late 1990s the big macro funds had closed after betting against the bubble. Index flows peaked in March 2000. "There wasn't a lot of money pushing back."
- **Silicon Valley as a sentiment indicator**: euphoria there means overbought markets. Pessimism there is often a time to buy.
- **MBA indicator**: the most popular destination for MBA graduates is "the sector that is peaking". In 2005 that was hedge funds.
- **Synthetic call**: a high-cost producer whose cash flow surges above a price point.

## Bubble Tells He Lists (2000)
- "Eye-popping valuations."
- Investors trying to invest "without knowing anything about the business".
- An investor in South Korea who "wired money with no signed documents".
- The same behavior "on a massive scale" nearby.
- In 2005: "the ratio of money to good ideas is high". He puts venture capital near 1999 to 2000 levels, and China and some emerging markets beyond them.

## Anti-patterns (Thiel's)
- **Working on your own tree and ignoring the forest.**
- **Passive indexing with no view on index valuation.**
- **Trading a bubble with no timing indicator**: he thought U.S. housing was a bubble two years earlier. Home-builder stocks then rose by a factor of four.
- **Trailing stops on long-horizon themes.**
- **A fixed management fee with low returns.**
- **Qualified answers**: MBAs who say "X is a little bit better than Y" and hold no strong view.

## Trades He Describes (CASE)
| Trade | Detail as stated |
|---|---|
| Long long-dated oil, presented April 2004 | See worked example. |
| Short Japanese government bonds, June 2003 | 10-year yield 45 bp. Started at 60 bp and added at 45. Most of the position went on within 1 bp of the all-time low. Worst case was "a little carry". He liked the trade from 1.5% down. |
| Short U.S. Treasuries, February 2004 (his worst) | Research on Fannie Mae's fixed income portfolio suggested a sharp sell-off. A weak employment report produced a rally. He was stopped out. Bonds then sold off. A month later he went long again on his core thesis. |
| Clarium themes, Q1 2004 | Long 10-year Treasuries. Long a basket of yen, real, New Zealand and Australian dollars against the dollar. Long oil equities. Short S&P 500 and DAX as financial-heavy indexes. |

Drobny notes the short equity view was the one theme that had not paid off by the interview.

## Worked Example: long-dated oil through oil sands equities (April 2004)
1. Demand: growth depends more on Asia, which uses oil inefficiently. No cheap substitute exists. "The best alternative product to cheap oil is more expensive oil."
2. Supply: a production peak is near. OPEC quotas are set as a share of estimated reserves, which gives an incentive to overstate them.
3. Market mind-set: after 15 years of low real prices, the market expects $20 to $30 a barrel with spikes.
4. Anomaly: the curve is inverted. The mispricing sits in the back months.
5. Obstacle: that part of the curve is thin.
6. Expression: Canadian oil sands companies. They are high marginal cost producers. They are worth little at current prices and their cash flow "will suddenly surge" at a price point. That makes "a synthetic call on oil prices".
The method inside the case: find where the curve embeds a stale belief, then find a liquid proxy with option-like payoff.

## Key Takeaways
1. State the thesis as a chain. Each link should have an observable.
2. Size for all positions moving together.
3. Check the theme against prices in rates, such as long real yields.
4. Wait for a timing indicator before trading a bubble.
5. His deflation and deleveraging call is a dated 2005 view. The book does not report its outcome.

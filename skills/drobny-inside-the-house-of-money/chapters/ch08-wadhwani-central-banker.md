# Chapter 8: The Central Banker - Dr. Sushil Wadhwani (Wadhwani Asset Management, former Bank of England MPC member)

Type: Mixed. METHOD on how a central bank reads data and on asset prices in policy. CASE on his two trades. The views are Wadhwani's own.
Interviewee: Dr. Sushil Wadhwani. LSE academic. Director of equity strategy at Goldman Sachs in London. Proprietary trader at Tudor. One of four outside members of the nine-member Monetary Policy Committee from 1999. Then his own fund.

## Core Idea
Independent central banks removed the political business cycle and with it many long macro trends. But a focus on consumer prices and not asset prices let "huge asset market distortions" build. Wadhwani expects those to be the new source of macro opportunity. This is the book's only insider account of how a policy committee uses data and market pricing.

## Frameworks Introduced (all Wadhwani's)
- **Political business cycle, before and after independence**:
  - Before: governments could not raise rates at some points in the electoral cycle. Policy was "reactive, not sufficiently preemptive". Booms and busts followed, with volatile output, inflation and rates, and "nice trending moves".
  - After: policy is more preemptive, so rates "need to move up and down less than they used to". There are "fewer of these long-lived trends".
  - Where it applies: UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and via EMU Spain and Italy. Less so the U.S., Germany and Switzerland, which already set rates independently.
- **Paradox of perfection** (term coined by Drobny and a Stockholm manager, endorsed by Wadhwani): low inflation, low output volatility and stable employment encourage excess risk taking. "If you take your eyes off the ball vis-à-vis asset price misalignments, you are then storing up trouble."
- **Asset prices in policy (his MPC position)**:
  1. Do not target asset prices. Do not give them too much weight in the inflation numbers.
  2. Do take asset price imbalances into account "more concretely".
  3. In a housing bubble, tell the public that rates will be set on a longer view than the two-year inflation forecast.
  4. Hold rates higher than the two-year forecast needs, and accept an undershoot. Defend it as stability over time.
  5. The aim is expectations. "As in any bubble, shaping expectations formation is critical."
  - Against Greenspan: bubbles can be seen as they emerge. Fed transcripts show concern "even in late 1995". The Fed "spotted it and just deliberately chose not to take action".
- **How a central bank reads data** (from inside):
  - "The effect of individual pieces of information is often much smaller than the markets think", above all for a big outlier.
  - The bank smooths. "What is very weak one month is often very strong the next month." It looks at the month's information as a whole.
  - In his time the MPC set policy for the economy and not for what was discounted. Proposals to wait a month and signal first usually lost. "If they get surprised, fine."
  - Since he left he sees more market guidance in the UK. Guidance matters more when deflation is a risk.
- **Inflation anchoring test**: before independence an oil price rise risked a need to tighten. Since independence the UK had "two significant oil shocks" and did not tighten, because expectations held wages and prices.
- **Two-part portfolio**:
  - *Tactical*: 20 to 30 smallish, distinct bets "to make some pennies along the way".
  - *Core*: long-term positions, often held in options.
  - When something big happens he overlays "a big bet" on the same trades.
  - About 30% of the fund is run purely by models. The rest is discretionary, with more confidence when models agree.
- **Big-bet trigger**: (1) "a big valuation misalignment", then (2) a catalyst that confirms it may correct. The catalyst can be technical, news or flows. He concentrates on the one instrument with the best reward/risk.
- **Worst-case sum**: if every position hit its stop and every option expired worthless tomorrow, how much is lost? If he is not comfortable with the number, he recuts the risk. He rates such simple checks above "the most sophisticated analytics".
- **Stop rules**:
  - A breached stop is taken, with no questions.
  - Re-entry needs a 24-hour break with no position, then a fresh case as a new trade.
  - He splits a trade into spot with stops and options he keeps. Dead options can revive if he is right in the medium term.
- **Tudor Jones lessons**: "good defense is paramount". Be willing to be wrong. Be able to take a position off even if the medium-term view may still be right. Take a profitable trade off when it will no longer reach target, instead of letting it fall back to the stop.

## Key Concepts
- **Beauty contest (Keynes)**: picking what average opinion expects average opinion to be (boxed text).
- **Narrow end of global macro**: directional bets in equity indexes, rates and exchange rates. He started there and intends to go multistrategy.
- **Should versus will**: his academic habit was to think what should happen. Positioning and sentiment decide what will happen first.
- **Short-termism**: few investors can hold to maturity. That is why anomalies persist and new ones appear. "Sometimes new $100 bills appear."
- **Zero-sum**: macro markets are close to it. Equities create wealth.
- **Reflation/deflation swings**: near zero inflation, swings between the two poles can be wide.

## Mental Models
- "When something's going up or down in a straight line and you start getting political resistance, you'd better pay attention."
- Read Street research to know the consensus. Decide from your own work.
- Use equity long/short people as a lead indicator. At Tudor they saw trends in economies before the macro data.
- Use fixed income relative value people to find the best point on the curve for a macro rate view.
- Technical levels matter because others trade them. He admits he was "dead wrong" to dismiss technical traders.
- "Yesterday's P&L is yesterday's P&L."

## Anti-patterns (Wadhwani's)
- **Junking valuation models for a story**: his 1998 dollar/yen.
- **Ignoring political rhetoric on a one-way move.**
- **Re-entering to win back a loss**: "a bad argument".
- **Anchoring on a view**: the academic's habit.
- **Complacency when a trade goes well.**

| Trade | Detail as stated |
|---|---|
| UK dividend tax change | He expected equities to fall. On the day the market rose and he lost. Prices fell three months later. |
| Short December short sterling, November 1997 | See worked example. |
| Long dollar/yen, June 1998 | Street stories pointed to 180. China complained about its unmoved peg. The Fed and Bank of Japan intervened in mid June. Dollar/yen closed eight big figures lower. Other longs unwound and unrelated positions fell together. He exited at the first chance after the stop was breached. |

## Worked Example: the best reward/risk he saw (UK, November 1997)
1. Setup: 33 of 33 economists expected no change. The Bank had said in September it would pause.
2. Pricing: the short sterling strip was "flat as a pancake".
3. Evidence: "virtually every economic number after that was strong". The strip did not move.
4. Trade, two days before the meeting: sell the front (December) contract.
5. Payoff: lose 1.5 ticks if nothing happens. Make 23.5 ticks on a hike.
6. Outcome: a 25 bp hike. He made about 5% that day.
He calls it luck and says the ratio "never happened to me again". The structure is stated guidance holding the price while the data moved.

## His 2005 Reading (dated view)
- Imbalances "in spades": the U.S. current account deficit and an overvalued dollar, and housing in Australia, New Zealand and the UK.
- "It is always difficult to know when a bubble is bursting."
- Deflation: not out of the woods. "We could only be one big shock away from deflation risk again."
- Three questions for a macro manager: the U.S. imbalances, the world's adjustment to China and India, and Japan. He thinks Japan may need "a lot of inflation".
- He admits he may be "too alarmist" on asset misalignments.

## Key Takeaways
1. A credible independent central bank lowers rate volatility and shifts risk into asset prices.
2. Discount single data outliers when judging the next policy move.
3. Trade a misalignment only after a catalyst.
4. Watch for guidance that pins the front end while data moves the other way.
5. Keep options as the part of a trade that survives the stop.

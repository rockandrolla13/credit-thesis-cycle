## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. How the Fed decides: constrained judgement
- The Fed is **"independent within the government"**. It picks policy from options the country will tolerate. That tolerance is a covert judgement in each policymaker's gut. (ch01)
- A regime change needs the **stars in alignment** (his image): the public sees the cost of the status quo, the gains of tightening reach voters, and no regulated sector breaks at once. (ch01, ch03, ch05)
- Policymakers are conservative and gradual. Staff forecasts sit near outside consensus. Together they **narrow the channel** of policy thinking. (ch02)
- Rules (Friedman, Taylor) are **"background music"**. The neutral rate is "something of a guess". Judgement on incoming data decides. (ch02, ch09)

### 2. The operating-target duality
- **"You can control an interest rate... or you can instead attempt to control the supply of money"**, not both. (ch02, ch05)
- Under a rate target, money is demand-determined, and an inflationary demand for money is supplied "willy-nilly" until the rate is changed.
- Under a quantity target, the rate takes "the full brunt" of every shock, with no vote needed.
- Use this first when reading any central bank. Then ask how wide a rate band it tolerates.

### 3. Regime by chairman
| Chairman | Operating target | What drove tightening or easing | Author's judgement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Martin, 1951-1970 | Free reserves and "money-market conditions" | Intuition from market contacts. Numeric forecasts from the mid-1960s | Built the modern process. Too cautious against late-1960s inflation |
| Burns, 1970-1978 | Funds rate, plus money ranges and a proviso clause | Fear of breaking thrifts and banks, no public support | "Containing rather than suppressing". Targets were a pretence |
| Miller, 1978-1979 | Unchanged | Dollar defence by intervention | Leadership vacuum |
| Volcker, 1979-1987 | Nonborrowed reserves (late 1979 to late 1982), then borrowed reserves | Money path. Stopped when inflation broke and recession deepened | Paradigm shift owed to the man |
| Greenspan, 1987-2006 | Announced funds rate | Growth against potential. Risk management against deflation | Good, then too easy and too predictable |
| Bernanke, 2006- | Funds rate, then zero rate and balance sheet | Crisis containment | Late to see it, then effective |

### 4. Tests of the policy stance
- **Real funds rate**: nominal funds rate minus concurrent CPI inflation. Negative in 1974-1977 and "somewhat negative" in 2002-2005. The author treats both as policy errors. (ch03, ch06, ch10)
- **Base drift**: a target rebased on the actual outcome has "no practical significance". (ch03)
- **Missing money**: when an aggregate undershoots its demand equation into a second year, adjust it and cross-check a broader one. In 1975-1976, M1 growth of about 5½ percent behaved like 8 to 10 percent. (ch03)
- A target met for the wrong reason hides the error.

### 5. Practical monetarism (the 1979-1982 method)
- The FOMC set a money path. Staff turned it into a nonborrowed-reserves path. The funds rate floated in a band of four to six points. (ch05)
- The author's reading: money was the device that got rates "as high as needed" fast. The committee was "not psychologically capable" of voting the funds rate to 15 to 20 percent.
- Identity behind it: N = R + F. Fix free reserves F and rates ignore money. Fix nonborrowed reserves N and rates respond to it. (ch10)
- Credibility came from "stick to it", aimed at business and labour. Never call a regime an "experiment".
- The procedure hid judgemental path adjustments cleared with the chairman.

### 6. The chairman and the committee
- Structural change needs the chairman. Routine votes give him only a small, earned benefit of the doubt. (ch09)
- Overreach loses power. Consensus-seeking makes the committee "flounder". A weak chairman cannot be replaced by staff. (ch02, ch03, ch04)
- **Artist framework**: a chairman succeeds when the stage fits his talent. Burns was a private-stage performer in a time that needed a public one. Volcker was the reverse, and lost the February 1986 Board vote four to three. (ch01, ch05)
- Staff present objective alternatives and do not recommend the stance. (ch03, ch04)
- Politics arrives as pressure (Johnson's jeep ride, Ehrlichman's "get the money supply up", the Plaza draft) and through shared forecasts. (ch02, ch03, ch05)

### 7. Communication and moral hazard
- Announce the current target and the forecast. Do not promise the future path. (ch06, ch08)
- A promised path ("considerable period", "measured pace") removes refinancing risk and breeds leverage. "They made it too easy for us." (ch06)
- The **Greenspan put** formed from a warning without action, easing in stress, and a visible rescue. (ch06)
- "Performance always and obviously trumps communication." (ch08)

### 8. Crises and rescues
- The central bank is the **deus ex machina** outside the system. It becomes part of the system when it waits too long. (ch02, ch07)
- A rescue protects creditors, not managers or shareholders. The larger moral hazard is weaker creditor diligence. (ch07)
- Test for a rescue: threat to payments, to holders of liquid savings, or to the stability of the system. Size alone is not the test. (ch06, ch08)
- Bagehot with two caveats: those responsible must lose heavily, and rescues must be rare. (ch08)
- Letting Lehman fail helped turn "a very bad crisis into a truly great one". (ch07, ch08)
- After a crisis, the horse-to-water limit is real. Fiscal policy is "the more effective expansionary instrument". (ch07)
- Two credibilities: policy credibility and **institutional credibility**. The second was "badly frayed" by 2010. (ch08)

### 9. The author's forward agenda
- Lean "to an extent" against capital-asset inflation. Waiting is the "worse alternative". (ch09)
- Use prudential tools early, as signals. (ch09)
- Give the funds target "a little give". Take more account of the rest of the world. (ch09)

# Appendices A and B: Chronology and Indicators (with selected endnotes)

## Core Idea
Appendix A dates each chairman against the presidents he served under. Appendix B gives two annual charts for 1960 to 2009: the Fed's two objectives (inflation and unemployment) and three policy indicators (funds rate, real funds rate, M1 growth). Use this file to place any episode from Chapters 2 to 7 in time and to check the author's real-rate arguments. This file is reference data, not a framework chapter.

## How the charts support the text
- **Burns era**: the real funds rate is below zero in the mid-1970s while inflation is high. This is the basis of the Chapter 3 judgement of "no real restraint".
- **Volcker era**: the real funds rate jumps from about zero to about 6 percent in 1981 and stays there for about four years, while inflation falls from its 1980 peak.
- **Late Greenspan era**: the real funds rate is below zero again in the early 2000s. The author's own figure is "somewhat negative on average" for 2002 to 2005 (Ch 6).
- **Money**: M1 growth swings from about 17 percent to about minus 4 percent between 1986 and the mid-1990s with low inflation throughout. This fits the author's view that money lost its predictable relation to prices.
- **Crisis**: M1 growth spikes in 2008 while inflation turns negative in 2009 and unemployment rises to about 9 percent.

## Selected Endnotes (substantive ones only)

- **The reserve identity behind the 1979 procedure** (Ch 5, note 4). Total reserves T equal required reserves R plus excess reserves E. Subtract borrowed reserves B from both sides:

```
T = R + E
T - B = R + (E - B)
N = R + F
```

  N is nonborrowed reserves, supplied at the Fed's initiative. F is free reserves. Before October 1979 the Fed in effect fixed F, so money-market conditions did not respond to money growth, and N adjusted to whatever R the banks needed. After October 1979 the Fed fixed N, so F and money-market rates moved with the banks' demand for required reserves. The author says this leaves out lagged reserve requirements, shifts in banks' demand for free reserves, clearing balances, and changes in the deposit mix and in currency demand.

- **How the 15 percent funds-rate guess was built** (Ch 5, note 3). Current inflation of about 12 percent plus three points of restraint. The author says he should have added more, because a 3 percent real rate was "not much, if any, more than the potential real return on capital".

- **Controllability verdict** (Ch 5, note 5). After all sides did their econometrics, money growth over the intermediate term was shown to be "at least no less controllable" with the Fed's reserve technique than with the alternatives.

- **Discount rate under a reserves target** (Ch 5, note 6). With nonborrowed reserves limited, a discount-rate rise raised short rates automatically, with no FOMC action. Under a rate target it does not.

- **Fed holdings as retired debt** (Ch 2, note 1). The Fed returns nearly all its interest to the Treasury, so its holdings are "for all practical purposes retired debt".

- **Deposit ceilings moved at the pace of the slowest boat** (Ch 3, note 1). Ceilings could rise only as fast as thrifts could bear. The Monetary Control Act of 1980 phased ceilings out over six years.

- **Burns and the Committee on Interest and Dividends** (Ch 3, note 2). Burns chaired this part of the Nixon controls, a potential conflict of interest. The author saw no effect on his monetary policy views.

- **Earlier proviso clause** (Ch 3, note 4). The directive carried a similar proviso in the last four years of the 1960s, tied to a "bank credit proxy". It had very little effect.

- **Steps to announcing the funds rate** (Ch 6, note 3). A qualitative statement when policy changed (1994), the specific rate when policy shifted (from the start of 1996), then the rate after every meeting (spring 1999).

- **Discount-rate spread to the funds target** (Ch 6, note 4). One point above from January 2003. One half point from August 2007. One quarter point from March 16, 2008. Back to one half point in mid-February 2010. The primary credit rate is close to a ceiling on the overnight funds rate. Secondary credit costs a further half point.

- **Which price index** (Ch 6, note 7). The author measures the real funds rate with total CPI. He thinks the trend of total CPI or PCE shows cost pressure on consumers, and so wage demands, better than core measures.

- **Measured-pace dates** (Ch 6, note 8). "Considerable period" appeared in the October 28, 2003 release, with the funds rate at 1 percent. "Measured" first appeared on June 30, 2004, when the rate rose to 1¼ percent. Long rates did not rise on balance and tended to fall as short rates rose.

- **Term Securities Lending Facility** (Ch 7, note 1). Up to $200 billion of government securities lent for twenty-eight days against collateral that included highly rated private-label mortgage securities. It was meant to help firms meet margin calls. Days later the Fed had to lend directly for the Bear Stearns sale.

- **Chairman and Treasury contact** (Ch 1, note 3). Weekly Monday breakfasts between the chairman and the Treasury Secretary, and Wednesday lunches with Treasury officials. At the lunches the author attended for two decades there were no discussions of monetary policy.

## Key Takeaways
1. Check any claim about the stance of policy against the real funds rate series first.
2. Date episodes by chairman and by president together. The political pressures in Chapters 2 to 5 follow the presidential column.
3. Use the reserve identity to tell a free-reserves regime from a nonborrowed-reserves regime.

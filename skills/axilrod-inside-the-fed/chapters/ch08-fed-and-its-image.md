# Chapter 8: The Fed and Its Image

## Core Idea
The Fed needs two kinds of credibility. Monetary policy credibility rises and falls with results. Institutional credibility is "the bedrock of public confidence" that protects the Fed's independence when policy is unpopular. The author judges that institutional credibility "became badly frayed" in the great credit crisis, and that Dodd-Frank moved the elected government into the front line of future rescues. This chapter is mostly general method. Its episodes are illustrations.

## Frameworks Introduced (general method)

- **Two-layer credibility model**:
  - Monetary policy credibility: does the public believe policy is run well and objectively? It is lost when the Fed misjudges the economy or when the right policy is resented.
  - Institutional credibility: the Fed's reputation for "honest, and highly expert administration" of itself as a whole, including supervision, consumer protection, community development and payments.
  - Function: the second buffers the first. It also protects the Fed's budget from congressional control and keeps Government Accountability Office audits away from monetary policy.
  - How to read erosion: look for (1) audit powers creeping toward policy, (2) governance changes that limit bankers' influence, (3) limits on delegation to reserve banks, (4) debate about removing regulatory powers. All four appeared after 2008.
  - Standard required: independence can be defended only when the institution is seen "as pure as Caesar's wife".

- **Why the central bank must stay in regulation**: regulatory issues "impinge in a serious way rather infrequently", but the Fed needs continuing knowledge that banks are soundly run, and "the ability and will to act constructively on a timely basis" when threats surface. The author calls 2007-2009 "an opportunity rather obviously missed".
  - Macro-prudential extension: countercyclical capital requirements could ease the path of monetary policy. Practical problems: timing, market misreading, bank planning, and coordination among regulators at home and abroad.

- **Rescue doctrine (Bagehot, with two caveats)**:
  1. When the financial fabric is threatened, lend as last resort, presumably at a penalty rate.
  2. Caveat one: those responsible "should suffer personally, by considerably more than they could have expected".
  3. Caveat two: rescues must be rare. A central bank balance sheet loaded with bad loans makes the public doubt the whole system, including the central bank.
  - Purpose of a rescue: not to save an insolvent firm, but to supply liquidity that protects depositors and creditors while a buyer is found.
  - Size is not the test: "the size of the institution, per se, is not at issue". The test is the effect on the stability of the system the firm belongs to. A small bank in an isolated region can qualify.
  - Why speed matters: "Liquidity crises have a way of turning into solvency crises."
  - The judgement is hard. No Fed money for LTCM. Fed credit for Bear Stearns and AIG. Lehman allowed to fail, which helped turn "a very bad crisis into a truly great one".

- **Dodd-Frank's shift of responsibility** (the author's reading):
  - Emergency lending now applies to a "program or facility with broad-based eligibility", not to one firm.
  - No loans to insolvent firms, and no programme to help one company avoid bankruptcy.
  - Collateral must protect taxpayers.
  - The Treasury Secretary must approve each programme in advance.
  - Orderly liquidation authority lets the Treasury Secretary name the FDIC as receiver.
  - Effect: the government in power is "more clearly in the front line, as politically and socially it should be". How it works in a real emergency is unknown.

- **Communication rule**: "performance always and obviously trumps communication".
  - Do: state the policy target for the period between meetings. Publish the economic projections that frame the decision.
  - Do not: indicate likely future changes in the operating objective. "Words can indeed unduly, and impractically, fence in practice."
  - On an explicit inflation target: the author doubts it would anchor expectations better. He approves of the third-year projection as an "ambiguous way to be on the side of the angels" under a dual mandate.

- **Institutional conservatism in analysis**: the Fed prefers to be "dull, accurate, and within a very defensible position rather than brilliant, adventurous, and possibly reckless". It fears the public cost of being wrong.
  - Corollary for advice: "insightful common sense" matters more than originality when making policy recommendations. Originality "might even be an impediment".

## Key Concepts
- **Institutional credibility**: defined above. The author's term.
- **Government Accountability Office audit**: extended after the crisis to the Fed's lending facilities.
- **Reserve bank governance change**: of nine reserve bank directors, the three who represent member banks can no longer vote for the bank's president. The author calls it "little but a warning shot".
- **Third-year projection**: from October 2007 the Fed updated forecasts quarterly and added a third year, which works as a soft inflation target.
- **Emergency loan authority**: the old power to lend to "individuals, partnerships, and corporations", used for Bear Stearns and AIG.
- **Continental Illinois**: the large Chicago bank the Fed helped rescue in the mid-1980s.

## Mental Models
- Think of the reserve bank network as "grass roots" political support, and of bankers on reserve bank boards as a source of both market knowledge and suspicion.
- Treat advice from a financial chief executive in a crisis as possibly tainted by loyalty to his own institution and by its exposure.
- Use the two theories of prediction with irony: predict often so the record is obscured, or predict rarely so none exists.
- Read Fed disclosure in a crisis as partly forced. Detailed programme data came from "prodding by the Congress" as well as Fed initiative.

## Anti-patterns
- **Letting the central bank become part of the problem**: see the Indonesia case below.
- **Neglecting non-monetary duties**: lapses on subprime mortgages, off-balance-sheet reporting and consumer protection damaged the whole institution's standing. The author adds that lapses were "endemic in the regulatory system".
- **Expecting monetary policy to deliver distributional goals**: policy aims at the whole economy. The Fed has no mandate or tool for income distribution.
- **Announcing a hard target under a dual mandate**.

## Key Takeaways
1. Track institutional credibility apart from policy credibility. Legislative encroachment is the indicator.
2. A rescue is a liquidity bridge for creditors. Judge it by systemic effect, not firm size.
3. Rescues spend the central bank's own credibility. The quality of its balance sheet matters.
4. After Dodd-Frank, expect the Treasury to be visibly in charge of emergency lending.
5. Prefer a central bank that states its current target and its forecast, and stops there.

# Chapter 2: In Bill Martin's Time

## Core Idea
Under Martin (1951 to early 1970) the Fed set money-market conditions and let money grow at whatever pace the public demanded. Martin's lasting work was procedural: numeric staff forecasts, a quantified operating directive, and control of policy by the Board in Washington. Inflation was rising when he left, because tightening was "too conservative and cautious".

## Regime Snapshot
| Item | Martin era |
|---|---|
| Operating guide | Free reserves (excess reserves minus borrowing from the Fed), set to give the FOMC's desired "money-market conditions" |
| Money-market conditions | Dealer loan rates, the three-month bill rate, and non-price factors such as reserve needs of city banks. The funds rate was not yet the key rate |
| Role of money supply | None as a target. Money was demand-determined |
| Instruments | Open market operations in Treasury bills ("bills-only"), discount rate |
| FOMC decision | Tighten, ease, or leave money-market conditions unchanged for a four- to six-week period |

## Frameworks Introduced (general method)

- **Rate control versus money control (the operating-target duality)**: "you can control an interest rate, and the supply of money to the public will depend on whatever stock of money is demanded at that rate. Or you can instead attempt to control the supply of money, and the interest rate will vary".
  - When to use: to classify any central bank operating regime and to predict rate volatility under it.
  - How: (1) Money demand depends on interest rates and income. (2) Policy does not move income in the short run. (3) So a short-run money target puts "the full brunt" of adjustment on interest rates. (4) A rate target makes money endogenous.
  - Why central banks resist money control: they fear that large rate swings send confusing signals and destabilise markets.

- **Timing asymmetry of policy with lags**: timing "is not quite everything, but it is crucial".
  - Late tightening: hitting hard later risks a deep recession.
  - Late easing: hitting hard later may fail because "there is nothing to hit". Attitudes are too negative to respond. Japan in the 1990s and early 2000s is the author's prime example.

- **Rules as a background check on judgement**: rules should not dominate judgement, but they help policymakers test whether a discretionary decision goes too far or not far enough.
  - Limits: the basis of a rule becomes outmoded as financial structure and attitudes change.
  - On the Taylor rule: more flexible than a money rule, but it needs the output gap and the neutral real short rate, both uncertain and revised. It also assumes a clear inflation objective and that the economy reacts as it did in the past.

- **Forecasts as indicative, not decisive**: read a numeric projection for direction and severity (strong or weak, dire or a blip, inflation inside or outside the comfort range). Weight the qualitative explanation over the number.
  - Failure mode, the **narrowed channel**: staff choose the least controversial forecast near the outside consensus to protect credibility. Policymakers prefer gradual change. Together they can unduly narrow policy thinking.
  - Policymaker self-confidence cuts both ways. Too little makes them captives of the staff forecast. Too much makes them ignore evidence against their preconceptions.

- **Lender of last resort as the reason for conservatism**: the central bank is the "deus ex machina" outside the financial system that can always supply credit and money. This duty explains its preference for small, smooth changes in market liquidity.

- **Staff influence scales with the chairman's strength**: the senior staff position had more influence under a strong chairman. Under a weak or uninterested chairman the FOMC "has no coherence", and staff cannot and should not fill the vacuum.

## Key Concepts
- **Bills-only policy**: operations confined to Treasury bills so that long rates reflect private supply and demand and keep their information content. Long markets were "thin". Bill markets had "breadth, depth, and resiliency".
- **Even-keel policy**: no change in stance around large quarterly Treasury financings priced by the Treasury. Dropped once the Treasury moved to auctions.
- **Green book**: the staff's numeric economic projections, begun under Dan Brill, normally assuming no change in policy.
- **Blue book**: the staff document, begun about 1965, that sets out alternative policy postures and quantifies money-market conditions.
- **Morning call**: daily call (about 11:00 a.m.) between the account manager in New York, a reserve bank president and senior Board staff. The manager took no action until the chairman was informed.
- **Quadriad**: Fed research head plus the Council of Economic Advisers, Budget Bureau and Treasury, preparing the budget's economic projections. It became a "troika" when Burns withdrew the Board in early 1970.
- **Membership problem**: state banks leaving or staying out of the Fed. The real worry was political, since the reserve bank network buffers the Fed in Congress. The Monetary Control Act of 1980 ended it by putting practically all depository institutions under reserve requirements.
- **Lagged reserve requirements**: required reserves based on deposits two weeks earlier.
- **M1 and M2**: M1 is currency and demand deposits. M2 adds time and savings deposits.

## Mental Models
- Think of the blue book as a power instrument. Numbers defining tightness "hemmed in" the New York account manager, and their interpretation ran through Board staff.
- Use the reserve bank network as a measure of the Fed's political insurance against periods when policy hurts small business, farmers and home owners.
- Treat fiscal and monetary "coordination" as a channel through which independence can leak in the normal give and take of joint forecasting.

## Anti-patterns
- **Trading monetary ease for fiscal tightening on a forecast**: see the worked example.
- **Trusting official spending estimates in wartime**: Vietnam outlays always exceeded budget figures. The author believes this made forecasts of activity and prices too low and contributed to slow tightening.
- **Reading a free-reserves target as a money target**: monetarists (Brunner, Meltzer, Homer Jones) showed free reserves do not control money. The author's reply was that the Fed was aiming at bank credit conditions, not money.
- **Overrating a two-week lag**: lagged reserve requirements delayed money control by at most two weeks, against an effective control horizon of three to six months. It still became a monetarist rallying cry.

## Dated Episodes (case material)
| Date | Episode |
|---|---|
| 1951 | Martin, as assistant Treasury secretary, negotiated the Accord that freed the Fed from supporting the government bond market. |
| Early 1960s | Brunner and Meltzer's report for the House Banking Committee: the Fed recognised cycle turns but did not act early enough, because it relied on free reserves. |
| About 1965 | Blue book created at Dan Brill's request. |
| Mid to late 1960s | Great Society and Vietnam spending widened the deficit. Johnson drove Martin over his ranch in a jeep to press against restriction. The Fed kept tightening, but too cautiously. |
| Late 1960s | Tax increase passed. The Fed cut the discount rate, then had to reverse it. |
| 1960s | Lagged reserve requirements adopted to ease member banks' costs. |
| Early 1970 | Burns ended Board participation in the quadriad. |

## Worked Example
The discount-rate cut after the late-1960s tax increase.
1. Congress passed a tax increase. An implicit political agreement held that tighter fiscal policy should bring easier money.
2. Most reserve bank boards resisted a cut, because inflation was fairly high and they doubted the economy would weaken much.
3. The Minneapolis Fed was persuaded to propose the cut. The others followed, by tradition and under the Board's power to "review and determine" discount rates.
4. The economy stayed stronger than forecast. The Fed reversed the cut.
5. Aftermath: a rumour that Board members felt misled by staff projections. Dan Brill left. Burns later withdrew from the quadriad.
Lesson: a policy move justified by a forecast shared with the administration carries political content, and the forecast error falls on the central bank's credibility.

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify the operating target first. It tells you whether money or rates absorb shocks.
2. Expect a judgement-based central bank to move gradually, and expect its staff forecast to sit near consensus.
3. Late action is costly in both directions, but late easing can fail outright.
4. Procedural changes (who writes and interprets the directive) decide who holds power inside the Fed.
5. Martin's "Well, we are not far apart" was a tool for cohesion, not a description.

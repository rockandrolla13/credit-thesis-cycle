# Chapter 6: The Greenspan Years, from Stability to Crisis

## Core Idea
Greenspan (August 1987 to early 2006) managed "the bulk of his tenure well": long prosperity with virtually no goods inflation. The last part was damaged by a stock-market crash, a recession "rescued primarily by fiscal policy", and easy money that "persisted too long, were too predictable, and helped sow the seeds of the great credit crisis". The author's central judgement is that telling markets the future path of the policy rate created a market-wide moral hazard. He writes as an outside observer.

## Regime Snapshot
| Item | Greenspan era |
|---|---|
| Operating target | Federal funds rate, announced after each meeting from 1994 |
| Guide to inflation risk | Real factors: growth against potential near full employment. Money "just about faded out" |
| Asset prices | Not a target. Policy's job was to "moderate adverse economic repercussions should bubbles burst" |
| Communication | Step by step more open: rate announced (1994), balance-of-risks wording, minutes after two weeks, explicit hints on the pace and direction of the rate |
| Decision principle, early 2000s | Risk management: guard against a low-probability, near-disastrous outcome (deflation) |
| Staff structure | Division of Monetary Affairs split from Research and Statistics, without the capital-markets function |

## Frameworks Introduced (general method)

- **The forward-guidance moral hazard**: it is "not merely the excessively low level of rates that was harmful but in particular the virtual guarantee that they would be around for years".
  - Mechanism: (1) The central bank signals that short rates will stay low or rise only slowly. (2) Refinancing risk on short-term debt funding long positions looks removed. (3) Participants borrow short and invest long with more leverage. (4) Long rates fail to rise when tightening starts. (5) The bubble grows until it bursts in the overleveraged sectors.
  - Tell: officials call low long rates a "quandary". The author's reading is that the market misperceived the Fed's own attitude toward long rates. "They did not look long and hard in the mirror."
  - A friend's summary: "Oh, you mean, they made it too easy for us."

- **Do not comment on future rates**: officials should avoid commenting on rates they do not control (long rates, stock yields) and on the future of the rate they do control.
  - Reason one: it taints the information that market rates send back to the central bank.
  - Reason two: misreadings can make spending and borrowing decisions worse for the economy.
  - Assumption to test: forward guidance presumes the Fed's forecast is better than the market's. "The future is unknowable both to the Fed and to the markets."
  - Limit of any guidance: it comes with "escape hatches, sometimes narrow and sometimes gaping".

- **Asset inflation as "too much money chasing too few goods"**: the goods are the existing capital stock.
  - Why it matters: equity inflation can create growth-threatening imbalances like goods inflation does.
  - Template, Japan in the 1990s: cheap equity and convertible financing built capital stock beyond what firms could profitably use. After the burst, the investment pullback and the bad-debt overhang prolonged weakness.
  - Contrast, the Greenspan view: bubbles are hard to gauge, so do not lean against them and clean up afterward. The author says the 2001 recovery seemed to support that view, and the great credit crisis "belies" it. He also judges the Fed "quite lucky" in 2001-2003, because the recovery owed much to fiscal expansion.

- **How the "Greenspan put" formed (three acts in about two years)**:
  1. A warning with no follow-up ("irrational exuberance", December 1996), then talk of a productivity boom that the market read as backtracking.
  2. Easing during market stress (three cuts, three quarters of a point in total, late 1998).
  3. Visible involvement in a private rescue (LTCM) at the same time as the easing.
  - Result: the market generalised that the Fed "would go to some lengths to rescue markets from their own worst behavior".

- **Systemic test for a rescue (the author's LTCM criteria)**: ask whether the failing firm matters to (a) the payments mechanism, or (b) the safety and soundness of banks and other holders of the bulk of the nation's liquid savings. The author thought a hedge fund, even one as large as LTCM, failed both tests and that the Fed overreacted. He allows that the Fed had fuller knowledge.

- **Judge the funds rate through capital-market conditions**: the real tightness of a given funds rate should be evaluated by the term and quality structure of interest rates and the cost and availability of credit, "in the same sense as in earlier periods its significance was evaluated by reference to money supply and bank credit".

- **The forecaster's real skill**: no forecaster is right most of the time. "What's really important is who most quickly recognizes that their forecast is turning out wrong."

- **Liquidity trap versus Pigou effect**: the Harvard answer was that money is useless at very low rates and fiscal policy is needed. The Chicago answer was that added money still raises real wealth and spending. The author calls it an empirical question and says Japan in the 1990s suggests "Keynes had a point". At a zero nominal rate with falling prices the real short rate is still positive.

## Key Concepts
- **Greenspan put**: market belief that losses could be put to the Fed. "Something of a joke", with enough seriousness to sustain bullish attitudes.
- **Measured pace**: FOMC wording from the summer of 2004 that accommodation would be removed "at a pace that is likely to be measured".
- **Considerable period**: earlier wording that "policy accommodation can be maintained for a considerable period", used when the funds rate was at its 1 percent low.
- **Margin requirements**: the Fed's legal power over the down payment on stock bought with credit. Held at 50 percent "for a great many years".
- **FOMC minutes code**: weight rises from "few" and "some" to "a number", "many" and "most". The only really valuable information is a forward-looking view of a chairman with stature.
- **Transcripts**: verbatim FOMC transcripts, published with a five-year lag after their existence became public in 1993.

## Mental Models
- Use the pivot image for the funds rate. It is "the pivotal rate around which the whole interest-rate structure... tends to revolve", not a rate anyone outside banks borrows at.
- Treat transparency as partly path-dependent. The author gives more weight to "piecemeal evolution", each step and its market reaction leading to the next, than to design.
- Pay serious attention only to the chairman's words. Other FOMC speeches deserve "some attention", not enough to move a position.
- Think of the cost of mining Fed speeches as probably above the benefit, given the risk of finding "something that is in reality simply not there".

## Anti-patterns
- **A warning without action**: "there was no follow-up action by the Fed", and the stock market kept rising.
- **Refusing a symbolic tool**: the author wanted a rise in margin requirements as a concrete sign. The Fed judged it futile because credit is fungible. The author agrees that the main restraint would have been "a more aggressive monetary policy".
- **A negative real rate in a recovery**: the real funds rate was "somewhat negative on average" from 2002 to 2005, "shades of Arthur Burns".
- **One cut too many**: with hindsight, the last quarter point of the 1998 easing "was a stage too far".
- **A chairman taking sides on tax specifics**: Greenspan at times supported an administration position on tax reduction. Chairmen favour fiscal restraint in general and avoid specifics.
- **Asking a chairman about future policy**: the Nikko president's opening question. Greenspan laughed and said, "Ask Steve."

## Worked Example
Tracing the housing bubble through the author's mechanism.
1. Starting point: stock crash and the 2001 recession. Risk-management policy holds the funds rate very low to insure against deflation, which the author thought "barely" possible.
2. Fiscal policy turns very expansive. The Fed accommodates it.
3. Statements promise accommodation for "a considerable period", then removal at a "measured" pace.
4. Tightening from mid-2004 is so gradual that the real funds rate averages below zero for 2002-2005.
5. Long rates do not rise. Officials call it a quandary.
6. The market reads the wording as "a license" to borrow short and invest long. Leverage builds in housing and other markets, helped by lax regulation.
7. The bubble bursts and spreads to other overleveraged markets (Chapter 7).
What the author would have done: drop the phrase earlier and raise the rate more quickly, though not as high.

## Key Takeaways
1. Predictability of the policy path is itself a stimulus to leverage.
2. Watch for a central bank whose warnings are not followed by action.
3. Test clean-up-afterward claims against who paid for the last clean-up. In 2001 the author credits fiscal policy.
4. Compare the real policy rate with the stage of the cycle.
5. Read a rescue by what it teaches the market, not only by what it costs.
6. Read minutes for the chairman's view, and discount the rest.

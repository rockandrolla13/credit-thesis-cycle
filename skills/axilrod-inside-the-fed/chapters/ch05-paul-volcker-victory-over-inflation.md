# Chapter 5: Paul Volcker and the Victory over Inflation

## Core Idea
From late 1979 to late 1982 the Volcker Fed stopped controlling the price of money and targeted its quantity through nonborrowed reserves, letting the funds rate move over a four- to six-point range. The author reads "practical monetarism" as the device that got interest rates as high as needed, fast, in a committee that could never have voted the funds rate there directly. The author credits the change to Volcker in person: "one of the few instances" where a policy shift came from a particular person and not mainly from circumstances.

## Regime Snapshot
| Item | Late 1979 to late 1982 | Late 1982 to 1987 |
|---|---|---|
| Operating target | Nonborrowed reserves, set from a short-term money path | Total bank borrowing at the Reserve Banks |
| Money objective | Annual money-growth objective, mainly M1 | Gradually diminishing role |
| Funds rate | Free within a band, usually four points (two up, two down), sometimes six | An expected funds rate was attached to each borrowing level in the blue book |
| Band status | Not absolute. A juncture for the FOMC to reassess | Not applicable |
| Reserve accounting | Lagged reserve requirements, kept | Not stated |
| Who held residual discretion | Staff path adjustments, cleared with the chairman | Chairman and market, through the loose borrowing-to-funds-rate link. The Board could also move the funds rate through the discount rate |

## Frameworks Introduced (general method)

- **Price or quantity, not both**: "one can control the price of a good or its quantity, but not both at the same time". Central banks have nearly always fixed a price: gold, foreign exchange, or a short rate.
  - Consequence of price control: if the public demands an inflationary amount of money, the Fed "willy-nilly" supplies it until the rate is changed. Conservative policymakers change it "too slowly, too incrementally".
  - Consequence of quantity control: excess money demand raises rates at once, with no vote.

- **The nonborrowed-reserves procedure**:
  1. The FOMC sets an annual money-growth objective.
  2. At each meeting it sets a short-term target path for money consistent with that objective.
  3. Staff convert the money path into a path for nonborrowed reserves through the reserves-to-money multiplier.
  4. The account manager supplies that quantity of reserves. He is not responsible for an interest rate.
  5. Staff recalibrate the path for changes in the composition of money that alter the multiplier. This step is technical.
  6. Staff also shift the path up or down when money veers too far from expectations, using "a consistent (more or less arbitrary) formula" based on the size of the deviation. This step is judgemental and changes pressure on rates.
  7. Staff report every adjustment to the chairman, then to the Board's weekly briefing, then to the FOMC.
  - Why it worked: the author trusted the multiplier, which rests on set institutional relationships, more than any estimate of the funds rate that would balance money demand and supply. On the latter, "the odds on being wrong were very high".
  - Failure mode: the judgemental step gives staff and chairman power the committee may not see. See Wallich's question below.

- **Why a money target can do what a rate target cannot (the committee-psychology argument)**:
  - Policymakers "were not psychologically capable" of voting the funds rate up far and fast. Staff could not have advised it.
  - A money target "finessed" this. Within the band, "no policy decision on the rate was required".
  - The money-to-inflation relationship was long studied, so the target was not arbitrary.
  - It appealed to the public through "too much money chasing too few goods".
  - It could be held for some time without frequent change.
  - Alternative the author states in retrospect: raise the funds rate quickly to a real level well above the real return on capital, a nominal rate of 15 to 20 percent. The real funds rate "was not an active element" in thinking then.

- **Four motives behind one vote**: do not look for a single reason. Voters included monetarist sympathisers, rate-channel believers with little faith in any money measure, pragmatists asking "How can we ever get this committee to move strongly enough?", and those who wanted a visible break to convince the public. The author subscribes to all four, with tepid monetarism.

- **Credibility through "stick to it"**: the audience is business and labour, not only dealers. The sooner lower inflation expectations enter pricing and wage bargaining, the shallower and shorter the recession.
  - Tell of a credibility threat: calling the regime an "experiment". Volcker "went ballistic" when a New York official did.
  - Order of conviction: short-term money markets first, bond markets later.

- **The thermostat rule**: the Fed is never an innocent bystander in money-market rates, whatever its stated target. It is the ultimate supplier of funds. The author rejects the view that the procedure was a cover to raise rates without responsibility.

- **Money as immeasurable**: a money stock with a stable relation to nominal output may exist, but "we can no longer find it". It is "hidden in all of the world's assets".

## Key Concepts
- **Practical monetarism**: Volcker's term. The author's reading is a money target used to raise rates with minimal delay.
- **Nonborrowed reserves**: reserves supplied directly through open market operations.
- **Borrowed-reserves target**: the later regime, "very close to targeting the federal funds rate... but literally was not".
- **Fed watching**: the profession that grew once the weekly money figure predicted rate moves.
- **Plaza Accord (September 1985)**: agreement among the United States, Germany, Japan, the United Kingdom and France, mainly to drive the dollar down against the yen.
- **Louvre Accord (February 1987)**: agreement to restore stability after the dollar's fall.

## Mental Models
- Use the stage test from Chapter 1. Volcker commanded the public stage and was shy on the private one. He lost the February 1986 vote on a private stage where the author thinks Burns might have seen the attack coming.
- Think of a shift in social attitudes as the enabling condition. The costs of inflation had become evident through stagnation, jobs lost to foreign competitors and eroded savings. Volcker saw "what the country would now accept".
- Treat fiscal expansion sold as supply-side as a demand risk for the central bank. The tax cut opened "explosive budget deficits", and the expected productivity gain did not arrive.
- Do not sell your own currency to push it down. Under the Plaza operating guidelines other countries sold dollars. The United States intervened mainly when the dollar tended to rise, to avoid a "panic-type flight".

## Anti-patterns
- **Credit controls as an anti-inflation tool**: Carter's request was "politically inspired and economically unnecessary". Consumers read the controls very adversely.
- **Officials of the same government attacking the central bank's method in public**: Treasury Undersecretary Beryl Sprinkel's statements undermined the credibility the Fed was rebuilding, until the administration moved to "place him under wraps".
- **Doctrinal purity over results**: monetarists objected to lagged reserve accounting and to nonborrowed reserves in place of the monetary base, and doubted control even over three to six months.
- **Writing monetary policy into an exchange-rate agreement**: Baker's draft said the Fed would consider lower rates. Volcker protested at once and the sentence came out.

## Worked Example
The discount-rate vote of February 24, 1986.
1. Setup: reserve banks had proposed discount-rate cuts for several weeks. The Board tabled them each week, and most were withdrawn.
2. Positions: the four Reagan appointees wanted a cut on economic grounds. Volcker was not convinced of the need and feared a unilateral cut would weaken a fragile dollar after the Plaza Accord.
3. Vote: the four insisted on a vote. Volcker lost four to three.
4. Aftermath that day: Volcker withdrew to his office and resignation was "in the air". Preston Martin chaired a tense session to draft the press release.
5. Reversal: that afternoon the Board voted unanimously to rescind. The annual report cited "a review of prospective actions by key central banks abroad".
6. Resolution: two weeks later the Board cut the rate, with Germany and Japan cutting at the same time. "Face was saved all around, but it was also lost."
7. Reading: the author suspects a personal power contest as much as economics. A Bank of Japan official later told him the bank "would not again let itself be put in that position".
Lesson: a chairman's public credibility does not protect him from a Board majority, and the administration backed down because a resignation would have hit the dollar.

Also from the chapter: Henry Wallich asked why he had to vote on the discount rate to raise rates between meetings when staff could do it by shifting the reserve path. The author's answer was that the committee voted for the procedure, and that the adjustment was cleared with the chairman.

## Key Takeaways
1. An operating-target change can be the way to deliver a rate path the committee would not vote for.
2. In a quantity regime, expect rate moves to "accumulate much more quickly" and watch the data release that drives the reserve path.
3. Credibility needs both action and repeated rhetoric, aimed at wage and price setters.
4. Every "automatic" procedure hides judgement. Find who makes the adjustment and who clears it.
5. Regimes end when their purpose is met and when financial innovation breaks the indicator.
6. Disinflation cost two recessions, and went faster than its engineers expected.

# Chapter 3: Arthur Burns and the Struggle against Inflation

## Core Idea
Under Burns (end of January 1970 to early 1978) the Fed "aimed more at containing rather than suppressing" inflation. It adopted money-growth ranges but kept the funds rate as the operating target and rebased the ranges every quarter, so the targets carried no weight. Markets came to see money targeting as a pretence, and the Fed lost its anti-inflation credibility.

## Regime Snapshot
| Item | Burns era |
|---|---|
| Operating target | Money-market and bank liquidity conditions, "characterized in particular by the federal funds rate" |
| Role of money | Short-run ranges for the intermeeting period from early in the decade. Annual target ranges from March 1975 |
| Link between the two | The proviso clause: the funds rate could move a bit further if money growth left its intermeeting range |
| Size of that move | Generally limited to one half or three quarters of a percentage point between meetings |
| Money measure that counted | M1 internally, though three to five measures were published |
| Indicator the FOMC did not use | The real funds rate |

## Frameworks Introduced (general method)

- **Real funds rate as the stance test**: nominal funds rate minus the concurrent rise in consumer prices.
  - When to use: to check whether a central bank that says it is restraining inflation is doing so.
  - How: (1) Compute the real rate. (2) Compare its direction with real GDP growth and inflation. (3) A falling or negative real rate while inflation rises means "no real restraint".
  - Evidence in the text: the real rate fell during 1971-1973 as real GDP growth rose. It was negative on average from 1974 through 1977, and negative or about zero for the rest of the decade. FOMC discussion of whether policy was too tight or too easy gave it little or no consideration. Policy "always seemed to be" about right once in place.

- **Base drift (Henry Wallich's term)**: a one-year money range that is reset from the actual level every three months forgives each overshoot.
  - How to detect: check whether the next target range starts from the old target path or from the actual outcome. If from the outcome, the range has "no practical significance".
  - Failure mode: publishing several money measures in the hope that one or two land inside their ranges makes the exercise look "rather deceptive".

- **Money-demand shift diagnosis ("the case of the missing money")**: financial innovation can make measured money understate monetary ease.
  - How: (1) Monitor money-demand equations (money against income and interest rates). (2) A miss for one quarter is normal. A miss for a full year is "not extremely unusual". A miss into a second year forces attention. (3) Adjust measured growth for the estimated shift. (4) Cross-check against a broader aggregate that the shift does not affect.
  - Recognition lags: about a year for staff to be reasonably certain, then a further lag for policymakers. The lag is longer when the unadjusted figure is inside its target, since "harassed policymakers" have no incentive to "believe ill of such figures".

- **The financial-fragility constraint (Scylla and Charybdis)**: deposit rate ceilings made fast rate rises dangerous either way.
  - If ceilings rise quickly: thrift funding costs outrun fixed-rate mortgage returns.
  - If ceilings do not rise: deposits drain, thrifts sell assets at a loss, capital erodes, and a drain can become a run.
  - Use: before expecting a central bank to tighten hard, ask which regulated balance sheets break first.

- **Public-conflict aversion**: opposition to tight money comes from a diffuse public (consumers, business, elected officials). Leaders who will stage conflict in private may shy from it in public, and so "miss the cues that call for risking bold policy action".
  - Tell: the chairman talks a good anti-inflation game, but the proposals are for other branches of government (deficits, labour and product market flexibility).

- **Limits and use of a chairman's power**: eleven other voters hold independent views.
  - The FOMC gives the chairman a little extra weight because he must defend the policy before Congress.
  - Public credibility adds influence. Overreaching what the FOMC accepts loses it. Never exerting leadership makes him "a neuter".
  - Chairmen "abhorred dissents". More than one or two reflect on his stature, so the directive is tuned to pass with few.

- **Staff objectivity rule**: no chairman interfered with the staff's policy alternatives. If members thought the chairman compromised staff objectivity, the backlash would undermine his own influence. Staff must not shade a best judgement to make the chairman's job easier.

## Key Concepts
- **Proviso clause**: the directive wording that tied small funds-rate changes to money growth between meetings.
- **House Concurrent Resolution 133**: led to the first one-year money targets, for March 1975 to March 1976. Four overlapping one-year targets were then set each year.
- **Humphrey-Hawkins Act (Full Employment and Balanced Growth Act of 1978)**: targets set for calendar years, starting with the year ending in the fourth quarter of 1979.
- **Deposit rate ceilings**: regulatory caps on bank and thrift deposit rates, abandoned by about the mid-1980s.
- **Beige book origin**: regional reports that Burns moved into a written document with a red cover. It was later made public because it gave "the least insight into policy", and the cover became beige.

## Mental Models
- Think of Burns's view as a business cycle with "a powerful inner dynamic" independent of the money stock over a wide range. The same money stock can turn over faster. That view removes urgency from policy timing.
- Contrast with Friedman: money growth drives fluctuations with a lag. The author thinks Friedman overstated it, but "there was something to what he was arguing". Both camps accepted that long-run inflation is monetary, with full price effects after perhaps a year or two.
- Treat "inside the box" manoeuvres (meeting order, no coffee break, sandwiches at the table, trimming others' presentations) as process control, not policy. "Bureaucratic maneuverings of one sort or another do not control inflation."

## Anti-patterns
- **Half-hearted targeting**: adopting a guide the institution only half believes in.
- **Judging stance by the nominal rate or by a narrow aggregate alone**.
- **Blaming the ethos of the time**: Burns's Per Jacobsson lecture argued that policy is limited by the political and social ethos. The author reads this as "either a recognition that he had not risen to the occasion or a rationalization".
- **Incrementalism in an inflation that needs a regime change**: caution is a virtue in normal times and "a real limit" in these.

## Dated Episodes (case material)
| Date | Episode |
|---|---|
| 1960s | CPI inflation averaged about 2½ percent, and about 4¼ percent in Martin's last four years. |
| 1970s | Inflation averaged close to 7½ percent. |
| 1971-1973 | Devaluation crisis. The United States "in effect went off gold". Money growth accelerated early in the crisis. |
| 1971-1972 | Modest price rises under the Nixon wage-price controls, which were unsustainable and abandoned. |
| Before the 1972 election | The White House spread gossip that Burns sought a pay raise. John Ehrlichman told the author and Chuck Partee to ask each morning, "What can I do today to get the money supply up?" The author says Burns resisted this pressure. |
| 1973-1974 | First oil shock. Inflation averaged 10½ percent. A sixteen-month recession followed. |
| 1975-1977 | Inflation fell back to a 5 to 7 percent range. |
| 1975-1976 | Missing money. See the worked example. |
| Early 1978 | Carter did not reappoint Burns and chose G. William Miller. |

The author's verdict: Burns "probably did about as well as, or maybe only a little worse than, any other likely choice would have done in the circumstances".

## Worked Example
Reading the 1975-1976 stance three ways.
1. Headline: M1 grew about 5½ percent a year, generally inside its targets. Real GDP growth was weak early in the period. Policy looked acceptable.
2. Shift estimate: a bank sample suggested about two percentage points of funds moved out of M1. Economy-wide money-demand equations later suggested three to four points in each of the two years.
3. Adjusted reading: the effect on activity and inflation matched M1 growth "on the order of 8 to 10 percent".
4. Cross-check one: M2, which includes the time and savings deposits the funds moved into, accelerated "into the low double-digit rates".
5. Cross-check two: the real funds rate was negative on average.
Conclusion: policy was much more expansive than the targeted figure showed. A target met for the wrong reason hides the error.

## Key Takeaways
1. Test stated policy against the real policy rate and against a broad aggregate.
2. A target that is rebased to actual outcomes is not a target.
3. Expect one to two years between a structural shift in a monetary relationship and a policy response.
4. Financial-system fragility and absent public support both reinforce step-by-step policy.
5. Once credibility is lost, inflation expectations become a driver of actual inflation.

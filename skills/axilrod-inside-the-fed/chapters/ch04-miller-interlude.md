# Chapter 4: The Miller Interlude

## Core Idea
In G. William Miller's seventeen months (early 1978 to August 1979) the Fed took no stronger stance against inflation, and belief that its monetary targeting was "essentially a sham" spread. The dollar came under attack, and the authorities answered with intervention, swap lines and foreign-currency bonds. The author's point is that such devices are holding actions. Only a change in monetary policy restores willingness to hold a currency. This chapter is thin on frameworks and is mostly dated episode.

## Regime Snapshot
| Item | Miller era |
|---|---|
| Operating target | Unchanged from Burns: funds rate and money-market conditions, with money ranges |
| Chairman's involvement in daily operations | The least of the chairmen the author served |
| FOMC coherence | Weak. A leadership vacuum that nobody on the committee filled |
| Main policy activity | Exchange-market defence of the dollar |

## Frameworks Introduced (general method)

- **Intervention is a holding action without a policy change**: the volume of dollars that can be sold is "almost limitless" against the foreign exchange the authorities hold or can borrow.
  - When to use: to judge a currency-defence package.
  - How: (1) Size the ammunition (reserves, swap lines, foreign-currency borrowing) against market volume. It is "a drop in the huge market bucket". (2) Ask whether domestic monetary policy changed. (3) Ask whether intervention is unilateral or multilateral. Unilateral works much less well. (4) Without a supporting monetary change, expect the defence to fail once attitudes get out of hand.

- **Who decides exchange-market policy**: the Treasury Secretary has the final say on whether, how and in what currencies to intervene. The Fed operates for its own account and as the Treasury's agent. The Fed cannot operate for its own account without FOMC authorisation, which gives it some leverage. The author thinks it unlikely the Fed would intervene without Treasury consent or refuse a government request.

- **Staff should not recommend the stance of policy**: the FOMC works best when staff give an objective assessment of the outlook and of likely reactions to each option, and stop there.
  - Why: FOMC staff come from the Board. Reserve bank presidents are sensitive to dominance by the Washington core.
  - Exception: technical matters (repo collateral, which maturity sector to buy) and Board-only instruments (discount rate, reserve requirements).
  - Failure mode: staff recommendations sit in the mainstream and add nothing. More adventurous ones would cast doubt on staff objectivity.

- **CEO temperament in a committee institution**: a chief executive used to holding the reins meets six or eleven equal voters, unstated motives, and policy levers only loosely tied to goals by uncertainty and long lags. Monetary policy was "too amorphous" for Miller's temperament.

- **Diffused staff power**: the Board has no chief of staff. Members resist one because a staff chief beholden to the chairman could control the content of what reaches the Board.

## Key Concepts
- **Swap network**: reciprocal lines with foreign central banks. The Fed obtains foreign currency (for example deutschmarks from the Bundesbank) against dollars, "something like a loan agreement".
- **Carter bonds**: United States government bonds denominated in foreign currency, part of the 1978 dollar-support package. Only a modest amount were sold, in the German and Swiss markets.
- **Eurodollars**: dollar deposits in banks abroad, mostly free of reserve requirements, so foreign banks could pay slightly more than banks in the United States.
- **G-11 central bankers**: the group meeting monthly at the Bank for International Settlements in Basel that considered the Eurodollar proposal.

## Mental Models
- Think of offshore dollar deposits as inside monetary control through price. They earn United States interest rates, so the Fed's influence on the funds rate carries over "to virtually the same degree". The author did not see them as much of a threat.
- Use "any little step seemed helpful" as a sign of how low credibility was. A proposal judged mainly by its signal value is a symptom.
- Read a concession from the administration as possibly a trade. The author suspects support for foreign-currency bonds was "tossing a bone" to keep money from tightening beyond the modest firming in the package.

## Anti-patterns
- **Foreign-currency borrowing as a signal**: perhaps sound financially, but politicians read it as "a public confession of failure", an admission that the country must seek help from foreigners. Later administrations resolved never to repeat it.
- **Asking other countries to change their banking structure for your credibility problem**: foreign central banks saw little reason to burden their banks for a Fed "viewed as not doing enough on its own".
- **Expecting staff or a committee member to fill a chairman's vacuum**: none did, and the author doubts anyone could.

## Dated Episodes (case material)
| Date | Episode |
|---|---|
| Early 1978 | Miller, former chief executive of Textron and a Boston Fed director, becomes chairman. |
| 1978 | Miller asks the author to give the FOMC an overt policy recommendation. The author complies, against his instincts. The practice stops when Volcker arrives. |
| 1978 | Dollar-support package: intervention, larger swap commitments, modest monetary firming, and Carter bonds. Treasury staff argued the bonds were hard to manage, of doubtful legality and politically risky. |
| Late 1970s | Second oil-price shock. Inflation intensifies. |
| 1978-1979 | Miller proposes reserve requirements on Eurodollars to the G-11 at Basel. See the worked example. |
| August 1979 | Miller leaves to become Treasury Secretary. Carter nominates Paul Volcker, then New York Fed president, who called it a "bolt from the blue". The author credits Volcker's international reputation from his years as Treasury undersecretary in the early 1970s. |
| Reagan years (narrated here) | The Treasury opposed intervention on principle. Undersecretary Beryl Sprinkel's standard reply to the author's suggestions was that he would check with Secretary Regan and get back. |

## Worked Example
The Eurodollar reserve-requirement proposal.
1. Motive: show more resolve on inflation. The author also credits the Bundesbank, which worried about German banks setting up subsidiaries abroad, for example in Liechtenstein. The United States wanted German cooperation on intervention.
2. Proposal: a reserve requirement on Eurodollars at least equal to the requirement on large certificates of deposit at major United States banks.
3. Reception: Fritz Leutwiler of the Swiss National Bank, who chaired the group, did not discourage Miller. The meeting was cold. Miller left in "genuine fury" and, as the author recalls, never attended another session.
4. Outcome: a parent committee on Eurocurrency issues under Alex Lamfalussy and a subcommittee on reserve requirements chaired by the author.
5. Technical finding: a workable system existed, but it had to cover all Eurocurrencies. A Euro-deutschmark sold forward into dollars at creation is in effect a Eurodollar.
6. Result: the subcommittee majority voted against. The Bank of England feared any damage to the City of London. The parent committee settled for "prudential exhortations and data gathering".
Lesson: a central bank with weak credibility cannot get others to bear costs for it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat intervention, swap lines and foreign-currency debt as signs of stress, not solutions.
2. A dollar crisis ends when monetary policy changes. That change came in the next chapter.
3. A chairman without interest in the committee process leaves the FOMC without coherence.
4. Keep staff analysis separate from the policy vote.

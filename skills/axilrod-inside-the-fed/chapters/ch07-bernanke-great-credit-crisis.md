# Chapter 7: Bernanke and the Response to the Great Credit Crisis

## Core Idea
Bernanke (from February 2006) inherited policy that had been too easy and a mortgage market that depended on rising home prices and low rates. The author's verdict: the Fed lagged "for too long early in the crisis", failed to show it grasped the danger, let Lehman fail with disastrous effect, then "in close to the nick of time" stretched its legal authority to the limit and "came to acquit itself fairly well". Official mishandling in September and October 2008 made the recession "deeper than it need have been". The text went to press toward the end of 2010.

## Regime Snapshot
| Item | Bernanke era to late 2010 |
|---|---|
| Operating target | Funds rate. Peak 5¼ percent from early summer 2006 for about fifteen months. 2 percent from the end of April 2008. A range of 0 to ¼ percent from mid-December 2008 |
| Guide to inflation risk | Real conditions plus indicators of inflation expectations |
| Lender-of-last-resort reach | Extended from banks to primary dealers, an investment bank, an insurer, money and commercial paper markets, and foreign central banks |
| Balance sheet | Something over $900 billion in early September 2008. Nearly $2.3 trillion by mid-December 2008. Held near there through early fall 2010 |
| Forward wording | Rates "exceptionally low... for an extended period", from January 2009 |
| Position relative to markets | "More like an integral part of the private market" than a deus ex machina outside it |

## Frameworks Introduced (general method)

- **Four stages of a crisis response** (the author's device for judging leadership):
  1. Late 2007 to September 2008. Traditional role: more active discount window, auction lending, one emergency loan. Balance sheet near normal, because lending was offset by lower securities holdings. Communication failed. The market felt the Fed "did not 'get it'".
  2. From September 2008. The system became systemically unstable, "in good part the result... of official actions themselves". The Fed looked dominated by the administration, and its independence "came to seem a chimera".
  3. From about spring 2009. Stature regained: use of liquidity facilities falling, sustained long-term purchases, no haste to drain excess reserves, positive real growth in the last two quarters of 2009.
  4. From spring 2010. Aftermath: managing a slow return to normal. Aftermaths "can be, and often are, long, arduous, and uncertain".

- **The leverage spiral ("house of cards")**:
  1. High leverage means a modest price fall removes the holder's equity.
  2. Lenders call for cash or collateral faster as their safety margins erode.
  3. Borrowers sell other assets to meet the calls.
  4. Good collateral becomes scarce, and prices of other assets fall.
  5. The problem spreads across interconnected markets, at home and abroad.
  - Ranking of causes: "the uncontained spirit of leverage and high-stakes gambling" contributed most. Slack regulation was "permissive" and "probably close to being a sine qua non". Behind both was a culture that valued risky investment and devalued prudence.

- **Who a rescue rescues (creditor moral hazard)**:
  - Executives lose reputation and position. Shareholders lose nearly all their equity. Being rescued "is no bed of roses".
  - The main beneficiaries are the firm's creditors, who are paid more promptly than in bankruptcy.
  - So the more likely moral hazard is that creditors skip due diligence, not that managers take more risk. The author thinks managers' risk taking comes from temperament and competitive pressure.

- **The horse-to-water limit, when it is real**: after a crisis, households and firms hold back on borrowing and banks prefer excess reserves. Then "fiscal policy is the more effective expansionary instrument".
  - What monetary policy can still do: shift the balance sheet toward long-term securities. The author would also have paid no interest on excess reserves, as a tangible sign of concern about bank lending.

- **Balance-sheet mechanics and exit**:
  - Expansion: the Fed pays for assets by crediting banks' reserve accounts. The result is higher excess reserves, higher money, or both, depending on how far banks lend.
  - Exit tools the author lists: let securities mature or sell them, short-term reverse repurchase agreements against the long-term holdings, interest on excess reserves, and term deposits for banks.
  - Judgement: "no very significant technical difficulties". The constraint is the chairman's public stature in a tense aftermath.

- **Neutral for the economy, too high for the financial structure**: the 5¼ percent funds rate was "perhaps... neutral or more likely the high side of neutral" for the economy and inflation, but "too high" given latent instability in mortgage and credit markets. A rate has to be judged against the fragility built up under the previous regime.

- **Mark-to-market in a panic**: literal marking when markets barely function "tended to perpetuate irrationality". The author suggests models that filter out very short-term price swings until they persist.

## Key Concepts
- **Term Auction Facility (TAF)**: from late 2007, banks bid for longer-term discount-window funds. "Quite creative (though... rather belated)".
- **Primary dealer lending facility**: overnight credit from mid-March 2008 to the twenty primary dealers against a broad range of investment-grade collateral.
- **TARP**: the Treasury programme first proposed to buy troubled assets, used in the end to inject capital.
- **TALF**: loans against asset-backed securities, "vastly underused" relative to the potential first indicated.
- **Emergency lending rule at the time**: collateral satisfactory to the lending reserve bank and five votes at the Board.
- **Interest on reserves**: power granted with the revised TARP law. The rate paid was 25 basis points.
- **Extended period**: the forward wording that the author finds "rather too reminiscent" of the late Greenspan directives.

## Mental Models
- Think of early inaction as a signal. Not putting safeguards in place earlier "helped to signal that the Fed was not particularly worried".
- Use confidence as a transmission channel. After official mishandling, the most postponable spending, durable goods, "declined immediately and spectacularly".
- Treat the dollar funding market as global. Dollar LIBOR rose to an unusually large premium over the funds target, and swap drawings peaked above the International Monetary Fund's total usable resources.
- Read interbank stress as doubt about the big banks. They were the borrowers in the funds market, and smaller banks the suppliers.

## Anti-patterns
- **Guidance that outlives its purpose**: the "measured pace" phrase should have gone "much earlier", with the funds rate rising more quickly, though not as high.
- **Over-weighting moral hazard at the worst moment**: on Lehman, the Fed "perhaps worried a bit too much about moral hazard... disastrously failed".
- **No contingency plan**: the Treasury's first TARP proposal showed no earlier planning, though the risk "had been so clear for many months".
- **Changing the remedy mid-course**: TARP moved from asset purchases to capital injections not clearly tied to each institution's position. Later bonuses fed public suspicion.
- **Academic-sounding communication in a panic**: "Some leadership charisma was required."
- **Paying banks to hold idle reserves in a weak recovery**.

## Worked Example
The author's reading of the Lehman decision.
1. Context: the market expected a rescue after Bear Stearns. Stocks were falling again and quality spreads were wider, but conditions still looked "shaky though as yet systemically stable".
2. Possible reasons for refusing: a wish to stress moral hazard, doubts about collateral, doubt about five Board votes, and no explicit Treasury support. The author guesses a combination.
3. Underlying error: the Fed "had not yet fully grasped how extremely weak and tenuous" sentiment was.
4. Consequences: stocks in something like free fall, wider risk spreads, short-term funding "virtually dried up", and the AIG loan a day later.
5. Weighting: the author thinks the failure contributed "to a significant extent", alongside TARP's handling and doubt about governments' capacity to respond.
6. Counterfactual the author offers elsewhere in the chapter: buy a moderate amount of long-term securities as early as the summer of 2008.

## Key Takeaways
1. Date a crisis by the authority's stance, not only by market prices. Stage two began with an official decision.
2. Leverage and collateral scarcity, not the size of the first loss, turn a sector problem into a systemic one.
3. In an aftermath, expect fiscal policy to carry recovery and excess reserves to sit idle.
4. Exit from a large balance sheet is a political and communication problem more than a technical one.
5. Watch for repeated "extended period" style promises. The author regards them as the seed of the previous bubble.
6. Rescues protect creditors. Look for weaker creditor discipline afterward.

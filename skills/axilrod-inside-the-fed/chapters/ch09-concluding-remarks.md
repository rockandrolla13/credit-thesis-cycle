# Chapter 9: Concluding Remarks

## Core Idea
Structural change in Fed policy does not happen without the chairman's lead, and it dies if he opposes it. In normal meetings his extra weight is "surprisingly limited" and must be earned. The author ranks the chairmen, then argues that future policy needs more judgement, not less: the neutral rate is "something of a guess", money can no longer be found, and the Fed should lean somewhat against asset inflation, use prudential tools early, and stop ignoring the rest of the world.

## Frameworks Introduced (general method)

- **Where the chairman matters**:
  - Structural change in how policy is implemented: needs his leadership. Other members' proposals fail if he strongly opposes them.
  - Routine tighten-or-ease decisions: the FOMC gives him "the benefit of the doubt" because he must defend the policy, but only up to a point.
  - No leadership: if the chairman only searches for consensus, "the committee will almost certainly flounder".
  - The subcommittee device: Burns used one (Maisel) to bury a reform. Bernanke used one (under Don Kohn) to get a form of inflation targeting the FOMC could accept.

- **Scorecard of the chairmen** (the author's judgements):

| Chairman | Circumstances | Leadership verdict | Main error the author names |
|---|---|---|---|
| Martin | Low inflation until the last four years of the 1960s. No dramatic crisis | Kept the real funds rate positive, to his credit | Did not tighten aggressively enough. The real funds rate was little changed while inflation rose |
| Burns | Great inflation, fragile regulated institutions, no public support | Kept influence through intellect and personality. "Excessively cautious" | "Pretended to implement a vaguely monetarist policy... but did not really mean it". Lost all anti-inflation credibility |
| Miller | Second oil shock, dollar crisis | Not ranked here. See Chapter 4 | None added here |
| Volcker | A decade of inflation and damaged credibility | Innovative on the largest scale. Deliberate and individual, with no groundswell for his method | Influence waned late in his second term after conflict with Reagan appointees |
| Greenspan | Inflation already reduced, modernised markets, a non-inflationary world | Good for most of his tenure. Opened communication, "long overdue" | Verbal suasion plus very easy money after 2000 created market-wide moral hazard. More open to question than Martin |
| Bernanke | Great credit crisis, for which the public held him partly responsible "with some reason" | Innovative "in a more workman-like way... once he realized the fix" | Recognition lags that worsened the crisis |

- **Volcker and Bernanke recessions compared**: the contraction under Bernanke lasted only two months longer than the post-1979 recession (leaving aside the credit-control episode), but was faster and deeper. Real GDP grew about 3 percent in the first four quarters of recovery, against about 7¾ percent at the same stage under Volcker.
  - Why: disinflation released pent-up demand and "a sense of relief". The credit crisis left a lasting blow to confidence. The author assigns part of the gap to recognition lags and the public contention they caused.

- **Evolution of the inflation guide**: money (as inflation surged), then away from money, then real variables (the output gap and the real rate against neutral), then inflation expectations.
  - Neutral rate: "the level that will neither inhibit the economy from growing at its potential nor encourage excessive inflation". Its inputs are uncertain: the real return on capital, attitudes to risk, and stock valuation relative to the capital stock.
  - Rules: Friedman-type and Taylor-type rules help "only as background music". "What counts are the current flows of data and judgments about them."

- **Where is the inflationary money now?** The author's open questions: in home equity and stocks, in foreign holdings of United States government securities, in other tradable assets worldwide. Inflation may still be "too much money chasing too few goods", but the money cannot be measured.

- **The asset-bubble dilemma**:
  - Choice one: raise rates sooner or further than the economy needs, at the risk of weakening it.
  - Choice two: leave policy unchanged and let imbalances grow until a worse collapse.
  - The authorities "have generally avoided the first" and ended with "the second and, I would say, worse alternative".
  - The author's position: focus on goods-price stability in the long run, but adjust policy "to an extent" against excessive capital-asset inflation.

- **Prudential tools as early signals**: margin requirements, bank holding company capital and liquidity ratios, and Dodd-Frank powers over systemically important firms.
  - Limit: money and credit are fungible, at home and abroad, so the direct restraint may be small.
  - Value: they "alter market attitudes" because they signal policy concern early. The author thinks early prudential action on mortgages would have moderated the crisis.
  - Reporting proposal: include a view on systemic stability in the semi-annual monetary policy report and testimony. Risk: it turns "benign and pro forma".

- **Operating flexibility proposals**:
  1. Give the funds-rate target "a little give", at least an implicit range, with discretion delegated to the chairman between meetings. Let the rate dip to supply liquidity under stress or rise as a signal to exuberant markets.
  2. Use foreign-exchange operations as a minor, occasional complement, "showing the flag".

- **International constraint**: the Fed "had the luxury of more or less ignoring the rest of the world" and "may have overindulged". It was passive about the growing current-account deficit and the inflow of foreign saving, which the author thinks facilitated the credit crisis. Congress resists any idea of policy set in response to events abroad.

- **Structure and people**:
  - Keep the reserve bank structure, though outdated. Choose reserve bank presidents mainly for supervisory and operational insight.
  - Want a more diverse leadership mix to add "street cred" to economists' skills and their "sometimes unfortunately misplaced faith" in models.
  - Give supervision the same stature inside the Fed as monetary policy.
  - Traits of a chairman with the best odds: leadership charisma, a feel for when to act "outside the box" and the courage to do it, empathetic communication, and "native good judgment and plain old common sense". Add an intuitive feel for markets.

## Key Concepts
- **Recognition lag**: delay in seeing the severity of a developing problem. The author's main charge against the Bernanke Fed and other regulators.
- **Neutral real funds rate**: defined above. A guess in practice.
- **Countercyclical capital requirement**: a tool Dodd-Frank asks regulators to consider.
- **Vice chairman for supervision**: new post that must report to Congress twice a year.
- **Discount rate rule**: now set by regulation at a fixed spread to the funds target, which makes reserve bank discount-rate recommendations "practically irrelevant".

## Mental Models
- Think of speculative bubbles as having "replaced inflation as the bane of Fed policy".
- Use the phrase "the more things change, the more the underlying dilemmas... remain the same" as a warning against any indicator that claims to remove judgement.
- Treat a chairman's record as part person and part circumstance. Asking how one would have done in another's place is "little better than a guessing game".
- Expect monetary independence to narrow as other large currency areas emerge (the euro area, later China, Brazil, India), but not to vanish short of a world currency.

## Anti-patterns
- **Publicising targets you then ignore**: if money measures are announced as targets, "you cannot also be seen as ignoring them" without a convincing explanation.
- **Ignoring the real rate**: the author accepts the charge that the Fed in the late 1960s and after was "not very well focused, if at all" on the difference between real and nominal rates.
- **Faith that markets will unwind bubbles in an orderly way**: "a misguided faith".
- **Risk management that creates risk**: easy money to insure against a small deflation threat encouraged the market itself to take excessive risk.
- **Consensus-seeking in place of leadership**.

## Dated Episodes (case material)
| Date | Episode |
|---|---|
| Last four years of the 1960s | Inflation rose. Martin's real funds rate stayed positive but did not rise. |
| 1970s | Maisel subcommittee proposed "reserves against private deposits" as an operating guide. Burns had staff track it as an experiment in the blue book, and "the proposal died of its own weight". |
| Late Burns and Miller years | Volcker was vice chairman of the FOMC, as New York Fed president. |
| After 2000 | Easy money justified on risk-management grounds. |
| 2000s | United States current-account deficit widened. China's surplus surged. |
| 2010 | Euro-area sovereign debt crisis. Dodd-Frank Act. |

## Worked Example
Using the author's dilemma on the mid-2000s housing market.
1. Signal: a credit-sector bubble appears to be forming while goods inflation is modest.
2. Choice one would be a faster rise in the funds rate than the economy alone required. The author says elsewhere that it should have risen "more quickly (though not quite as high)".
3. Choice two, which was taken, was to keep the measured path and rely on markets to adjust.
4. Third route the author adds: early prudential action on mortgage lending and on bank holding company exposures, used as a signal even if credit is fungible.
5. Fourth: state the stability concern in the semi-annual report and testimony.
6. Outcome of choice two: collapse, a deep recession and a weak recovery at about 3 percent growth in its first year.
The steps restate positions the author takes in Chapters 6, 7 and 9. He does not set them out as a numbered case.

## Key Takeaways
1. For regime change, watch the chairman. For routine moves, watch the committee.
2. Compare chairmen by the real funds rate they kept and by whether announced guides were honoured.
3. Expect the Fed to keep choosing clean-up over leaning against bubbles unless prudential tools are used early.
4. Treat any estimate of the neutral rate as a judgement call.
5. Factor foreign saving flows and exchange markets into a reading of United States policy.
6. Supervisory failure costs monetary policy its room to act.

# Patterns: Inside the Fed (Axilrod)

Reusable methods the author states or applies. Each names its source chapter. These are general method. The episodes behind them are in the chapter files under "Dated Episodes".

## Classify the operating regime (price or quantity)
**When to use**: first step in reading any central bank.
**How**: ask what the trading desk is held to. A rate (or free reserves set to give a rate) makes money demand-determined. A reserve quantity makes the rate absorb every demand shock. Check the width of the tolerated rate band: one half to three quarters of a point under Burns, four to six points under Volcker.
**Trade-offs**: rate control gives calm markets and slow reaction to inflation. Quantity control gives fast rate moves and high volatility. (Ch 2, Ch 5)

## Real policy rate test
**When to use**: to check a claim that policy is restraining inflation.
**How**: nominal funds rate minus concurrent CPI inflation, using total CPI. Compare with the stage of the cycle. Negative or flat while inflation rises means no restraint.
**Trade-offs**: simple, and the Fed itself ignored it in the 1960s and 1970s. It says nothing about financial fragility: 5¼ percent was about neutral for the economy in 2006 and too high for the mortgage market. (Ch 3, Ch 6, Ch 7, Ch 9)

## Target-honesty check (base drift)
**When to use**: whenever a central bank announces a quantitative guide.
**How**: (1) Is the next target set from the old path or from the actual outcome? (2) How many alternative measures are published? (3) Does the operating instrument move enough to hit it?
**Trade-offs**: a guide that fails these checks costs more credibility than having no guide. (Ch 3, Ch 9)

## Structural-shift detection in a monetary relationship
**When to use**: an indicator stops tracking its usual determinants.
**How**: one quarter of forecast error is noise. One year is not extremely unusual. A second year is a shift. Estimate its size, adjust the indicator, and cross-check with a broader measure the shift does not touch.
**Trade-offs**: staff need about a year and policymakers longer. The lag is worst when the unadjusted figure is on target. (Ch 3)

## Feasibility test for a regime change (stars in alignment)
**When to use**: to judge whether a central bank can make a paradigm shift.
**How**: check (1) public recognition of the cost of the status quo, (2) market-structure changes that spread the gains of the new policy, (3) fragile regulated balance sheets that would break, (4) a chairman suited to a public stage, (5) technical readiness of a procedure.
**Trade-offs**: the same chairman can fail on a stage that does not suit him. (Ch 1, Ch 3, Ch 5)

## Use an operating-target change to get past committee inertia
**When to use**: the needed move in the policy rate is larger than a committee will vote.
**How**: adopt a target with independent intellectual standing (money in 1979). Let the rate move inside a wide band without a vote. Repeat "stick to it" to wage and price setters. Never call it an experiment.
**Trade-offs**: hidden staff and chairman discretion in the adjustments. The regime lasts only while the indicator holds. (Ch 5)

## Read the chairman's leverage
**When to use**: forecasting whether the FOMC follows its chairman.
**How**: on structural change he is decisive, for or against. On routine moves his extra weight is small and earned through judgement and public stature. Count dissents. Watch for overreach (lost power) and for consensus-seeking (a floundering committee). A Board majority can outvote him on Board-only tools.
**Trade-offs**: stature built in public does not protect him in a private confrontation. (Ch 3, Ch 5, Ch 9)

## Forecast handling
**When to use**: using staff or official projections.
**How**: read for direction and severity. Weight the qualitative reasoning. Expect staff forecasts to sit near outside consensus. Value the forecaster who sees soonest that the forecast is wrong.
**Trade-offs**: consensus forecasts plus gradualist policymakers narrow the range of policy considered. (Ch 2, Ch 6)

## Forward-guidance moral-hazard check
**When to use**: a central bank promises a rate path ("considerable period", "measured pace", "extended period").
**How**: look for (1) short-funded long positions growing, (2) long rates not rising as tightening starts, (3) officials puzzled by long rates, (4) a negative real policy rate in recovery.
**Trade-offs**: guidance aligns markets with policy only if the central bank forecasts better than the market. (Ch 6, Ch 7, Ch 8)

## Rescue test
**When to use**: judging whether a failing firm will be, or should be, supported.
**How**: ask whether failure threatens the payments mechanism, the holders of the public's liquid savings, or the stability of the system the firm sits in. Size alone is not the test. A rescue is liquidity while a buyer is found. Those responsible should lose heavily. Rescues should be rare and the central bank's own balance sheet must stay credible.
**Trade-offs**: every rescue weakens creditor due diligence. Refusing one at the wrong moment can turn a bad crisis into a great one. (Ch 6, Ch 7, Ch 8)

## Crisis-stage reading
**When to use**: tracking an official crisis response.
**How**: stage one, traditional lender of last resort with a normal balance sheet. Stage two, the authority becomes part of the market after a confidence break. Stage three, stature returns as facilities run off and growth resumes. Stage four, a long aftermath in which fiscal policy does most of the work.
**Trade-offs**: stage two can be caused by official action itself. (Ch 7)

## Currency-defence assessment
**When to use**: a package of intervention, swap lines or foreign-currency borrowing.
**How**: compare ammunition with market volume. Check for a change in domestic monetary policy. Prefer multilateral action. Note who decides: in the United States, the Treasury.
**Trade-offs**: without a policy change these are holding actions, and foreign-currency borrowing reads as a confession of failure. (Ch 4, Ch 5)

## Two-layer credibility tracking
**When to use**: assessing a central bank's room to act.
**How**: track policy credibility (results against stated aims) and institutional credibility (audit reach, governance limits, regulatory powers under debate) as separate series.
**Trade-offs**: the second erodes through non-monetary failures and takes longer to rebuild. (Ch 8)

## Prudential action as an early signal
**When to use**: an asset or credit bubble with no goods inflation.
**How**: use margin, capital or liquidity requirements early, and state the stability concern in regular reports.
**Trade-offs**: credit is fungible, so direct restraint is small. The value is in the signal. Coordination among regulators is slow. (Ch 6, Ch 9)

# Chapter 11: Taming Capital Flows

Author: Barry Eichengreen (sole author). First published in World Development 28 (6), June 2000. Part IV, Policy Implications. This is the book's policy template: general method, with one figure.

## Core Idea
Capital account liberalization is "neither panacea nor plague. What it is, is unavoidable." The question is not whether to liberalize but in what order and with what supporting policies. The order is: domestic financial reform, then foreign direct investment, then stock and bond markets, then offshore bank borrowing last, using market-friendly taxes and an exchange rate regime that removes one-way bets.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The messy middle**: the position between two extremes.
  - Extreme 1 (after Lucas 1990): if output per person in the United States is 15 times India's and both share a Cobb-Douglas technology with capital elasticity 0.4, the marginal product of capital in India is 58 times that in the United States. Capital mobility also disciplines policy.
  - Extreme 2 (Rodrik 1998b; Bhagwati 1998): output gaps reflect different production functions, markets are riddled with information asymmetries, and market discipline is "arbitrary and erratic". Punishment is "disproportionate to the crime" (Calvo and Mendoza 1996).
  - Middle: both hold in part. Liberalize "just to the point where the benefits ... continue to dominate the risks", and choose policies that push the growth-stability frontier outward.
- **Sequencing rules for national policy** (section 11.3, the section headings are the rules).
  1. Open the capital account only after financial markets have been liberalized and decontrolled. Liberalization "should not precede the recapitalization of the banking sector, strengthening of prudential supervision and regulation, and the removal of blanket guarantees".
  2. Liberalize foreign direct investment first. It carries expertise, is least likely to aggravate bank weakness, and is less footloose. This includes entry by foreign banks.
  3. Liberalize stock and bond markets next. Withdrawals show up in prices, not in a contractual obligation to repay at par.
  4. Liberalize offshore bank borrowing last. "The most fundamental lesson of the Asian crisis." Also avoid artificial incentives for bank-to-bank lending.
  5. Rely on market-friendly instruments for managing the capital account. Taxes beat administrative controls, on inflows and on outflows.
  6. Align domestic institutions and policies to the capital account regime. Abandon pegged-but-adjustable rates, crawling bands and target zones for a currency board, dollarization, or a more flexible rate.
- **Chilean capital-import tax**: a 30 percent non-interest-bearing deposit for one year on all capital imports.
  - Why it works: the cost falls more heavily on short-horizon investors. It is transparent, insulated from discretion, and harder to evade than taxes on selected flows.
  - What it does: changes average maturity, not the level of borrowing. It need not be evasion-free to work.
- **Prerequisites for a float, and for a currency board**: sound fiscal policy backed by hierarchical fiscal institutions (agenda-setting and veto power with the finance minister or prime minister); an independent central bank; a clear monetary rule such as inflation targeting; stronger bank regulation. For a currency board, the analog of central bank independence is a statute or constitutional amendment.
- **Two international responses** (section 11.4).
  - Giving sanction: endorse retained controls where prerequisites are missing, avoid pushing rapid liberalization, encourage Chilean-style taxes and flexible rates, and stop lending to prop up shaky pegs. To make the last credible, add renegotiation-friendly (collective action) clauses to loan contracts.
  - Applying peer pressure: codes and standards for monetary and fiscal policy, supervision, securities regulation, auditing and accounting, insolvency and corporate governance, with monitoring through IMF Article IV surveillance, loan conditionality, Contingent Credit Line eligibility, and capital requirements keyed to compliance.

## Key Concepts
- **Adverse selection**: lenders who cannot judge quality pay an average price, good firms withdraw, and bad projects get financed.
- **Moral hazard**: borrowers raise project risk after the loan, since the lender bears most of a loss.
- **Rational herding**: (a) information cascades, where investors infer quality from others' trades; (b) payoff externalities, where selling pays more the more others sell (Obstfeld 1996; Krugman 1996).
- **Globalization and information** (Calvo and Mendoza 2000): diversification cuts the return to learning about any one country, which worsens herding.
- **One-way bet**: a peg or band lets speculators fund a short position cheaply with little risk of appreciation.
- **Fiscal dominance / unpleasant fiscal and monetary arithmetic**: unsound fiscal policy destabilizes a floating currency, "as in Brazil in 1998".
- **Collective action clauses**: loan contract provisions that ease orderly restructuring. Critics warn they may make borrowing harder for weaker sovereigns.
- **Basel Capital Accord (1988)**: a minimum weighted capital requirement of 8 percent for international banks. Its limits: securitization to move high-weight assets off balance sheet, and compliance with the letter but not the spirit.
- **Wall Street complex**: the author's term for what keeps the US Treasury from backing Chilean-style inflow taxes more strongly.

## Mental Models
- Think of liberalization as learning to swim: "not by jumping into the deep end of the pool".
- Use instrument type to rank inflows by risk. A bank deposit must be repaid at par, so withdrawal threatens the banks. A security sold simply falls in price.
- Think of the FDI advantage as early-stage only. A direct investor "cannot easily unbolt machines from the factory floor", but can hedge through securities markets once those are open. The more open the account, the less the FDI share protects.
- Think of speculators as an infectious disease: "they are likely to pick off the weak, not the strong. But ... even robust health is no guarantee of survival."
- Use the cost of insurance to judge short-term borrowing. If the proceeds must sit in low-yield reserves, "wouldn't it be better to avoid it in the first place?"

## Anti-patterns
- **Opening the capital account with capped domestic interest rates**: the 1980s lesson. It is "a recipe for capital flight (as in Argentina in the early 1980s)".
- **Opening with undercapitalized, guaranteed banks**: the 1990s lesson. Management levers up with offshore funding and foreign lenders supply it.
- **Counting on liberalization to force reform in time**: Asia "casts doubt" on this. "Crisis itself can breed reform, of course, but at a price."
- **Micro-managing the capital account**: it breeds bureaucracy, rent seeking and evasion by relabeling.
- **Tax and licensing favors for offshore bank borrowing**: Thailand's Bangkok International Banking Facility.
- **Relying on large reserves**: reserves are small next to M2, encourage the carry trade (Dooley 1997), and are costly to hold. This is the author's caveat on the Feldstein (1999) and Greenspan (1999) advice.
- **Standards without monitoring or sanctions**: they can "degenerate into a lowest common denominator".
- **Sole reliance on rating agencies for bank capital rules**: given their "spotty record", regulators should diversify sources.
- **Expecting much from outflow controls in a crisis**: incentives to avoid them are then strong.

## Reference Tables

### The liberalization sequence, with the reasons and caveats the chapter gives

| Step | Reason | Caveat or counter-case |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Domestic financial reform (recapitalize banks, strengthen supervision, remove blanket guarantees, free interest rates) | Otherwise opening funds capital flight or leveraged bets by guaranteed banks | Keeping barriers reduces pressure to restructure |
| 1. FDI, including foreign bank entry | Expertise, stability, better-capitalized and home-regulated banks | 144 of 184 countries surveyed by the IMF still controlled FDI in 1996. Dooley (1996) doubts FDI is more stable. Foreign bank entry squeezes weak domestic banks at first and weakens controls on portfolio flows |
| 2. Stock and bond markets | Losses fall on prices, not on banks at par. The term structure of portfolio inflows is "the single most reliable predictor" of currency crises (Rodrik and Velasco 1999) | Securities markets develop late and need disclosure rules and a government bond benchmark. A crash still hurts holders' balance sheets |
| 3. Offshore bank borrowing | Last because it runs through the weakest link | South Korea did this first. Thailand subsidized it through the BIBF |
| Throughout: market-friendly taxes | Fall on short horizons, transparent | Avoidance is still debated |
| Throughout: hard peg or flexible rate | Removes one-way bets | "No panacea": Australia floated and still had "a sour experience with hedge funds" |

### Advice on building reserves, and the objections

| Advice | Source | Author's objection |
|---|---|---|
| Accumulate reserves as insurance | Feldstein 1999 | Even large reserves are small relative to market liquidity. A panic can move "the whole of M2" |
| Hold reserves equal to all short-term debt due within 12 months | Greenspan 1999 | Costly, because US Treasury yields are below the cost of the borrowing (Grenville 1999). Large reserves reassure carry traders and raise bank-to-bank lending (Dooley 1997) |

## Worked Example
Applying the sequence to the two Asian cases the chapter uses.
1. South Korea. The government resisted inward FDI and, under foreign pressure, opened other parts of the account. Banks got access to foreign funding before other components were liberalized. Result on the chapter's reading: the crisis "cannot be understood without reference to" that decision. Rule broken: step 3 came before steps 1 and 2.
2. Thailand. It lifted most restrictions on inward FDI in import-competing industries in the 1970s and in export industries in the 1980s, and "opened other components of the capital account before giving banks access to offshore funds". The order looks right. Two things still went wrong: portfolio flows were opened without strengthening the financial system and supervision (step 0 skipped), and the BIBF then gave banks borrowing offshore favorable tax and licensing treatment (an artificial incentive under step 3).
3. Chile as the positive case. A 30 percent one-year non-interest-bearing deposit lengthened the maturity of external debt without aiming to cut its level. The finance minister's reply to the evasion charge, as paraphrased by the author: if the taxes are so easily evaded, why does the central bank hold so many non-interest-bearing foreign deposits?
Lesson for a research engine: check step 0 and the incentives around step 3 separately. Correct ordering of steps 1 to 3 is not enough.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat liberalization as inevitable, because domestic financial liberalization, information technology, securitization and derivatives make selective controls leak. The choice is about order and safeguards.
2. The order is FDI, then securities markets, then offshore bank funding, all after domestic financial strengthening.
3. Prefer price-based measures (holding-period taxes, exit taxes) to administrative controls. Malaysia moved from comprehensive outflow controls to an exit tax.
4. With an open account, choose a hard peg or a flexible rate, and build the fiscal and central bank institutions either one needs.
5. Do not treat reserves as a substitute for avoiding short-term foreign borrowing.
6. International help comes in two forms: sanctioning cautious liberalization and applying peer pressure through standards that someone actually monitors.
7. Open question the author leaves: whether small economies need Chilean-style taxes, or dollarization, permanently and not only in transition.

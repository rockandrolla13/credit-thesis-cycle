# Chapter 2: A Century of Capital Flows

Author: Barry Eichengreen (sole author). Written as a background paper for the World Bank's Global Development Finance 2000. Part I, Background. This chapter is historical: four lending episodes compared on one template.

## Core Idea
Four lending booms (1880 to 1913, the 1920s, 1973 to 1982, the 1990s) occurred in similar settings, and each can be read through the same four contexts: economic, commercial, political and financial. Lending is "stimulated and stabilized by how it fit[s] into the larger economic system". Relative to the pre-1914 era, crises became more frequent but not more severe.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four-context template for a lending boom** (the chapter's own section structure, applied to each episode).
  - When to use: to judge whether a current surge of cross-border lending rests on supportive conditions or only on liquidity.
  - How: answer four questions, then the fifth.
    1. Economic context: is the boom on the upswing of the global business cycle, and is the growth sustainable or a rebound?
    2. Commercial context: is world trade expanding faster than output, so borrowers can earn the foreign exchange to service debt?
    3. Political context: do governments keep their distance at the lending stage (which lets them commit not to bail out), or do they steer and implicitly endorse loans?
    4. Financial context: which instrument and intermediary carries the flow (bonds, syndicated bank loans, equity, FDI), and which innovation enabled it?
    5. Crisis problem: what shock in the lending countries stops the flow, and how do defaults get resolved?
  - Why it works: without export markets borrowers cannot earn foreign exchange, and without expanding commercial opportunities their incentive to stay on good terms with creditors, "who are also their customers", is weak.
- **Countercyclical versus procyclical capital flows**.
  - Pre-1914: when Britain slowed, interest rates fell under passive gold-standard rules and capital was redeployed abroad. Falling export receipts at the periphery were offset by rising capital inflows. Flows were stabilizing because they were countercyclical.
  - 1920s: monetary policy turned activist. US rate cuts in 1924 and 1927 raised both US growth and capital exports. Federal Reserve rate rises in 1928 to 1929 cut both. "Conditions in commodity markets and capital markets now moved together."
  - Failure mode: when the lender's commodity imports and capital exports fall together, the borrower loses export revenue and financing at once. Note 31 says capital importers such as Germany, Brazil, Argentina, Canada and Poland entered the Depression first for this reason.
- **Importer of last resort** (Kindleberger 1978): the creditor's open market for distress goods stabilizes debt service. Fishlow (1986) generalizes this: pre-1913 lending was part of an export-oriented development pattern whose exports found a market in the creditor countries.
- **Hands-off lending and market discipline** (after Feis 1930): because the British government distanced itself at the lending stage, it could commit not to intervene later, and capital flowed along development-oriented lines. French and German governments intervened at the lending stage, loans served strategic aims and public consumption, and debt-servicing difficulties were frequent.
- **Regime change restores creditworthiness**: after default, market access required (1) a settlement acceptable to the bondholders' committee and (2) restored gold convertibility, preferably at the old rate. Bordo and Rockoff (1996) find gold-standard countries borrowed at better rates.
- **Three-phase 1980s debt strategy**: (1) 1982 to 1984, IMF assistance with conditionality, multi-year rescheduling, and concerted ("involuntary") lending, on a diagnosis of illiquidity not insolvency; (2) the Baker Plan; (3) the 1989 Brady Plan, which used official money to collateralize conversion bonds.

## Key Concepts
- **Kuznets Cycle upswing**: migration attracts capital for infrastructure and housing, the capital raises growth, growth attracts more migrants, until an exogenous shock such as a rise in European interest rates stops it.
- **Clustering of infrastructure investment**: networks must be complete before returns accrue, so investment bunches in time and early debt service is hard without further finance.
- **Guarantee and monitoring problem**: with a government guarantee, bondholders had little incentive to monitor use of funds.
- **Corporation of Foreign Bondholders**: British standing committee that could bar a defaulter from the Stock Exchange. Described as probably the most effective such body.
- **State Department Rule of March 3, 1922**: the department gave an opinion on each projected foreign loan, and the public read "not detrimental" as a commercial endorsement.
- **Hot money**: interwar flight capital. Once the franchise widened and policy had other goals, capital stopped flowing in stabilizing directions after shocks.
- **Commodity lottery**: Díaz-Alejandro's term (note 44) for countries' fates depending on their export commodity.
- **Disaster myopia and overlending**: Guttentag and Herring's terms for 1970s bank behavior. The author's counter: risky lending may have been rational if banks expected official intervention.
- **Concerted lending**: lending just enough to keep the debtor out of arrears, needed because no single bank gained from liquidity that benefited creditors as a group.
- **Brady bond**: collateralized security that cleared nonperforming loans and reopened lending through the bond market.

## Mental Models
- Think of a lending boom as safe to the degree that it fits the wider system: exports the loans create must find a market in the creditor.
- Use "liquid liabilities, illiquid assets" as the common thread between Central and Eastern European banks in 1931 and Asia in 1997. Those banks offered high rates for foreign deposits and invested in industry and real estate.
- Think of deeper financial development as raising the output cost of a banking crisis: where firms rely little on external finance, a crisis does less damage (pre-1914, and the transition economies in the early 1990s per note 42).
- Use bank exposure relative to capital to predict the creditor governments' response to a debt crisis.

## Anti-patterns
- **Lending to governments for consumption**: 1920s loans funded pensions and municipal swimming pools, and streets paved "out in the desert". They added no export capacity.
- **Reading official review as endorsement**: the State Department rarely objected, because loans were a weapon in the US and UK contest for financial supremacy.
- **Short-term inflows into a post-stabilization high-rate economy**: Germany after 1924, where by some estimates half the lending was short term, which the author likens to the carry trade.
- **Blaming intermediaries alone for the 1930s defaults**: the author argues that "whatever the financial structure, international financial transactions will come to grief in an unstable macroeconomic environment".
- **Soft currency pegs under high capital mobility**: the "unstable middle ground" that central banks should leave for hard pegs or more flexible rates.

## Worked Example
The 1982 debt crisis walked through the template.
1. Economic and commercial context, 1973 to 1981: commodity price rises, negative real interest rates for borrowers, world trade up 9 percent a year. Exports of non-oil developing countries grew 21 percent a year in dollars from 1974 through 1980, while the interest rates they paid averaged less than 13 percent. Their debt/export ratios were lower in 1979 than in 1970 to 1972.
2. Shock from the lending countries: disinflation raised real rates. Three-month LIBOR rose from 9 to 17 percent between 1978 and 1981. The industrial countries entered recession from 1979. Export growth of non-oil developing countries halved between 1976-1979 and 1980-1981.
3. Result: debt/export ratios rose 23 percent between 1980 and 1983, and 38 percent for Latin America's non-oil exporters. Net private inflows were falling before Mexico threatened default in August 1982.
4. Differential response: South Korea cut labor costs and raised exports from 1979. Latin American governments ran deficits to hold demand, which fed inflation and overvaluation.
5. Resolution: involuntary lending to Latin America fell from $16 billion in 1984 to $2 billion in 1985. The Baker Plan targeted $20 billion of bank money for 15 debtors over three years, and banks delivered about two-thirds. By 1987 banks had cut exposure relative to capital to half the 1982 level, so jawboning stopped working. Under the Brady Plan 18 countries with about 80 percent of eligible debt settled within five years, about a third of the debt was written off, and official collateral enhancements came to about 40 percent of the debt forgiven.

## Key Takeaways
1. Check all four contexts. A boom with expanding trade, complementary labor flows and arm's-length governments differs in kind from one resting on liquidity alone.
2. Watch the lender's monetary policy. In every episode a rise in lending-country interest rates curtailed the flow: European discount rates in 1906 to 1907, the Federal Reserve in 1928 to 1929, disinflation after 1979.
3. Ask whether capital flows offset or amplify the borrower's trade cycle.
4. Crisis frequency doubled relative to pre-1914, driven by currency crises, which the author ties to soft pegs. Output losses and recovery times look similar.
5. Clustering of crises is greater since 1973 than before 1913 but was somewhat less in the 1990s than in the preceding decades.
6. What was new in the 1990s was breadth: every sector, equity as well as bonds, and recipients without advanced-country institutions.
7. National stabilizers (automatic fiscal stabilizers, deposit insurance, central bank backstops) have no international counterpart yet.

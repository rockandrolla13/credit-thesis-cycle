# Chapter 7: The Baring Crisis in a Mexican Mirror

Author: Barry Eichengreen (sole author). First published in The International Political Science Review 20 (3), July 1999. Part III, Crises. This is a historical case study: Argentina 1880 to 1893 set beside Mexico 1988 to 1995.

## Core Idea
The Mexican crisis of 1994 to 1995, which Camdessus called "the first financial crisis of the 21st century", is better read as "the last financial crisis of the nineteenth". The same sequence ran in Argentina before the Baring crisis of 1890: low interest rates at the center, reform at the periphery, inflows through state banks into consumption, political weakness that blocked adjustment, foreign-currency-linked debt, then a stop. What differed was the official response.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Bond-market boom-bust template** (the chapter's section structure, applied to both cases).
  - When to use: to test whether an emerging-market lending boom has the Baring pattern.
  - How: check each stage.
    1. Context: structural reform that improves growth prospects and locks in a regime (Mexico: Brady deal, trade liberalization, privatization, GATT, NAFTA. Argentina: Pampas secured, national currency, bimetallic standard in 1881, falling ocean freight rates and refrigeration).
    2. Global financial conditions: low rates at the center push funds outward (push factors).
    3. Private investment under public guarantee: long-gestation projects whose debt service starts at once.
    4. Public finances: published budgets hide deficits in state banks.
    5. State banks: foreign capital flows through the banking system, which magnifies its effect on demand.
    6. Consumption and investment: saving fails to rise, and inflows finance consumption.
    7. Exchange rate and monetary policy: policy too accommodating for the commitment.
    8. Outbreak: center rates rise, a political shock hits, a bond issue fails, and reserves are spent to defer adjustment.
    9. Aftermath: current account reversal through exports (mild) or import compression (severe).
  - Why it works: demand rises at once while supply and exports respond with a lag, so debt capacity is questioned before the investments pay.
- **Tying oneself to the mast**: a government under pressure converts domestic-currency debt into foreign-currency-linked debt to reassure investors.
  - Argentina, May 1889: plan to buy paper cedulas and issue gold cedulas.
  - Mexico, 1994: tesobonos, dollar-linked securities, rose from $1 billion at the start of 1994 to $18 billion by December.
  - Failure mode: "A disturbance to the flow of foreign exchange could now lead not just to devaluation but to default."
- **Guarantee-induced moral hazard**: with a government interest guarantee, investors watch only the government's credit, nobody monitors the project, and promoters raise capital for lines of doubtful viability.
- **Imputed national saving** (the author's proxy for a period without national accounts): saving equals investment plus the current account. Approximate investment by imports of investment goods, and the current account by the trade balance and debt service.
- **Rescue coordination as a function of market concentration**: the Bank of England could assemble a fund in secret within a week because the market was concentrated and London houses dominant. In 1995 the market was decentralized, the rescue was public, banks would not contribute, and the ratio of public to private money was higher.

## Key Concepts
- **Baring crisis (1890)**: the near-failure of Baring Brothers after it was left holding the Buenos Aires Water Supply and Drainage Company loan, first floated in November 1888.
- **Cedulas**: peso-denominated negotiable bearer bonds issued by national and provincial mortgage banks to landowners against real estate, then sold on to investors.
- **Free Banking Law (1887)**: allowed banks to issue notes backed by government bonds bought with funds borrowed abroad. It was poorly enforced.
- **Gold premium**: the price of gold in paper pesos, the measure of depreciation.
- **Tesobonos**: Mexican dollar-indexed short-term government securities.
- **Development banks (Mexico)**: state banks that borrowed on the market, often abroad, and lent for activities the government would otherwise have done. A hidden deficit.
- **Rothschild Committee**: the London houses' committee, headed by Nathan Rothschild, that restructured Argentina's debt.
- **Push factors**: center-country interest rates as the driver of flows (Calvo, Leiderman and Reinhart 1993 for the 1990s; John H. Williams 1920 for the 1880s).
- **Tequila Effect**: the 1995 spillover to Argentina, Brazil, Thailand and Hong Kong. After 1890, rates rose in Brazil, Uruguay, Venezuela and Turkey.

## Mental Models
- Think of the benchmark borrower as the fuse. Mexico was the largest borrower and set the floor for spreads. Argentina was "The United States of South America".
- Use the order in which investors arrive as a late-cycle signal. In both cases those "slow to join the bandwagon climbed on board in the final stages". Small investors bought cedulas "just as institutional investors drew back".
- Use the crossover of new borrowing and debt service as a stop signal. Argentina's national government borrowed less than it paid in service in 1889, and the nation as a whole did so in 1890.
- Think of the adjustment path as the measure of severity. Export-led adjustment (Mexico) is quick. Import compression (Argentina) depresses the economy for years.

## Anti-patterns
- **Being "too much struck by the novelty of the event"**: Fishlow's (1986) remark on Mexico in 1982, which the author applies to 1994.
- **Trusting collateral you cannot check**: cedula holders believed real estate backing made the bonds safer than government bonds, while face values regularly exceeded the market value of the land despite a legal 50 percent loan-to-value cap.
- **Financing a deficit with reserves before an election**: the Bank of Mexico's choice in 1994. It "only put off the day of reckoning".
- **Reading the published budget only**: in both countries "much of the action in the budget was hidden in the accounts of the state banks".
- **Inflating to avoid taxing your own supporters**: Juárez Celman chose inflation over Uriburu's proposed 15 percent rise in customs duties.

## Worked Example
The Baring rescue, step by step, as the chapter tells it.
1. Barings holds most of the unsold Water Supply and Drainage loan and has borrowed heavily to meet obligations.
2. Weekend of November 8-9, 1890: management tells the Bank of England. The Bank estimates Barings needs £4 million at once.
3. The Bank offers £1 million if the government matches it. Chancellor Goschen refuses direct involvement but agrees to share any loss the Bank incurs in the first 24 hours.
4. To protect the gold reserve ratio, Rothschilds arranges a £3 million gold loan from the Bank of France against Treasury bills, and the Bank of England's William Lidderdale obtains half that sum from Russia.
5. The Bank's £1 million is matched by £3 million from the major houses. Within little more than a week the joint-stock banks raise the fund to £18 million.
6. The news becomes public only the following Friday, after the £4 million is in place.
7. The Rothschild Committee then restructures Argentina's debt. Argentina must first meet its short-term debts, many of them acceptances due to Barings. In return the houses underwrite a bond issue covering three years of remaining debt service.
8. French and German financiers object that the terms are too generous and that British acceptances were paid first. London proceeds anyway.
The author's point: the rescue aimed at the London market and only secondarily at Argentina. In 1995 the order was reversed.

## Key Takeaways
1. Low center rates plus periphery reform is the recurring recipe for a bond-market lending boom.
2. Look for the state banks. They route foreign money into demand and carry the hidden fiscal position.
3. Watch saving. Inflows that finance consumption cannot generate the foreign exchange to service foreign-currency debt.
4. Converting to foreign-currency-linked debt under pressure raises the stakes from devaluation to default.
5. Political opposition and weak governments explain why adjustment comes late. Markets read political shocks as doubts about the capacity to adjust.
6. Better-informed investors grew wary well before both crises. Warnings about Argentina date from 1886 and 1887.
7. Official rescues are harder now. Markets are more decentralized, information moves faster, and no single creditor dominates as London did.

# Chapter 5: Exchange Market Mayhem: The Antecedents and Aftermath of Speculative Attacks

Authors: Barry Eichengreen with Andrew Rose and Charles Wyplosz. First published in Economic Policy 21 (October 1995). Part III, Crises. This is a method chapter: a panel of 20 industrial countries from 1959, quarterly.

## Core Idea
Devaluations, regime transitions and currency crises are overlapping but distinct sets of events and must be analyzed separately. Devaluations are fairly predictable and follow loose money, inflation and weak external accounts. Flotations and other regime changes cannot be told apart from tranquil periods. "There are no unambiguous early warning signals of impending crisis."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Events versus crises taxonomy**.
  - Events: officially declared changes taken from the IMF's Exchange Arrangements and Exchange Restrictions (EAER): devaluations, revaluations, flotations, fixings, and other regime changes (band widening, unification, crawling pegs).
  - Crises: quarters of extreme speculative pressure, whether or not policy changed.
  - Failed attack (successful defense): a crisis that does not coincide with an event. Successful attack: a crisis followed within a year by an event.
  - When to use: before any study of "currency crises". Studying exchange rate changes alone includes orderly realignments and excludes repelled attacks, which the authors call selectivity bias.
- **Index of exchange market pressure** (after Girton and Roper 1977).
  - How: (1) compute, relative to the reference country Germany, the percentage change in the exchange rate, the change in the interest differential, and the percentage change in reserves; (2) weight the three so that their conditional volatilities are equal, because reserve changes are several times more volatile than exchange rate changes, which are several times more volatile than interest differential changes; (3) call a quarter a crisis when the index is at least two standard deviations above its mean; (4) drop later crises within plus or minus two quarters of the first.
  - Why it works: an attack can show up as reserve loss, as an interest rate defense or as depreciation. The index catches all three.
  - Failure modes named by the authors: reported reserves miss credit lines, swaps and forward intervention; third-country intervention is invisible; quarterly data miss attacks repelled within days; capital controls change what interest and reserve moves mean.
- **Event-study plots against a tranquility control group**: for each of 16 variables, plot the mean deviation from tranquil-period values for eight quarters before and after the episode, with a band of two standard deviations. Tranquility excludes all events and crises and a one-quarter window around each.
- **Multinomial logit over seven states**: tranquility (omitted), failed attack, devaluation, revaluation, flotation, fixing, other event. Regressors are fourth-order moving averages of leads and lags. The model is multivariate, multinomial, dynamic and non-structural. 1,300 observations and McFadden R2 of 0.18.
- **Generations of crisis theory, with their observable predictions** (section 5.2). See the reference table.
- **Three options for the exchange rate system** (section 5.6): (1) monetary union on the Maastricht model; (2) a Tobin tax on foreign exchange transactions, which would have to be global; (3) living with the dirty float. "All of these options have drawbacks."

## Key Concepts
- **Shadow exchange rate**: the rate that would prevail if the peg were abandoned. In Krugman (1979) the attack comes when it equals the current rate.
- **Self-fulfilling attack**: policy is consistent with the peg unless attacked, and shifts to accommodation only if attacked (Flood and Garber 1984b; Obstfeld 1986).
- **Focal point**: a prominent event, such as a successful attack elsewhere or a Maastricht referendum, that coordinates many small traders.
- **Interest rate defense**: raising rates to repel an attack. It can backfire if traders believe the marginal cost to the government rises with the rate.
- **Sterilized intervention**: reserve sales offset so that money and credit keep growing. The mark of the run-up to a crisis as against a devaluation.
- **Stable zone in a band** (Chen 1995): a middle region where neither the speculator nor the central bank acts. Small shocks that push the rate toward the edge can trigger a large attack.
- **Reference country**: Germany throughout, chosen for a consistently strong currency. Results were largely unchanged with the United States before 1971 (note 25).
- **Sacrificial lamb**: realignments follow a new finance minister, consistent with Cooper (1971).

## Mental Models
- Think of a devaluation as a correction made in time and a crisis as a correction not made. Before devaluations, money growth slows as sterilization becomes incomplete, interest rates rise and stock prices fall. Before crises, credit keeps growing, interest rates do not rise and stock prices do not fall.
- Use the political and business cycle to explain why the correction is not made. Crises occur when unemployment is high and politics are unpropitious.
- Think of self-fulfilling attacks as a market bet that the government will not take tough action. The conditions are "recession, high unemployment, past or impending elections, and finance ministers on thin ice".
- Use the position of the rate inside its band as an indicator of realignment expectations. Several studies the chapter cites find it a strong one.

## Anti-patterns
- **Relying on sterilized intervention to defend a peg**: governments serious about defense need painful unsterilized steps.
- **Treating virtue as immunity**: "many flotations are not preceded by lax monetary or fiscal policy".
- **Reading leads as effects**: faster growth after a successful defense may mean the defense succeeded because recovery was expected.
- **Building early warning systems on regime transitions**: nobody knows beforehand whether an attacked peg will be devalued, floated or defended.
- **Target zones and narrower bands**: pursuing the "right" policies does not insulate a band. Pegged-but-adjustable systems "disintegrate quickly under stress".
- **Assuming fiscal deficits drive attacks**: no link is found in this sample of advanced economies. The authors expect a different answer in a sample with high-inflation developing countries.

## Worked Example
The authors' three-way reading of a government under pressure (section 5.6).
1. Accommodating monetary policy, usually in response to unemployment, produces inflation and reserve losses.
2. Branch A: the government sharply cuts money growth through unsterilized intervention. It "may succeed in defending the rate".
3. Branch B: it retrenches less. It "may still have to devalue", but without an atmosphere of crisis.
4. Branch C: it relies purely on sterilized intervention. It "may find [itself] unable to avoid a full-blown currency crisis".
5. Separate case: a government with sound policy is attacked anyway, because markets test its resolve or bet it will not act. Here self-help is not enough and the system needs insurance: automatic and commensurate support from strong-currency partners. Moral hazard deters those partners, and limits the IMF in the same way.
The authors add one tactic for fiscally sound countries: use fiscal expansion, not monetary relaxation, to keep unemployment from making the defense too costly.

## Key Takeaways
1. Count crises with a pressure index, not with exchange rate changes.
2. Realignments are "fundamentally alike". Regime transitions are "unpredictable and idiosyncratic".
3. The variables that matter jointly are capital controls (lags and leads), past government defeats, past and future inflation, future GDP and employment growth, and past current account balances.
4. Unemployment rates, budgets and domestic credit growth are "essentially unrelated to exchange rate episodes" in the logit.
5. Controls cut both ways. They make devaluations less likely and failed attacks more likely, they tend to appear after devaluations and disappear after failed attacks, and they allow laxer policy.
6. No policy switch toward ease is found after attacks, which counts against the simple self-fulfilling story (note 40).
7. Clusters of crises appear after 1980, after 1985 and after 1991, consistent with contagion.

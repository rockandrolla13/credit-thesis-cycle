# Chapter 10: Capital Controls: Capital Idea or Capital Folly?

Author: Barry Eichengreen (sole author). A different, edited version appeared in The Milken Review 1 (Spring 1999). Part IV, Policy Implications. This is a short policy essay: general method, no data tables. It is thin by design, so it lands below the study budget.

## Core Idea
There are two separate cases for capital controls in developing countries, and they must not be confused. The prudential case (inflow controls as a third line of defense behind bank owners and regulators) is strong for most emerging markets. The emergency case (outflow controls to regain macro policy freedom in a crisis) holds only if investors are irrational, and the better fix is institutional reform. In both cases controls are transitional.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three questions that sort the debate**: (1) Is the problem that the transactions are financial, or that they are international? (2) Are controls emergency measures or permanent protection? (3) Do they apply to all financially exposed economies or only to emerging markets?
- **Three lines of defense against excessive bank risk-taking** (section 10.3).
  1. Owners and managers: capital requirements and subordinated debt, so that owners and important creditors have something to lose.
  2. Regulators: monitoring and prompt corrective action on fraud, incompetence or gambling for redemption.
  3. Limits on banks' borrowing abroad, where the first two are weak. If corporations can borrow and on-lend to banks, this means "controlling or taxing, à la Chile, all capital inflows", whoever the borrower of record is.
  - When to use: to decide whether a country's capital account should be open. The third line is needed where the first two cannot be relied on.
- **Three preconditions that justify prudential controls**: financial markets are thin; private risk-management practice is underdeveloped; regulators' capacity is limited. The author judges that these apply to "the vast majority of developing countries", so "an open capital account should be the exception, not the rule".
  - Constraint: controls are justified only if they do not arbitrarily favor some banks and residents over others.
- **Why an open capital account disables the safety net**.
  - If banks borrow in foreign currency, the central bank cannot be lender of last resort: it "can't print foreign currency", and its capacity is limited to its reserves.
  - Even with domestic-currency liabilities, a central bank that pegs must choose between draining liquidity to defend the rate and injecting liquidity to defend the banks. "It will not be possible to do both."
- **Two models of market discipline** (section 10.4): the test for emergency controls.
  - Model A, irrational investors: they panic when a government uses its stabilizers. Controls are then a sensible shield.
  - Model B, rational investors: they expect a government with a history of laxity to monetize deficits, so deficits today imply inflation tomorrow. Then "the solution is not to use controls to relax market discipline but for the government to clean up its act".
  - How to choose: look at the country's fiscal and monetary record and institutions.
- **Institutional reforms that restore counter-cyclical policy** (the first-best).
  1. An independent central bank.
  2. An independent national fiscal council with power to set a ceiling on each year's deficit, and automatic procedures if the ceiling is threatened.
  3. Less ambitiously, more agenda-setting power for the prime minister or finance minister, to curb the common-pool problem among spending ministries.
  4. Transparent budgeting.

## Key Concepts
- **Liquidity transformation**: bank assets are less liquid than deposits, so a loss of confidence can produce a system-wide panic.
- **Information cascade**: imperfectly informed investors infer value from each other's actions and move as a herd.
- **Financial safety net**: deposit insurance plus a lender of last resort. It invites risk-taking that regulation must offset.
- **Perverse market discipline**: crisis countries are forced to cut deficits in recessions, so fiscal stabilizers are "thrown out the window". The author says the IMF "is merely mirroring market sentiment".
- **Fail-safe econometrics**: Jeffrey Frankel's phrase for defining a hypothesis so that failing to find significance counts as support. The author applies it to Rodrik's no-correlation result.
- **Double standard**: asking developing countries to stay closed for good when every advanced country has opened.
- **Peg as lock on the refrigerator**: a country that pegs to end inflation signals that the inflation tax is gone. Devaluing is read as taking the lock off.
- **Common-pool problem**: no spending ministry counts the full effect of its spending on the overall deficit.

## Mental Models
- Think of the financial system as a trapeze artist: capable of "miraculous tricks" and of "a bone-shattering fall if allowed to perform without a net". Regulators limit the difficulty of the tricks.
- Use the fat man and the cake to read market reaction to a deficit. A government with a record of laxity that loosens on "a lousy macroeconomic day" is re-rated as having relapsed.
- Use the monetization test to predict the currency's response to fiscal expansion. In the United States nobody expects monetization, so spending raises the real interest rate and the real exchange rate. In Brazil monetization is a real possibility, so it implies inflation and devaluation.
- Think of controls as scaffolding. They come down once markets deepen, risk management matures and regulators gain competence and independence.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a zero cross-country correlation as proof that liberalization has no benefit**: omitted variables that lower growth and raise the odds of opening can hide a real effect.
- **Permanent controls as development policy**: the valid argument is for transitional measures only.
- **Controls that discriminate among banks or residents**: these are not prudential.
- **Using controls as "an excuse to slow down" institutional development and policy reform**: the author calls this "the most critical point of all".
- **Devaluing out of a recession when the peg is the anti-inflation anchor**: it can have "catastrophic effects in emerging markets".
- **Opening early and counting on reform to follow**: "After Mexico in 1994 and Asia in 1997, do we really need a third reminder of the dangers of premature and precipitous financial liberalization?"

## Worked Example
The Malaysian controls of 1998, judged with the chapter's two tests.
1. Facts as given: Malaysia controlled all purchases and sales of the ringgit for international financial transactions. Citizens could not take as little as $100 out of the country, with random airport searches. Krugman (1998) had argued that emergency conditions warranted emergency measures and that controls could shelter reflation.
2. Stated rationale: leeway for a more expansionary fiscal policy to offset the recession, which markets would otherwise punish.
3. Costs listed by the author: a burdensome administrative bureaucracy, less pressure for policy reform, and interrupted access to foreign investment finance.
4. Test: if investors panic irrationally at any stimulus (Model A), the benefits may dominate. If investors rationally fear monetization (Model B), controls only relax a discipline the government needs.
5. The author's ranking: first-best is to reform the institutions that make future policy credible. Controls are a second-best measure for economies that still have the "defining characteristics of an underdeveloped economy": institutions without credibility, regulators without capacity, shallow markets, and no ability to borrow abroad in the domestic currency.
The chapter does not report how Malaysia's economy fared under the controls.

## Key Takeaways
1. Separate prudential inflow controls from emergency outflow controls. The arguments, the evidence and the conditions differ.
2. For most emerging markets, keep limits or taxes on foreign borrowing until markets, risk management and supervision are ready. "It is smart to err in the direction of caution."
3. Foreign-currency bank liabilities remove the lender of last resort. A peg forces a choice between the currency and the banks.
4. Whether fiscal expansion strengthens or weakens a currency depends on whether investors expect it to be monetized.
5. Build institutions (central bank independence, fiscal councils, hierarchical budgeting, transparency) to regain counter-cyclical freedom. Then "capital mobility will no longer be a threat".
6. The destination is the same as for today's rich countries: an open capital account, reached after the preconditions are in place.

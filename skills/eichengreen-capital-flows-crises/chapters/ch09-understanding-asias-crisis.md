# Chapter 9: Understanding Asia's Crisis

Author: Barry Eichengreen (sole author). First published as appendix C of his Toward a New International Financial Architecture (Institute for International Economics, 1999). Chapter 1, note 6 says the formulation "was heavily influenced by Morris Goldstein's 1998 book". Part III, Crises. This is a historical case study of East Asia, 1997 to 1998.

## Core Idea
Asia's crisis was "a financial crisis with self-fulfilling features" that hit countries whose governments lacked the economic and political means to defend their currencies. Three vulnerabilities put them there: modest macroeconomic imbalances, serious banking weakness, and short-maturity foreign-currency debt. The deeper cause was a development model that used banks as instruments of industrial policy, extended by a capital account opened "exactly backwards".

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three sources of vulnerability** (section 9.4).
  1. Macroeconomic imbalances: capital inflows produced some real appreciation and, in some cases, large current account deficits. If financing stops, closing a deficit fast means compressing demand and a recession. Avoiding that needs reserves or high interest rates.
  2. Financial sector weakness: banks do liquidity transformation, so high rates raise funding costs relative to income, and passing them on causes defaults. The defense that the balance of payments needs is the one the banks cannot survive.
  3. Short maturity of debt: a stock problem on top of the flow problem. Short-term foreign-currency debt must be rolled over, and the authorities may feel compelled to guarantee banks' foreign liabilities, which turns a banking crisis into a currency crisis.
  - When to use: as a three-item screen for a pegged emerging economy. All three together mean a government cannot credibly raise rates.
- **Zone of vulnerability** (the same construct as Ch 8): "Painful policies were required to sustain confidence if it were disturbed, but financial systems could not bear the pain." The crisis was not inevitable, "except in Thailand perhaps".
- **Banks as instruments of industrial policy** (section 9.5): the causal chain behind the regulatory failure.
  1. Banks receive franchises, with other channels of intermediation suppressed.
  2. In return they accept government direction of credit.
  3. The quid pro quo is a guarantee. Banks are too big and too important to fail.
  4. Guarantees give owners and managers a reason to take more risk.
  5. While high-return projects are plentiful, directed and connected lending does little harm. When they run out, it puts bank solvency at risk.
  6. Banks then borrow abroad to keep going, and governments free them to do so.
- **Backwards capital account liberalization**: South Korea kept stringent controls on inward FDI, limited foreign purchases of corporate bonds and equities, and restricted corporate borrowing abroad, while freeing banks to borrow abroad. Thailand promoted the Bangkok International Banking Facility (BIBF), which let Thai banks borrow offshore and on-lend in foreign currency. The author's reading: not incompetence but "a logical outgrowth" of a bank-centered system.
- **Peg links monetary policy to the center**: with pegged rates and mobile capital, the authorities could not restrain domestic credit once the Federal Reserve eased. Policy suited to a United States recovering from a banking crisis and a Japan mired in one was wrong for economies at risk of overheating.
- **Six amplifiers of severity** (section 9.6): unhedged foreign exposure; the scramble for cover; other positive feedback (margin calls, rating downgrades, acceleration clauses); cascading defaults under weak insolvency law; contagion; Japan's slump.
- **Five lessons** (section 9.7): see Key Takeaways.

## Key Concepts
- **Carry trade**: borrowing where rates are low (the United States, Japan) to hold higher-yielding deposits or securities in middle-income Asia, safe only while the peg holds. The author notes its appearance in Malaysia in 1991-1992.
- **Contractionary devaluation**: with a stock of foreign-currency debt, depreciation raises debt service, cuts investment and lending, and depresses output, which pushes the exchange rate lower in "a vicious spiral".
- **Scramble for cover**: unhedged borrowers buy foreign exchange for current and feared future obligations once the rate moves, which drives it down further.
- **Sovereign ceiling**: the assumption that corporate and financial issuers cannot be rated above the sovereign. Sovereign downgrades to below investment grade in December 1997 made other issuers' securities junk as well.
- **Acceleration provisions**: bond clauses allowing creditors to demand immediate repayment on a downgrade, "not well known to other investors or, for that matter, to officials".
- **Asset grab race**: creditors without faith in insolvency procedures liquidate claims at once, even where forbearance would serve them collectively.
- **Wake-up call hypothesis** (Goldstein 1998): the Thai devaluation alerted investors to deeper problems elsewhere. The author says the usual version "simply begs the question".
- **Information-impacted banking**: bank-based systems produce few price signals and opaque balance sheets, so bad news discredits all borrowers as a group.
- **Crises within crises**: Camdessus's phrase by spring 1998.
- **Chaebol**: Korean industrial conglomerates. Hanbo (fourteenth largest) failed in March 1997 and Kia (eighth largest) in July.

## Mental Models
- Think of rapid growth as high water. The author borrows Mahathir's image that it "submerges rocks that can otherwise punch holes in the sturdiest boat". Weak insolvency law and connected lending show only when growth slows.
- Use the stock and flow split. A current account deficit is a flow to finance. Short-term debt is a stock to roll over. A country can fail on either.
- Think of a long-held peg as an anaesthetic. Success at pegging "lulled domestic banks and corporations into a false sense of security", so they did not hedge.
- Use the regional anchor's cycle to judge recovery odds. Mexico had a growing United States in 1994-1995 and Europe had strong German demand in 1992-1993. Asia had a shrinking Japan.
- Think of private-sector debtors as harder to restructure than a sovereign. Mexico's tesobonos were government liabilities. Asia's debts sat with many banks and firms and there was no mechanism to coordinate them.

## Anti-patterns
- **Explaining every Asian crisis with one model**: the author calls a horse race between theories "unlikely to be helpful".
- **Leading indicators found afterwards**: slowing exports, falling equities and short-term debt ratios were visible, "But this is wisdom after the fact." Spreads, ratings and bank forecasts gave no warning even for Thailand.
- **Defending a peg in the forward market without fixing fundamentals**: the Thai authorities' choice, which made devaluation look unavoidable.
- **Supporting banks and the currency at once**: the Bank of Thailand lent over $8 billion to distressed institutions through its Financial Institutions Development Fund while intervening for the baht. "Speculators drew the obvious conclusion."
- **Liberalizing finance without upgrading supervision**: the "crucial blunder".
- **Treating large current account deficits as benign because the money is well used**.

## Worked Example
The crisis walked through in the chapter's five phases.
1. Trigger. Thailand has a current account deficit of 8 percent of GDP, a basket peg dominated by a strengthening dollar, falling equities and a burst property bubble. Pressure on the baht starts in July 1996 after the Bangkok Bank of Commerce collapses. Three more sell-offs follow in the nine months before the July 2, 1997 devaluation. Thailand approaches the IMF on July 29. The baht loses nearly 50 percent against the dollar by year end.
2. Spread in Southeast Asia. The Philippines abolishes its band on July 11, and Indonesia widens its band the same day and abandons it a little over four weeks later. The Thai, Indonesian, Malaysian and Philippine currencies fall 24 to 33 percent in the third quarter. The affected countries share little but proximity.
3. North Asia. Taiwan devalues in October. Hong Kong is attacked and its stock market crashes. Korea's short-term debt turns out higher than thought and matures at about $1 billion a day. Between Christmas and New Year foreign banks agree to roll over. On January 28, 1998, $24 billion is rescheduled into long-term bonds. Korean output falls nearly 4 percent in the first quarter.
4. Crisis within the crisis. Indonesia gets a $23 billion IMF package in October 1997 and signs second and third agreements. The rupiah hits Rp17,000 to the dollar on January 22, against Rp2,434 before the crisis. Debt service is suspended and trade credit evaporates. Suharto resigns in May. Japan's first-quarter GDP falls more than 5 percent at an annual rate and the yen passes 140 on June 8.
5. Global phase. Russia devalues and suspends debt payments in mid-August 1998. Investors who hedged Russia with Brady bonds sell Latin American assets to meet margin calls. Long-Term Capital Management collapses. The Federal Reserve cuts rates three times, once between meetings, and the IMF front-loads a package for Brazil.
The author's reading at each step: governments could not raise rates because of the banks, and each shock found more countries still inside the zone of vulnerability.

## Key Takeaways
1. Large current account deficits are not benign, however well the money is used. The author's analogy is earthquake insurance.
2. How the deficit is financed matters. Short-term funding in foreign currency is "risky business", and worst when it is a claim on the banking system.
3. Banks are a special source of vulnerability. Guarantees justify, on second-best grounds, capital requirements tied to the source of bank funding and regulation of short-term foreign inflows to banks.
4. Move toward exchange rate flexibility, and start "before problems arise", so that banks and firms hedge.
5. Crises cannot always be predicted or prevented. Bigger bailouts and standing aside are "equally unacceptable", so a third option is needed.
6. Contagion ran through third-market trade competition, common creditors (Japanese banks under the Basel Standard, margin-called investors) and a general revision of expectations in an opaque bank-based system.

# Chapter 1: Introduction

Author: Barry Eichengreen (sole author). Part I, Background.

## Core Idea
The effect of capital mobility on growth and stability is contingent, not universal: it depends on the institutions and markets that mediate foreign funds, on the sequencing of reforms, and on whether the exchange rate regime is compatible with an open capital account. The chapter is a short road map of the book, so it is thin by design. Use it to route a question to the right chapter.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four capital market regimes of the twentieth century**: the book's periodization.
  - How: (1) before 1914, free capital movement, with net flows at levels "never achieved again, not even in the 1990s"; (2) the interwar transition, with smaller flows, a brief lending boom, heavy government involvement, then collapse into the 1930s crisis, defaults and controls; (3) the third quarter of the century, with tight restrictions; (4) from the 1970s, progressive relaxation and growing flows.
  - When to use: to place any lending boom or crisis in its regime before comparing it with another (developed in Ch 2).
- **Contextual conditions for benign capital flows**: lending surges, and has its most favorable impact, when four conditions hold.
  - How: check (1) international trade is expanding, (2) other factor flows, labor in particular, are unimpeded, (3) macroeconomic policies are sound and stable, (4) political ties between lender and borrower form a "nexus of contracts" that discourages opportunistic behavior.
  - Why it works: finance that flows into export-linked activity generates the foreign exchange needed to service the loans. The pre-1914 system had this property and the interwar system lost it.
- **Contingent growth effect of capital account liberalization**: the growth effect depends on (a) the rule of law, for which Ch 4 finds considerable support, (b) financial depth, for which Ch 4 finds only weak support, and (c) sequencing. Countries that open the capital account before eliminating severe macroeconomic imbalances "are courting disaster".
- **Currency crisis measurement method** (Ch 5 and Ch 6, with Andrew Rose and Charles Wyplosz): separate currency crises from devaluations, measure crises with an index of exchange market pressure built from exchange rate changes, interest rate changes and reserve changes, then compare variables across crisis and noncrisis periods.
- **Contagion definition** (Ch 6): contagion is present if a crisis elsewhere raises the probability of a crisis in a given country after controlling for observable fundamentals. The chapter states the estimate: a crisis elsewhere raises that probability by 8 percentage points.
- **Sequencing rule for the capital account** (Ch 11): limit external borrowing until corporate governance and supervision are upgraded, then liberalize foreign direct investment, then stock and bond markets, and only then let banks borrow offshore. Abandon pegged-but-adjustable rates, crawling bands and target zones for harder pegs or freer floats.

## Key Concepts
- **Twin crisis**: a banking crisis and a currency crisis together, the "bad seed" of the two, and especially costly to output throughout the century.
- **Monsoonal effects**: Paul Masson's (1998) term for common shocks from sudden changes in global conditions, which are hard to tell apart from contagion.
- **Trade-weighted and macro-weighted contagion**: weighting crises elsewhere by trade links (beggar-thy-neighbor effects) or by similarity of macroeconomic structure and policy (guilt by association) to identify the channel.
- **First and second generation models**: successive families of currency crisis model. Note 4 says Eichengreen, Rose and Wyplosz 1996 coined the terminology.
- **Leading indicators**: variables with support in the data (weak growth, high unemployment, overvalued real exchange rate, lax macro policy) whose explanatory power is limited and whose predictive power is "even more limited".
- **Crony capitalism as integral to the Asian model**: banks acted as agents of industrial policy and received implicit guarantees in return (Ch 9).

## Mental Models
- Use the pre-1914 versus post-1971 comparison when someone claims crises are getting worse. The book's finding is that severity (output loss, time to recovery) shows little sign of rising, while frequency, of currency crises in particular, clearly rose. The frequency of banking crises was "essentially unchanged", so the rise in twin crises came from more currency crises.
- Think of a crisis as heterogeneous by default. Devaluations are easier to generalize about than crises, and multiple equilibria can arise because the return to attacking depends on how many others attack.
- Think of the 1990s crises as old problems in new settings. In Europe, removing capital controls was enough to create vulnerability. In Latin America and Asia, liberalization combined with democratization, which made it harder for governments to subordinate every other goal to the exchange rate.

## Anti-patterns
- **Searching for a universal rule linking capital mobility and growth**: the author calls this the economist's habit of generalizing excessively. Cross-country studies "say disappointingly little, positive or negative".
- **Trusting IMF on/off control indices as a measure of openness**: they are a crude summary of statutory measures, and observed flows also reflect domestic policy and global conditions.
- **Over-trusting leading indicators of crises**: the fundamentals have limited explanatory and less predictive power.
- **Running an exchange rate band, target zone or crawling peg with an open capital account**: described as extremely difficult and extremely dangerous.
- **Liberalizing by letting banks borrow offshore first**: the "worst possible way", as in Asia, where foreign investment in domestic bond and equity markets stayed restricted and short-term flows got artificial incentives.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask "contingent on what?" before asserting any effect of capital flows. The book's answers are institutions, sequencing and the exchange rate regime.
2. Crisis frequency rose between the two eras of high capital mobility. Crisis severity did not clearly rise.
3. Contagion is measurable as an excess crisis probability after controls, and it runs strongly through trade links, which the author finds surprising given the growth of finance. Note 5 cites Glick and Rose 1999 as confirming the pattern.
4. The Baring crisis of 1890 and the Mexican crisis of 1994 to 1995 share causes: low interest rates in the financial center, reform enthusiasm in the borrower, public banks that amplify the inflow, and political weakness.
5. Capital controls have a future, but a limited one, because technology and democratization both work against them.
6. No guideline guarantees against crises. The author treats crises as part of a Schumpeterian process of creative destruction and wants the destruction kept cheap.

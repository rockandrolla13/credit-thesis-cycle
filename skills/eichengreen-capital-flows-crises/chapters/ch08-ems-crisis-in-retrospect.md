# Chapter 8: The EMS Crisis in Retrospect

Author: Barry Eichengreen (sole author). Commissioned by the Bank of Mexico. Part III, Crises. This is a historical case study of the European Monetary System crisis of 1992 to 1993, with two general tools: a synthesis of crisis models and an out-of-sample test of an early warning model.

## Core Idea
The EMS crisis combined a current account problem (overvaluation, the German unification shock, a falling dollar) with a capital account problem (convergence plays that reversed once the Danish referendum cut the expected reward for defending pegs). Countries in a "zone of vulnerability" could not bear the interest rates a defense required. The lesson is that only two regimes are viable with open capital accounts: monetary union or a float anchored by inflation targeting.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Current account crisis versus capital account crisis** (the author's gloss on first- and second-generation models).
  - First generation (Krugman 1979): expansionary policy produces current account deficits that cannot be financed forever. When financing binds, the crisis erupts. Speculators merely foresee it.
  - Second generation (Flood and Garber 1984b; Obstfeld 1986): swings in the capital account first finance deficits and then force them to close at once through a large relative price change. The attack precipitates the policy change.
  - How to use: treat them as complements. Ask (1) which fundamentals weaken competitiveness, and (2) which fundamentals limit the government's tolerance for high interest rates.
- **Zone of vulnerability**: the set of countries for which an interest rate defense is intolerable. Four markers named in the chapter:
  1. High unemployment (France, Spain, the United Kingdom).
  2. Weak banking systems (Sweden, Finland).
  3. Large short-term public debt (Italy: debt/GDP above 100 percent, where a 100 basis point rise in the discount rate added 13 trillion lire to the deficit).
  4. Indexed mortgage rates (the United Kingdom).
  - Why it works: the government trades the cost of high rates now against the benefit of the peg later (here, EMU membership). A shock that lowers the expected benefit or raises the required rate flips the choice.
- **Convergence play**: borrow in low-rate markets and invest in high-rate ERM currencies on the assumption that pegs hold and rates converge. The author says the mechanism is "fundamentally the same" as the carry trade. Scale: capital inflows into Italy and Spain tripled between 1986-1988 and 1989-1991, one-year Eurocurrency spreads fell from 800 to 200 basis points, and the IMF reported estimates as high as $300 billion.
- **Three routes for a required relative price change** (the German unification shock): demand shifted toward German goods, so their relative price had to rise. (1) German prices rise, blocked by the Bundesbank. (2) Parities change against the Deutschmark, blocked by disinflation strategies and the Maastricht rule. (3) Other countries run inflation below Germany's, which is slow and painful.
- **Risk premium variant of the self-fulfilling attack** (Flood and Marion 1999): if the risk premium rises with exchange rate volatility and the authorities will not raise rates to match, the currency falls and floats, volatility rises, and the higher premium is validated. No change in monetary or fiscal policy is needed.
- **Out-of-sample early warning test** (Bussiere and Mulder 1999 model, applied backwards to Europe in 1992).
  - Model: exchange market pressure regressed on five indicators, each lagged one year: current account/GDP, export growth, percentage change in reserves, deviation of the real exchange rate from trend (over the preceding 48 months, note 53), and short-term foreign debt relative to reserves.
  - Three variants: five variables; five variables with domestic plus international debt; three current-account variables only (drop short-term debt and reserves, which may be consequences and not causes).
- **Peggers versus targeters**: post-crisis comparison of three strategies: stay in the ERM at lower parities (Spain, Portugal); float then re-enter (Italy, Finland, late 1996); float with inflation targeting (the United Kingdom, Sweden).

## Key Concepts
- **Old EMS and New EMS** (Giavazzi and Spaventa 1990): 11 realignments from 1979 to January 1987 under capital controls, then none until 1992 once controls went.
- **Exchange rate based stabilization**: importing credibility by pegging. Residual inflation cumulates into overvaluation: "One or two points of extra inflation will cumulate to 5-12 points of overvaluation over a five-year period."
- **Exit strategy problem**: a peg adopted to stabilize is the "repository" of anti-inflation credibility, so leaving it is costly (Eichengreen and Masson et al. 1998).
- **One-way bet**: in a narrow band a weak currency can only fall. Widening the bands to 15 percent removed it.
- **Bath formula**: a general realignment against the Deutschmark in exchange for a German interest rate cut. Offered in early September 1992 and not agreed.
- **Black Wednesday**: September 16, 1992, when sterling left the ERM.
- **Guilt by association**: Portugal with Spain and Ireland with the United Kingdom. The early warning model gives "some support" to their complaints.
- **Inflation targeting**: the monetary anchor adopted by the United Kingdom within six weeks and by Sweden within two months of floating.

## Mental Models
- Think of a peg's survival as a cost-benefit decision that markets can price. The reward (EMU) and the cost (unemployment, bank losses, debt service) are both observable.
- Use "which way is the center's interest rate hurting you?" Falling US rates hurt Europe through a weaker dollar and competitiveness (current account). Rising US rates hurt Mexico through reduced inflows (capital account).
- Think of an adjustable peg under free capital mobility as "an oxymoron". A first devaluation makes markets expect a second, so governments refuse the first until forced out entirely.
- Use the absence of official warnings as evidence that fundamentals alone were not decisive. The IMF's October 1992 World Economic Outlook, discussed September 2-4, did not warn of overvaluation or a crisis.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging competitiveness from wholesale or retail price indices**: traded goods arbitrage hides divergence. Use relative unit labor costs.
- **A feeble, late interest rate defense**: the Bank of England's rise from 10 to 12 percent came an hour after markets opened, had "no discernible impact", and the promised second rise was rescinded.
- **Raising rates when debt is short and large**: in Italy markets "were more alarmed by the implied increase in debt service than they were reassured by the signal of commitment".
- **Counting on cooperation in a collective peg**: France would not devalue before its referendum, weak-currency countries feared losing credibility, and the officials at Bath did not control interest rates.
- **Forecasting crises with short-term debt and reserve losses**: both may respond to a crisis already expected, "generating spurious forecasts".
- **Shadowing an anchor currency without a hard commitment**: the pre-1992 Swedish and Finnish practice, "no longer attractive or, it would appear, viable".

## Worked Example
Running an emerging-market early warning model on Europe (section 8.8).
1. Take the Bussiere and Mulder coefficients estimated on Latin America and East Asia for 1994-1997.
2. Apply them to European data for 1992. Finland, the United Kingdom and Sweden, three of the first countries attacked, top the list. Finland's predicted pressure is like that forecast for Brazil and Argentina in 1998. The next two are like Mexico in 1998.
3. Notice the miss: Italy ranks tenth. The cause is low short-term external debt. Note 54 puts short-term international debt at 41 percent of reserves for these European countries in 1992 against 96 percent for the emerging markets in 1997.
4. Fix the debt measure by adding domestic to international debt. Italy moves to first. France moves to second, a false alarm for 1992 (note 55: it did have a crisis in 1993).
5. Drop the two variables that may be effects, short-term debt and reserves. Finland leads, then Spain, Sweden, the United Kingdom and Italy "closely clustered".
6. Read the residual. Portugal is last in all three columns and Ireland is low, yet both were attacked. That supports contagion or guilt by association.
Conclusions drawn: current-account variables explain much of who was hit, the short-term debt load explains severity (Italy), and some attacks are not explained by fundamentals.

## Key Takeaways
1. Removing capital controls turned a flexible adjustable-peg system into a rigid one, and then into a target.
2. Markets attached no significant probability to realignment until weeks before the crisis, on three different measures.
3. Unit labor costs show overvaluation only for Italy, Spain, the United Kingdom and Sweden. The attacks spread well beyond those four.
4. The Danish referendum mattered because it lowered the expected value of staying the course, not because policy then loosened. No monetary or fiscal relaxation followed, before or after the pegs broke.
5. The output cost of the EMS crises sat midway between typical emerging-market and typical OECD crises: growth fell 1.63 points against 3.34 and 0.00.
6. Three reasons Europe looked like an emerging market: full capital mobility for the first time, clustering that blocked exporting to neighbors, and banks already weakened by deregulation and external funding.
7. Europe's evidence "does not obviously favor inflation targeting over a hard currency peg". It says both work and the middle does not.

# Chapter 3: Capital Account Liberalization: What Do the Cross-Country Studies Tell Us?

Author: Barry Eichengreen (sole author). Commissioned for The World Bank Economic Review. Part II, Capital Account Liberalization. This is a literature survey: method, not case history.

## Core Idea
Cross-country studies do not give a clear answer on whether capital account liberalization raises growth or crisis risk. Three problems recur: the policy is badly measured, causality runs both ways, and studies lump all controls together. The chapter is a map of what each measure and each study can and cannot show.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three ways to measure capital account openness** (section 3.2).
  1. Statute-based: the IMF's Exchange Arrangements and Exchange Restrictions annual, usually "restrictions on payments for capital transactions" (line E2), turned into the share of years with restrictions. Limits: a zero/one dummy, before 1996 it covers resident-owned funds only, and the line between current and capital account measures is blurred.
  2. Flow-based: actual inflows and outflows as a share of GDP (Kraay 1998, Swank 1998). Limit: flows also reflect monetary, fiscal and exchange rate policy, global conditions and politics, so this is "unlikely to be an informative indicator" of the regime.
  3. Asset-price-based: (a) one minus the ratio of market capitalization of the IFC Investable Index to the IFC Global Index (Bekaert 1995; Ahearne, Griever and Warnock 2000), which captures equity inflow restrictions only; (b) correlation of expected stock returns, which is "only as good as the model used to generate the expected returns"; (c) onshore-offshore interest differentials and deviations from covered interest parity, available for few countries and widening exactly when crisis fear gives capital a reason to move.
  - When to use: before using any openness series as an input, name which of the three it is and inherit its limit.
- **Quinn (1997) index**: 56 countries for 1950 to 1994, plus eight more from 1954. Seven categories of statutory measure: four current account, two capital account, one for international agreements such as OECD membership. Each is coded from 0 (most intense restriction) to 2 (no restriction) in half-point steps. The combined index runs 0 to 14 and the capital account index 0 to 4.
- **Second-best argument against liberalization**: removing one distortion need not raise welfare when others remain. The chapter lists three cases: capital flows into protected import-competing sectors with immiserizing effects (Brecher and Díaz-Alejandro 1977); a downwardly rigid real wage that already over-allocates resources to capital-intensive activity (Brecher 1983); endemic information asymmetry (Stiglitz 2000).
- **Determinants of who uses controls** (section 3.3). See the reference table below.
- **Channels from liberalization to growth** (section 3.5): investment, real interest rates and financial depth, macroeconomic discipline, capital taxation, and the maturity structure of foreign debt.
- **Inflow controls versus outflow controls as signals**: inflow controls of the Chilean kind can be read as prudential, a sign that the authorities take stability seriously. Outflow controls "may only suggest that the authorities are desperate".

## Key Concepts
- **Share-of-years openness variable**: the proportion of years in a period in which the IMF dummy shows no restriction. The standard regressor in growth studies.
- **Policy contagion**: countries are more likely to liberalize when their peer group has. Simmons and Elkins define peers as competitors for foreign investment (capital account) or in export markets (current account).
- **Seigniorage motive**: controls stop residents escaping the inflation tax and let the authorities raise reserve requirements (Alesina and Tabellini 1989; Drazen 1989).
- **Negative signal of controls** (Bertolini and Drazen 1997a, b, as the book spells the name): a country that keeps or imposes controls signals reluctance to commit to stabilizing policy, which may incite flight if the controls leak.
- **Reverse causality in crisis studies**: countries in turbulence may impose controls (Malaysia) or relax them (Thailand in January 1998, South Korea some months later).
- **Holding period tax**: Chile's tax on capital inflows, found to shift the maturity of inflows and not their level.
- **Capital taxation puzzle**: open capital accounts should press capital tax rates down, yet Quinn, Swank, Garrett, and Garrett and Mitchell find them unchanged or higher.
- **Wagner's Law**: invoked as a possible reason high-income open economies have large public sectors and high tax rates.

## Mental Models
- Think of the dispute as trade theory against second-best theory. The case for free capital mobility is "the same as the case for free trade, but for the subscripts". The counter-case is that other distortions are present.
- Use "controls come in packages" as the default assumption. Controls are imposed and removed with other measures, so their measured effect includes the rest of the package.
- Think of liberalization's growth effect as indirect: open capital account, then financial development, then growth. That is why a direct link is hard to document.
- Use the order of opening to predict which part of the financial system grows: banks if offshore bank borrowing is freed first (Korea), securities markets if foreign investment in domestic securities is freed first (Malaysia).

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating current account restrictions as a proxy for capital controls**: they also affect trade and are imposed by countries with deeper policy imbalances, so their "effects" pick up those imbalances.
- **Using raw cross-country stock return correlations as an integration measure**: returns differ with the characteristics of the issuers.
- **Reading an OLS coefficient on the exchange rate regime as causal**: pegs may induce controls, and capital mobility may force floats.
- **Using a time trend for technology**: it is contaminated by every other omitted factor that changes over time.
- **Lumping all controls together**: intensity, direction and the market affected all change the result.
- **Unconditional advocacy of liberalization**: the author recalls the mid-1990s version and the Asian crisis that followed.

## Worked Example
Reconciling Rodrik with Quinn. The author walks through why two leading studies disagree and does not settle it ("What explains the contrast is not clear"). Candidate explanations, in the order given:
1. Period. Quinn starts in 1960, so the "lost decade" of the 1980s, when almost no capital flowed to emerging markets, weighs less.
2. Type of liberalization. An earlier sample may hold more FDI liberalizations (positive for growth) and fewer short-term portfolio liberalizations (mixed).
3. Specification. Quinn has more controls and uses the change in openness, not the level.
4. Measure. Quinn's index records whether opening was partial or across the board. The IMF dummy does not.
5. Sample. Quinn has fewer low-income countries, where second-best problems are worst.
Edwards (2001) supports explanations 4 and 5. Kraay (1998) and Ch 4 then test the institutional story directly and mostly fail to confirm it. The lesson for a research engine: a sign flip across studies can come from the measure, the period, the specification or the sample, and each must be checked before the finding is used.

## Key Takeaways
1. No openness measure is clean. State which one a claim rests on.
2. The most robust political-economy facts are that richer countries, countries with independent central banks, and democracies have fewer controls.
3. The cross-country evidence says controls are associated with more currency crises, not fewer. Reverse causality and signaling both offer explanations, and the direction is not identified.
4. Distinguish inflow from outflow controls and bank channels from securities channels before judging a control regime.
5. Chilean-style inflow controls change the maturity mix of inflows, not their volume.
6. Policy consensus (liberalize eventually, with care over timing and order) outruns the evidence. The author calls the lack of empirical support "worrisome".
7. Firm-level studies give clearer results than macro panels: international bond issues lower the required return on equity (Tandon 1994), foreign listing raises trading volume (Smith and Sofianos 1997), and emerging-market firms listing in the United States relax capital constraints (Lins, Strickland and Zenner 2000).

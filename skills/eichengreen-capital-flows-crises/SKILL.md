## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. The effect of capital flows is contingent (Ch 1, Ch 3, Ch 4)
- Do not look for a universal link between capital mobility and growth. Ask what the effect is contingent on: institutions, sequencing, and the exchange rate regime.
- Cross-country studies "say disappointingly little". Rodrik (1998b) finds no growth effect with the IMF on/off dummy. Quinn (1997) and Edwards (2001) find one with the graded Quinn index.
- Ch 4's own tests: the positive effect is robust only for 1988-1992. Financial depth does not condition it. Rule of law conditions it weakly. A small black market premium conditions it most consistently. With macro imbalances present, opening "is as likely to hurt as to help".
- Use the Quinn index (0 to 4) over the IMF dummy. Always name the openness measure a claim rests on: statute-based, flow-based, or asset-price-based.

### 2. Four-context template for lending booms (Ch 2)
Use it to compare any surge in cross-border lending with 1880-1913, the 1920s, 1973-1982 and the 1990s.
1. Economic: is it a global upswing, and is growth sustainable?
2. Commercial: is trade growing faster than output, with the creditor's market open? (Kindleberger's importer of last resort.)
3. Political: do lender governments stay at arm's length, or steer and implicitly endorse loans?
4. Financial: which instrument and intermediary carries the flow?
5. Crisis problem: which lender-country shock stops it?
- Prefer countercyclical flows. Before 1914 British lending rose when Britain slowed. In the 1920s US lending and US imports fell together.
- Crises are about twice as frequent as before 1914, driven by currency crises under soft pegs. Output losses and recovery times are similar.

### 3. Events versus crises, and the exchange market pressure index (Ch 5, Ch 6)
- Separate events (declared devaluations, flotations, fixings) from crises (extreme pressure, whether or not policy changed). Include failed attacks. Exclude orderly realignments.
- `EMP = a x %Δ exchange rate + b x Δ(interest differential) - g x relative %Δ reserves`, against a reference country, with weights that equalize the volatility of the three parts.
- Flag a crisis when EMP exceeds its mean by 1.5 standard deviations (Ch 6) or 2 (Ch 5). Use an exclusion window so one episode is not counted twice.
- Devaluations are fairly predictable: loose money, inflation, weak external accounts, high unemployment, then tightening. Crises look similar but with no last-minute tightening and fully sterilized intervention. Flotations and other regime changes cannot be told apart from tranquil periods.
- "There are no unambiguous early warning signals of impending crisis." Do not use fundamentals to time an attack.

### 4. Contagion as a conditional probability (Ch 6)
- Define contagion as the effect of a crisis elsewhere on crisis probability at home after controlling for fundamentals. The estimate is about eight percentage points, and far higher inside the EMS.
- Identify the channel by weighting crises elsewhere. Trade weights beat macro-similarity weights when both are entered. An unweighted effect remains, so other channels exist.
- Keep the caveat: a common unobserved shock can mimic contagion.

### 5. Zone of vulnerability (Ch 8, Ch 9)
- A peg survives only while the government can bear the interest rates a defense needs. Check four things: unemployment, bank health, short-term or foreign-currency debt, and rate-sensitive debt such as indexed mortgages or a large short-dated public debt.
- A crisis combines a current account story (overvaluation, deficits) with a capital account story (inflows that reverse and force a contingent policy shift). Treat first- and second-generation models as complements.
- A trigger is whatever lowers the reward for holding the peg or raises the required rate. In 1992 it was the Danish referendum. In 1997 it was the Thai devaluation.
- Asia's three vulnerabilities: modest macro imbalances, weak banks, short-maturity foreign-currency debt. The deeper cause: banks used as instruments of industrial policy in exchange for guarantees.

### 6. The bond-market boom-bust pattern (Ch 7)
Argentina before 1890 and Mexico before 1994 share one sequence: low rates at the center, reform at the periphery, inflows through state banks, saving that fails to rise, political weakness that blocks adjustment, a switch to foreign-currency-linked debt (gold cedulas, tesobonos), then a stop. The rescue differed: in 1890 it protected the London market, in 1995 the borrower.

### 7. Sequencing and regime rules (Ch 10, Ch 11)
- Order: stabilize and reform domestic finance, then FDI, then stock and bond markets, then offshore bank borrowing last. Korea did the last step first.
- Prefer market-friendly instruments. Chile's 30 percent one-year non-interest-bearing deposit lengthens maturity without cutting the level of inflows.
- With an open capital account choose a hard peg or a float with a clear monetary rule. Pegged-but-adjustable rates, bands and target zones are one-way bets.
- Prudential inflow controls are a third line of defense behind bank owners and regulators. Emergency outflow controls are justified only if investors are irrational. Controls are transitional and must not delay institutional reform.
- Do not treat reserves as a substitute for avoiding short-term foreign borrowing.
